# Module 8

# Death Isn't the End. Terms and Conditions Apply.

*The story of where the ancient Norse believed people went after death, why Valhalla wasn't the only destination, and why fallen warriors had to die all over again, every single day. We'll travel to Hel's kingdom, meet the mistress of the ocean depths, explore real Viking Age burials, and finally encounter Ragnar Lothbrok. And at the end, we'll hear music that, a thousand years later, still makes us think about the final journey.*

**Time:** about 34–43 minutes

## Chapter 1. Dying Is the Easy Part. Where Do You Go Next?

---

![Dying Is the Easy Part. Where Do You Go Next?](/visuals/norse/m08/01-afterlife-journey-1448.webp)

---

In our previous story, we met Loki's children.

Fenrir was left bound on a cold island.

Jörmungandr grew large enough to encircle the entire human world.

And Hel was given authority over a realm of the dead.

It's to her world that we're now heading.

But first, a question.

**What did the ancient Norse believe happened to a person after death?**

Perhaps you already know the answer.

A warrior dies in battle.

Valkyries carry him away.

He arrives in Valhalla.

There, endless feasts await him, along with mead, weapons, and the company of other warriors.

Wonderful.

But what happened to everyone else?

Farmers?

Blacksmiths?

Merchants?

Children?

Women?

People who lived long lives and died peacefully in their beds?

Did they simply fail the selection process?

The Norse world wasn't populated exclusively by warriors.

And ancient beliefs about death were considerably more complicated than the version we're used to seeing in films.

First, we need to understand that there was no single, universal doctrine of the afterlife shared by every Scandinavian community throughout every period.

We learn about their beliefs from different kinds of evidence.

The poems of the *Poetic Edda*.

The *Prose Edda*.

The sagas.

Archaeological discoveries.

And accounts written by travellers and authors who observed northern peoples from the outside.

These sources don't always agree.

One describes a magnificent hall for fallen warriors.

Another speaks of a cold realm of the dead.

A third suggests that the dead might continue existing inside their burial mounds.

And a fourth connects the fate of drowned sailors with a being who lives beneath the sea.

No universal departure schedule.

No single map of the afterlife.

And absolutely no information desk.

**Death was inevitable. The onward travel arrangements were considerably less straightforward.**

But for the ancient Norse, death didn't necessarily mean that a person simply ceased to exist.

The dead remained present in the memories of their families.

Their names survived in songs.

Memorial stones were raised in their honour.

Stories were told about them.

And sometimes the dead returned to the world of the living.

Not always with friendly intentions.

The sagas contain beings called **draugar**.

These are dead people who continue to exist in physical form.

They may guard their burial places, harm the living, or become particularly unpleasant neighbours.

Especially if you've made the unfortunate decision to settle near their burial mound.

They're not quite like the familiar ghosts of modern fiction.

They can be physically dangerous.

In some stories, they leave their graves.

And occasionally they're so powerful that dealing with them permanently requires extraordinary measures.

Imagine someone who was so unpleasant in life that even death failed to relieve everyone else of their company.

Still, not every image of the dead was frightening.

Ancestors could remain an important part of family memory.

Burial places connected people with their land and their kin.

Funeral rituals helped the living endure loss and recognise the transition of the dead into another state of existence.

That's why it's important not to turn the Norse afterlife into a simple system of rewards and punishments.

It reflected ideas about military glory, family, the sea, memory, and powers beyond human control.

Archaeology also shows us how varied real funerals could be.

Some people were buried in the ground.

Others were cremated.

Some were buried in boats or ships.

Others were accompanied by objects that had been significant during their lives.

But none of these customs can automatically be treated as the rule for every Viking.

Incidentally, the word "Viking" didn't simply mean anyone who lived in ancient Scandinavia.

It was associated with particular activities, including seaborne expeditions.

Most people spent their lives doing perfectly ordinary things.

Growing food.

Building houses.

Raising children.

Working.

And eventually dying.

Without a final battle.

Without a heroic speech.

And usually without any prospect of appearing in a beautifully produced television drama.

So we're not going to begin our journey into the afterlife at Hel's terrifying gates.

We'll start with the most famous destination for fallen warriors.

**Valhalla.**

And if you believe that death means freedom from all your responsibilities, I have some unfortunate news.

## Chapter 2. Valhalla. Eternal Feasting. Mandatory Training.

---

![Valhalla. Eternal Feasting. Mandatory Training.](/visuals/norse/m08/02-valhalla-einherjar-1448.webp)

---

Imagine an enormous hall.

So magnificent that an ordinary human palace would look like a modest farm building beside it.

Long tables fill the interior.

Fires burn.

You can hear conversations, laughter, and drinking vessels clinking together.

Warriors are everywhere.

Lots of warriors.

An extraordinary number of warriors.

Welcome to **Valhalla**.

The name *Valhöll* is commonly translated as "Hall of the Slain".

It belongs to Odin.

And it's where selected fallen warriors gather.

They're called the **einherjar**.

We've encountered that name before.

Odin doesn't collect them simply because he enjoys having good company.

He has a purpose.

He knows Ragnarök is coming.

The final battle of the gods.

And he's going to need an enormous army.

So Valhalla isn't merely somewhere to relax after death.

**It's also a military training programme for the end of the world.**

A rather unusual arrangement for the afterlife.

But let's see how it works.

In *Grímnismál*, Valhalla is described as an extraordinary hall.

Its roof is associated with shields.

Its interior with weapons.

According to the *Prose Edda*, the hall has numerous doors through which the warriors will march when Ragnarök begins.

The numbers emphasise the immense scale of the army.

But the really interesting part begins in the morning.

The einherjar leave the hall.

Pick up their weapons.

And start fighting.

One another.

All day.

They strike.

They're wounded.

They kill each other.

Yes.

This is genuinely the arrangement.

Then evening arrives.

The fallen rise again.

Their wounds disappear.

Everyone returns to the hall.

And sits down together.

For a feast.

Imagine spending your day being repeatedly struck by swords, only to find yourself sitting next to the person who killed you that evening.

Discussing the quality of today's battle.

Perhaps even suggesting that you do it again tomorrow.

Because tomorrow, that's exactly what happens.

**You died a hero. Congratulations. You may now continue dying every day.**

At least the catering is excellent.

Valhalla has an extraordinary boar named **Sæhrímnir**.

Every day, his meat is prepared by a cook named Andhrímnir in a cauldron called Eldhrímnir.

There's enough for the warriors.

And afterwards, the boar is whole again.

Ready to be cooked the following day.

A remarkably efficient catering arrangement.

Although it would be interesting to hear the boar's opinion.

Drinks are provided too.

On Valhalla's roof stands a goat called **Heiðrún**.

She feeds on the leaves of a tree, and mead flows from her udders, filling an enormous vessel for the warriors.

There's enough for everyone.

Notice how differently this works from the living world.

Among the living, people struggle to obtain food.

Make dangerous journeys.

Risk their lives for wealth.

But if they reach Valhalla after death, they receive an endless supply of food and drink.

In return, they're expected to fight every single day.

With no prospect of permanent retirement.

And all because one final battle is coming.

Odin gathers the einherjar to confront the forces that will threaten the gods at Ragnarök.

We'll discover exactly how that happens in our final module.

For now, one thing matters.

**Valhalla isn't necessarily a reward for living a good life.**

It's associated with a particular warrior's fate and the selection of the fallen.

The ancient sources don't tell us that every kind, honest, courageous person automatically ended up there.

Nor does dying in battle allow us to describe the precise fate of every individual warrior with certainty.

In the previous module, we met the Valkyries.

They're associated with the choosing of the slain and Odin's will.

But it turns out Odin isn't the only one who receives the battle-dead.

There's another goddess.

And she has her own place for fallen warriors.

We've already encountered her golden necklace, magic, and cat-drawn chariot.

Now it's time to meet Freyja as a mistress of the slain.

And discover why even Odin didn't have a monopoly on the afterlife.

## Chapter 3. Fólkvangr. Freyja Has Places Available Too.

---

![Fólkvangr. Freyja Has Places Available Too.](/visuals/norse/m08/03-freyja-folkvangr-1448.webp)

---

Whenever people discuss the Norse afterlife, Valhalla usually gets all the attention.

Odin.

Valkyries.

Enormous feasts.

Warriors who fight each other every day and return to life afterwards.

A spectacular arrangement.

But *Grímnismál* contains a detail that popular retellings often overlook.

**Freyja chooses half of those who fall in battle.**

Odin receives the other half.

We mentioned this in Module Six.

Now let's look a little closer.

Freyja's realm is called **Fólkvangr**.

Its name is commonly associated with a field or plain of people or armies.

There stands her hall, **Sessrúmnir**.

Fólkvangr is associated with the fate of the portion of the slain chosen by Freyja.

But what do they do there?

Fight every day?

Hold feasts?

Live in magnificent halls?

Enjoy unlimited food and drink?

Here's where things become particularly interesting.

**We don't know.**

At least, the surviving ancient sources don't provide a detailed account of everyday life in Fólkvangr comparable to the descriptions of Valhalla's warriors.

We can speculate.

Create artistic interpretations.

Imagine beautiful fields flooded with northern light.

But we can't present imagination as established mythological fact.

So we won't invent a training programme and a catering service for Freyja.

Even if regular feasting would suit the overall atmosphere of this course rather nicely.

But Freyja's authority over the slain is extraordinarily important.

We tend to think of her as a goddess of love and beauty.

Remember her golden tears.

Her necklace, Brísingamen.

Her cats.

Yet Freyja is also associated with magic, death, and the selection of those killed in battle.

There is no contradiction here in the Norse mythological world.

Love and death can belong to the same being.

Beauty and danger can coexist.

And power isn't limited to the ability to kill. It can also mean having the authority to choose what happens after death.

There's another detail worth noting.

Some popular retellings claim that Freyja chooses the dead first, and Odin receives whoever remains.

*Grímnismál* does describe a division of the slain.

But it doesn't explain the selection process in detail.

So we can imagine Freyja chairing a committee that reviews the list of casualties before Odin gets a turn.

But only as a joke.

And definitely not as established mythology.

Let's try a thought experiment.

Imagine two warriors.

They fought in the same battle.

Side by side.

And died almost simultaneously.

But one arrived in Valhalla.

The other in Fólkvangr.

Why?

We have no universal answer.

And that's precisely what makes Norse beliefs about death so fascinating.

They don't form one comprehensive administrative system.

Different myths preserve different images.

Not every detail is explained.

And not every fate can be reduced to a neat spreadsheet.

Incidentally, there's another common misconception.

That all women went to Freyja after death, while all men went to Odin.

The ancient sources don't establish any such universal rule.

Fólkvangr is associated with a portion of those killed in battle.

Not with the automatic allocation of all the dead by gender.

So we'll close the mythological recruitment department for now.

Without authorising any new job descriptions.

But the question remains.

If not everyone went to Valhalla or Fólkvangr, where did the others go?

To find out, we'll need to leave the shining halls of the gods.

And travel into colder lands.

To a place where the living are rarely welcome visitors.

And where Loki's daughter rules.

## Chapter 4. Hel. Not Hell. Just a Different Address.

---

![Hel. Not Hell. Just a Different Address.](/visuals/norse/m08/04-hel-underworld-1448.webp)

---

Remember Hel?

The daughter of Loki and Angrboda.

Sister to Fenrir and Jörmungandr.

The being whom Odin appointed to rule over a realm of the dead.

Now we're going to explore her domain.

Modern fiction sometimes presents Hel as a terrifying underworld filled with monsters and punishments.

But let's establish something important immediately.

**Hel isn't a direct copy of the Christian Hell.**

And ancient Norse ideas about her realm shouldn't automatically be interpreted through the later Christian understanding of judgement after death.

In mythological texts, Hel is both the name of a ruler and a name associated with the world of the dead.

People who died of sickness or old age might be found there.

But the surviving sources don't support the idea that every ancient Scandinavian believed in precisely the same afterlife system.

Now let's travel there.

Our guide is someone we already know from Module Five.

**Hermod.**

After Baldr died, his mother Frigg asked whether anyone would dare travel to the world of the dead and try to bring her son back.

Hermod volunteered.

Odin lent him Sleipnir.

The eight-legged horse.

Perhaps an ordinary mode of transport seemed insufficiently reliable for a journey beyond the world of the living.

Hermod set off.

For nine nights, he rode through dark valleys.

Farther from the familiar world of the gods.

Deeper into lands where almost no light could be found.

Finally, he came to the river **Gjöll**.

A bridge crossed it.

The bridge was guarded by a maiden named **Móðguðr**.

She asked who the traveller was.

What was his name?

Why was he heading towards the realm of the dead?

Hermod explained that he was searching for Baldr.

Móðguðr told him that Baldr had already passed that way.

And directed him onwards.

Hermod continued until he reached the gates of Hel's realm.

And jumped over them on Sleipnir.

A spectacular method of getting past a closed entrance.

Although not every visitor to the afterlife has access to an eight-legged horse.

Inside, Hermod found Baldr.

He was sitting among the dead.

Hermod spent the night there.

The following morning, he asked Hel to release the fallen god.

We already know her answer.

Everything in existence must weep for Baldr.

But one giantess refused.

And Odin's son remained in the realm of the dead.

This story reveals something important about Hel's authority.

Even a messenger sent by the chief of the gods couldn't simply take someone back without her permission.

The realm had rules of its own.

But we shouldn't imagine Hel exclusively as an endless torture chamber.

The sources describe her domain in different ways, and many details survive only in individual accounts.

Some images are certainly gloomy.

High walls.

Enormous gates.

Cold lands.

Halls with names associated with sickness and deprivation.

But Baldr's story suggests that the dead could exist there in circumstances different from the punishments we might normally imagine.

There's another important point.

Modern retellings sometimes present a perfectly detailed map of the Nine Worlds, as if the ancient Norse had left us a geographical atlas with clearly defined borders.

But our sources provide no single, completely consistent system.

Names such as Niflheim, Niflhel, and Hel appear in complex contexts.

We shouldn't assume each represents a clearly defined district with officially approved borders.

Although an administrative map of the afterlife would probably have made Hermod's journey considerably easier.

If, of course, he'd still been alive.

So now we know about three important destinations.

Valhalla.

Fólkvangr.

Hel.

But another power is also associated with death.

And nearly every seafarer would have understood the danger it represented.

Because the ancient Scandinavians lived beside the sea.

Used it.

Traded across it.

Fought on it.

And frequently died in its waters.

The sea had a mistress of its own.

And she had her own ideas about who belonged to the dead.

## Chapter 5. Rán. The Sea Claims Its Own.

---

![Rán. The Sea Claims Its Own.](/visuals/norse/m08/05-ran-sea-and-net-1448.webp)

---

Imagine the northern sea.

Heavy clouds.

A bitter wind.

Waves rising higher and higher.

A small ship struggling through a storm.

Its crew fights to stay on course.

But water is already pouring over the sides.

The sea doesn't care how experienced the captain is.

It doesn't ask how many battles his crew has won.

And it's completely indifferent to the fact that somebody promised to be home for dinner.

Somewhere in the depths waits **Rán**.

A being associated with the sea and those who perish in its waters.

Her name is connected with an Old Norse word for robbery or forcible seizure.

An expressive name for the mistress of a force that regularly takes ships and crews without asking permission.

Rán's husband is named **Ægir**.

We've met him before.

Remember the feast where Loki decided to publicly insult practically every god in existence?

Ægir was the host.

Together, he and Rán have nine daughters.

They're associated with the waves of the sea.

Some of their names evoke different characteristics and appearances of the water.

It's a large family.

Although when your daughters are literally sea waves, family gatherings are probably rather noisy.

But Rán possesses one particularly interesting object.

**A net.**

According to ancient accounts, she uses it to capture those who fall into the sea's power.

In skaldic poetry, Rán becomes an image of the danger of drowning itself.

Sailors can fall into her hands.

Or into her net.

And in one ancient poetic image, a drowned person is treated almost as someone stolen away by the sea's mistress.

This creates an unexpected connection with Module Five.

Remember the story of Andvari's cursed gold?

Loki killed an otter that turned out to be Hreidmar's son.

The gods were forced to obtain an enormous ransom.

And Loki went searching for Andvari, a dwarf hiding in the shape of a pike.

We already know that Loki used a net to catch him.

But one ancient version adds an interesting detail.

**Loki borrowed that net from Rán.**

The same being who captures people at sea.

Loki borrowed her equipment to catch a dwarf.

Steal his treasure.

And set in motion a curse that would destroy a family and eventually become connected with the dragon Fafnir.

Quite an impressive history for something somebody simply borrowed.

One wonders whether Loki was aware of the terms and conditions for returning rented equipment.

But let's go back to the sea.

For the Scandinavians, it was both a source of wealth and a deadly danger.

Ships allowed them to reach distant lands.

Trade.

Explore new routes.

Raid.

Migrate.

But the sea could destroy everything within moments.

And even the greatest warrior's skill offered no guarantee of survival.

Because the enemy wasn't another human being.

It was nature itself.

So it's hardly surprising that death at sea acquired its own mythological figure.

Some literary traditions suggest that those who drowned came into Rán's possession.

Other stories connect gold with the hope of receiving a favourable welcome in her domain.

But we shouldn't assume we possess proof of a universal custom in which every sailor carried gold to pay for their accommodation in the afterlife.

The evidence is more complicated, and some details belong to later literary traditions.

Yet the image is extraordinarily powerful.

A person sails out to sea.

Hoping to return.

While somewhere beneath the waves lies a force that may take them forever.

Perhaps this is where Norse mythology comes particularly close to the everyday realities of its people.

Because the ancient Scandinavians genuinely faced the sea's dangers.

Not everyone returned from a voyage.

And not every family even knew where a loved one had died.

Sometimes there wasn't a body to bury.

Only memories.

And the sea.

One of the most moving expressions of such grief survives in *Egil's Saga*.

The poet Egil Skallagrímsson mourns his son Böðvarr, who drowned.

In his lament, Rán becomes the force that took the boy away.

And suddenly, the mistress of the sea is no longer simply a frightening mythological character.

She becomes a way of expressing human grief.

A way of naming something that cannot be brought to justice.

The sea took his son.

But you cannot argue with the sea.

You cannot challenge it to a duel.

And you cannot force it to return the dead.

Even with an exceptionally good sword.

Perhaps that's one reason people gave so much thought to what happened after death.

If someone disappeared from the world of the living, they wanted to believe the journey didn't simply end.

And those left behind could at least give them a meaningful farewell.

Now, for the first time, we're going to step out of the world of mythology.

Because we have real ships.

Real graves.

Real objects that belonged to dead people.

And archaeologists trying to understand why all these things ended up beneath the ground.

## Chapter 6. The Final Journey. Ships, Fire, and Personal Belongings.

---

![The Final Journey. Ships, Fire, and Personal Belongings.](/visuals/norse/m08/06-viking-ship-burial-1448.webp)

---

You've probably seen this scene before.

A longship rests on the shore.

The body of a fallen leader lies upon it.

Warriors gather around.

Someone delivers a solemn speech.

The ship is pushed away from land.

It drifts slowly out to sea.

A warrior raises his bow.

Releases a flaming arrow.

The sail catches fire.

And the ship disappears into the flames.

Beautiful.

Dramatic.

Extremely cinematic.

Unfortunately, there's one small problem.

**This wasn't how every Viking funeral happened.**

Archaeology certainly doesn't support the idea that sending burning ships out to sea was a universal ancient Scandinavian custom.

Real burial practices were considerably more varied.

Let's begin with one of the most famous discoveries.

In Norway, archaeologists uncovered the extraordinary **Oseberg burial**.

During the early ninth century, two women were buried inside an enormous ship.

They were accompanied by a remarkable collection of objects.

Jewellery.

Textiles.

Wooden items.

A wagon.

Sledges.

Everyday possessions.

And other objects suggesting the high status of the deceased.

The ship wasn't set adrift on the sea.

It was buried beneath a mound.

On land.

Yes.

An entire ship.

Under the ground.

If your idea of Viking funerals comes exclusively from burning ships, that might seem surprising.

But for archaeologists, discoveries like this are invaluable.

They allow us to examine objects from real lives rather than a fictional cinematic ceremony.

Another famous example is the **Gokstad ship**.

It too was used for the burial of a high-status individual.

Inside were numerous objects, including equipment, everyday possessions, and other evidence of burial customs.

Discoveries like these show that ships could hold meaning beyond their use in life.

They could accompany someone after death.

Why?

Perhaps they symbolised a journey.

Perhaps they reflected wealth and social status.

Perhaps they were connected with religious ideas about entering another world.

But we can't assume every ship burial carried exactly the same meaning.

And we shouldn't forget that a ship burial required enormous resources.

Most ordinary people couldn't afford such an elaborate farewell.

So people were buried in other ways.

In the ground.

Beneath mounds.

Within stone structures.

Sometimes their bodies were cremated.

And sometimes graves contained very few possessions.

Burial customs varied according to period, region, family status, local tradition, and religious change.

That's why archaeology doesn't support one universal "standard Viking funeral".

Although such a standard would certainly make life easier for historical television productions.

Especially when every season requires another spectacular farewell.

Now let's return to the objects discovered in graves.

Weapons.

Jewellery.

Tools.

Everyday belongings.

Sometimes even animals.

Why were these things placed beside the dead?

Some researchers connect them with ideas about continued existence after death.

Perhaps the deceased would need possessions in another world.

But grave goods could also express status, social relationships, memory, and the participation of the living in a ritual.

We shouldn't automatically assume every knife found in a grave was intended as a spare tool for the afterlife.

Sometimes it was simply part of a meaningful burial assemblage.

Still, if we imagine the final journey as a voyage, a practical question arises.

What should you bring?

Weapons?

Jewellery?

Everyday possessions?

A ship?

Twelve horses?

That last option might make packing a little complicated.

Although the gods generally had to deal with logistical challenges on an even greater scale.

There's another particularly famous piece of evidence about ancient funerals.

In the tenth century, the traveller **Ahmad ibn Fadlan** described the funeral of a prominent man among the Rus on the River Volga.

His account includes a ship, grave offerings, elaborate rituals, violence, and the burning of the vessel with the dead man.

But there's an important distinction.

Ibn Fadlan was describing a particular group and a particular ceremony.

His account doesn't prove that everyone living in ancient Scandinavia buried their dead in the same way.

And some details of the ceremony are genuinely horrifying to modern readers.

Ancient funeral customs weren't always beautiful.

Nor can we assume that everyone involved participated willingly.

We shouldn't romanticise real suffering simply because it happened a thousand years ago.

Yet beneath these different rituals lies something familiar.

The living were trying to send the dead on their way.

To preserve a memory.

To mark a transition — however that transition was understood.

And sometimes a burial was also an opportunity for a family to demonstrate its status, wealth, and power.

So the final journey could tell us just as much about the living as about the dead.

Who had this person been?

How did the community regard them?

What did their family want others to remember?

And what should future generations see?

That brings us to a particularly fascinating story.

Because some people remained in human memory for so long that their very biographies became legends.

Even when nobody could clearly separate fact from fiction anymore.

There's one such figure you almost certainly recognise.

Especially if you've watched *Vikings*.

**Ragnar Lothbrok.**

## Chapter 7. Ragnar Lothbrok. Die Once. Become a Legend.

---

![Ragnar Lothbrok. Die Once. Become a Legend.](/visuals/norse/m08/07-ragnar-serpent-pit-1448.webp)

---

Imagine a man standing before his own death.

He knows there's no escape.

His enemies have won.

Only a few moments remain.

Yet he refuses to show fear.

Instead, he remembers his battles.

His victories.

His distant journeys.

And the fate that eventually comes for every warrior.

In medieval legend, this is how **Ragnar Lothbrok** faces his final moments.

Perhaps you know him from the television series **Vikings**.

A cunning leader.

A warrior.

An explorer.

A man who dreams of discovering lands beyond the sea and repeatedly finds himself caught between power, family, and ambition.

But we need to make an important distinction.

**The Ragnar of the television series, the Ragnar of medieval legend, and a real historical individual aren't necessarily the same person.**

In medieval sources, Ragnar Lothbrok is a famous legendary hero.

Campaigns, marriages, victories, and numerous descendants are attributed to him.

But historians cannot confidently verify his entire biography as preserved in the sagas.

Perhaps the legends combined stories about several historical people.

Perhaps they grew around a real individual whose life was dramatically transformed through storytelling.

But the details cannot be treated as established facts.

And that's important.

Because myths and legends don't become less interesting when we learn to distinguish them from historical documents.

Quite the opposite.

They reveal what people wanted to remember.

And what they imagined an exceptional hero should be.

Now let's return to Ragnar.

According to the famous legend, he's captured by an English king named **Ælla**.

The king orders him to be thrown into a pit filled with snakes.

Not the most welcoming conclusion to a diplomatic encounter.

And probably sufficient grounds for a strongly negative review of his stay in Northumbria.

But Ragnar doesn't beg for mercy.

A later poetic tradition places a work called **Krákumál** in his mouth.

It's Ragnar's famous death song.

In it, the hero remembers his battles.

His youth.

His military achievements.

And the world awaiting fallen warriors.

The poem presents someone who doesn't regard death as a final humiliation, but as an inevitable part of a warrior's life.

He doesn't deny danger.

He doesn't claim to be immortal.

But he refuses to let fear define his final moments.

It's a powerful literary image.

There's an important historical detail, however.

*Krákumál* is a medieval literary work, probably composed long after the period in which Ragnar supposedly lived.

We cannot treat it as a documentary recording of a real person's final words.

That's why it's more accurate to say:

**"According to legend, Ragnar recites a poem before his death."**

Rather than:

"Historians know exactly what Ragnar said in his final moments."

But the scene remains extraordinary even without documentary certainty.

Here is someone for whom death doesn't mean complete disappearance.

His achievements continue through memory.

And future fame becomes part of his understanding of a life worth living.

In some versions of the legend, Ragnar's death leads to revenge by his sons.

They gather an army and turn against their father's enemies.

Historical Scandinavian armies really did campaign in ninth-century England, and King Ælla died in 867.

But we can't automatically treat the entire revenge legend as a historically accurate explanation of those events.

We need to distinguish between what historical evidence confirms and what survives in later heroic stories.

Now let's return to the television series.

Its creators drew on the names of both historical and legendary figures.

Combined different events.

Changed chronology.

Invented dialogue and storylines.

And created the Ragnar known to millions of viewers.

Not a strictly historical reconstruction.

But a modern dramatic character who grew out of northern legends.

And that doesn't make the series meaningless.

It encourages viewers to become interested in a world they may previously have known almost nothing about.

Who is Odin?

Why do these characters talk about Valhalla?

What does Ragnarök mean?

Why is dying in battle imagined differently from dying of old age?

And where did all these stories come from?

That's exactly why we're creating MIYU Academy.

So familiar images from films, music, and television can become more than beautiful scenery.

So we can recognise the cultural and historical ideas behind them.

There's another important thing about Ragnar's legend.

His fame survived not only the character's death.

It survived the centuries.

Generations told new stories about him.

Poets placed words in his mouth.

Writers recorded traditions.

And centuries later, television brought him back into popular culture.

Today, millions recognise a name associated with events more than a thousand years old.

Even though we can't reconstruct its bearer's biography with complete certainty.

**For a man who died in a pit full of snakes, Ragnar has enjoyed a remarkably successful posthumous career.**

And that brings us back to the central theme of this module.

Death and memory.

What remains after someone is gone?

Bodies disappear.

Possessions decay.

Graves are forgotten.

But sometimes a story survives.

A name.

A song.

And a song will be the final part of our journey.

Because modern northern music includes artists who engage with ancient images and traditions in extraordinary ways.

And one of them helped create the unforgettable sound of the television series *Vikings*.

Imagine a dark northern forest.

A cold wind.

The gentle sound of water.

And a human voice.

Slow.

Ancient in atmosphere.

Almost like something that might have been heard a thousand years ago.

But this music is modern.

This is **Wardruna**.

A Norwegian musical project founded by Einar Selvik.

Its work draws inspiration from ancient Scandinavian culture, language, mythology, nature, and northern musical traditions.

It uses historical and traditional instruments alongside modern ways of creating sound.

Which means Wardruna shouldn't be presented as a group that literally reproduces music from the Viking Age.

We don't have enough evidence to know exactly what all the songs of that period sounded like.

Wardruna does something different.

**It creates contemporary music in conversation with an ancient culture.**

Einar Selvik has also contributed music to the television series *Vikings*.

So if you recognise the distinctive atmosphere of certain scenes, you now know where part of that musical world comes from.

But for the ending of this module, I've chosen one particular song.

**Helvegen.**

Its title can be translated as **"The Road to Hel"**.

It's associated with death, transition, farewell, and the memory of those who have passed away.

And it brings us back to the stories we've explored today.

Remember Hermod.

He rides through dark valleys.

Travels towards the River Gjöll.

Reaches the gates of the realm of the dead.

And searches for Baldr.

Now imagine another traveller.

An ordinary person.

Without an eight-legged horse.

Without Odin's protection.

Without magical weapons.

They're setting out on their final journey.

And they don't know what lies ahead.

Will it be cold?

Will they be alone?

Will they meet anyone?

Will somebody remember their name?

Those questions lie at the emotional heart of *Helvegen*.

Not a desire to glorify death.

Not a promise of immortality.

But an attempt to understand farewell.

And to consider what happens to memory when somebody leaves us.

There's a particular restraint in the song.

It doesn't demand that listeners believe in the literal existence of Hel's kingdom.

It doesn't promise that everyone will arrive in the same afterlife.

And it doesn't turn ancient mythology into a simple guide to what happens after death.

Instead, it speaks to experiences familiar to people across different times and cultures.

The fear of loss.

Love.

Grief.

And the desire not to leave someone alone on their final journey.

We already know how important memory was in the ancient North.

Names survived in songs.

Deeds in legends.

Families remembered their ancestors across generations.

Sometimes a stone was raised.

Sometimes a poem was composed.

Sometimes a story was told, then retold over and over again.

In Module Three, we encountered *Hávamál*, The Sayings of the High One.

It contains a famous idea: people die and wealth disappears, but the memory of worthy or remarkable deeds may outlive the person who performed them.

We can remember that idea today.

Because this is where our story connects with the present.

Ragnar Lothbrok continues to exist in legends.

Baldr in mythology.

Ancient burials in museums.

And Wardruna's music allows modern listeners to engage once more with the questions that troubled the people of the North.

Even though the answers haven't become much simpler in a thousand years.

## 1. What is the sea being Rán associated with?

A. Building ships for Odin.  
B. Controlling the weather exclusively above Asgard.  
C. Catching people who perish at sea in her net.  
D. Checking boarding documents before departure. Sword baggage costs extra.  

**Answer:** C.

Rán is associated with the sea and those who drown. Her net also appears in the story of Andvari's cursed gold. A remarkably versatile piece of equipment with a deeply unpleasant reputation.

## 2. Which statement about real Viking Age burials is accurate?

A. Everyone was sent out to sea on a burning ship.  
B. Burial practices varied, including inhumations, cremations, and ship burials.  
C. Only men could be buried in ships.  
D. Every deceased person received a ship after providing proof of participation in three raids.  

**Answer:** B.

Archaeologists have discovered different types of burials. The famous Oseberg and Gokstad ships were buried on land. The cinematic burning-ship funeral was far from universal.

## 3. What should we remember about Ragnar Lothbrok and Wardruna?

A. The television series *Vikings* precisely reconstructs Ragnar's biography, while Wardruna performs original ninth-century recordings.  
B. Ragnar personally wrote every Wardruna song before his death.  
C. Ragnar was invented entirely by television writers and doesn't appear in medieval sources.  
D. Ragnar is known from medieval legends, while Wardruna creates modern music inspired by ancient Scandinavian culture.  

**Answer:** D.

Ragnar survived his own death as a literary hero. Wardruna helps ancient cultural images speak to the present. Historical accuracy and artistic power aren't the same thing. Fortunately for screenwriters.
