# Module 1

# First Came Chaos. Then Came the Gods. Things Didn't Get Any Better.

*The story of how the sun, sky, earth, and the first divine family emerged from endless waters. And how humanity ended up in a world where keeping everything in order became a daily responsibility.*

**Time:** about 25–30 minutes

---

## Introduction. A World That Doesn't Exist Yet

---

Imagine waking up one morning, opening your eyes, and seeing absolutely nothing.

No walls. No ceiling. No window. No phone that ran out of battery overnight because you forgot to charge it again.

Actually, there's no phone either.

And there's no you.

No Earth, no Sun, no Moon, no stars, no time, and not even such a thing as "morning."

We're facing a question people have been asking for thousands of years: **what existed before the world began?**

Today, we can turn to cosmology and the Big Bang theory. The ancient Egyptians didn't have that option. What they did have was a remarkable ability to observe the world, a vivid imagination, and several thousand years to develop an incredibly complex mythology.

The Egyptians saw the world as a living system. The sky could be a goddess, the earth a god, the sun a divine power, and the order of the universe depended not only on the gods but also on human actions.

And here's something particularly interesting: they didn't have one universally accepted story of creation. Different religious centres offered their own explanations. Sometimes the stories differed in their details; sometimes they complemented one another.

The result was a mythology in which several different accounts of the universe's origins could exist side by side.

Welcome to Ancient Egypt.

**Even the creation of the world comes with alternative versions.**

---

## Chapter 1. Nun. When the Whole World Was One Giant Puddle

---

![The dark primeval waters of Nun and the first mound of land](/visuals/egypt/m01/01-nun-and-first-mound-1448.webp)

---

In the very beginning, nothing familiar to us existed. No sky, no earth, no sun, no trees, and no animals.

But according to ancient Egyptian beliefs, something did exist.

It was called **Nun**.

Imagine endless, dark water. No shores, no horizon, no bottom. It doesn't roar, flow towards the sea, or reflect the stars — because the stars don't exist yet either.

This isn't quite an ocean as we understand it. There are no fish, coral reefs, shipwrecks, or people who decided to spend their holidays by the sea.

There's nowhere for the water to flow anyway.

Nun represents the primeval waters, the state of the world before familiar forms and boundaries appeared.

There is no distinction between above and below, land and water, day and night. But the possibility that all these things might one day exist is already there.

The ancient Egyptians sometimes depicted Nun as a deity in human form. In some images, he lifts the solar barque in his arms, as though supporting the world that has already come into existence.

This is an important detail: **the primeval waters do not disappear after the universe is created.** In Egyptian mythology, they continue to exist beyond the boundaries of the ordered world.

So chaos hasn't gone anywhere.

It just doesn't occupy all the available space anymore.

### Why Water?

To understand this, we need to leave the mythical beginning of time and travel to the very real banks of the Nile.

Every year, the river flooded, covering vast areas of land. When the waters receded, they left behind fertile silt that made agriculture possible.

For a farming civilisation, this was an event of enormous importance.

Water covered the land. Then dry ground appeared. After that, new life began.

Year after year.

Generation after generation.

The Egyptians observed this cycle for thousands of years. It's hardly surprising that water, emerging land, and renewal became such important images in their understanding of creation.

What happened on the banks of the Nile seemed to repeat the birth of the universe on a smaller scale.

Water could be both a source of life and a dangerous force. If the flood was too weak, crops could fail. If it was too strong, destruction and disaster could follow.

So the primeval waters cannot simply be described as good or evil.

They exist before order is established.

### The First Piece of Land

In one of Egypt's most important creation myths, the first mound rises from these waters.

A small piece of solid ground surrounded by endless water.

No mountain ranges or continents. At this point, even a modest hill is an impressive achievement.

In the tradition of Heliopolis, this primeval mound was associated with the sacred Benben.

And this is where the next stage of our story begins.

Because the first piece of land is about to welcome the being who will create the gods and set the world in motion.

His name is Atum.

And he has one small organisational problem.

**He is currently the only being in the entire universe.**

## Chapter 2. Atum. The God Who Had to Start with Himself

---

![Atum on the first mound as Shu and Tefnut come into being](/visuals/egypt/m01/02-atum-shu-tefnut-1448.webp)

---

Atum is one of the principal creator gods in ancient Egyptian mythology.

According to the Heliopolitan tradition, he emerges on his own from the primeval state. He has no parents in the usual sense. He exists as the original completeness from which everything else gradually emerges.

Now imagine his situation.

Endless water all around.

The first patch of land beneath his feet.

No other gods.

No one to suggest a development plan for the universe, share responsibilities, or even ask whether things are going according to plan.

Atum has to start with himself.

### How Do You Start a Family When You're the Only God in Existence?

Ancient Egyptian texts offer different versions of this event.

In some accounts, Atum creates the first deities through an act of self-generation. In others, they emerge from bodily substances — including his spit.

Yes, ancient Egyptian myths can be surprisingly explicit.

But the point wasn't to shock the audience. These stories expressed the creative power of the divine body.

In a world where life begins through birth, it made sense to describe cosmic creation using images of physical generation.

And so the first two deities of the next generation appear.

**Shu — the god of air.**

**Tefnut — a goddess usually associated with moisture.**

With their arrival, the universe begins to acquire the qualities necessary for life to develop.

Shu is associated with air, breath, and space. In Egyptian art, he is often depicted as a man with a feather on his head.

Tefnut frequently appears as a lioness or a woman with a lioness's head. Her nature is more complex than a single function: besides moisture, she could also be associated with powerful solar forces.

Why does the goddess have a lioness's head?

Because ancient Egyptian gods weren't required to look entirely human.

Animal forms helped express divine qualities such as strength, danger, protection, and speed.

To the Egyptians, this didn't mean the goddess was literally an ordinary lioness who had somehow been given a divine job title.

A deity could have several forms and manifestations.

### The First Generation of Gods

Atum now has children.

Shu and Tefnut become a couple and give rise to the next generation.

Their children are Geb and Nut.

**Geb is the god of the earth. Nut is the goddess of the sky.**

And here's something unusual.

In many other mythological traditions, the earth is female and the sky is male.

In Egypt, it's the other way around.

The earth is male.

The sky is female.

But what's even more interesting is that Geb and Nut don't simply represent parts of the natural world.

They are brother and sister, husband and wife, and the future parents of another generation of gods.

For the Egyptians, family relationships between deities helped explain the relationships between natural forces.

Air, moisture, earth, and sky don't exist independently of one another. They're connected within a single system, which mythology describes through the language of family.

Unfortunately, the family relationships here are so close that a problem of cosmic proportions soon emerges.

Geb and Nut are far too close to each other.

There is practically no space between the earth and the sky.

And without space, the world as we know it simply has nowhere to exist.

Shu, the father of both deities, is forced to intervene.

And perform a task that will literally change the structure of the universe.

---

## Chapter 3. Nut and Geb. When the Parents Had to Step In

---

![Shu lifts the star-covered Nut above the reclining Geb](/visuals/egypt/m01/03-nut-geb-shu-1448.webp)

---

Imagine one of the most recognisable scenes in ancient Egyptian art.

At the bottom lies a man — Geb, the god of the earth.

High above him arches a female figure. Her body is covered in stars, while her outstretched arms and legs touch the earth at the horizons.

This is Nut, the goddess of the sky.

And between them stands Shu.

He lifts Nut and holds her above Geb.

As a result, space appears between heaven and earth.

This scene survives, for example, on the famous Greenfield Papyrus, now held by the British Museum.

### The Separation That Made the World Possible

In Egyptian mythology, the separation of Geb and Nut was one of the most important events in the creation of the universe.

While they remain together, the world cannot take its familiar shape.

When Shu raises Nut, the sky, earth, and space between them become distinct.

Now the air has somewhere to exist.

The sun has a sky to travel across.

And living creatures have room to live.

Creation, then, isn't only about the birth of new deities.

It's also about separation.

Land from water.

Earth from sky.

Light from darkness.

The world becomes possible when different forces take their proper places.

And Shu ends up standing between them forever.

You could say he was the first person in history to establish healthy family boundaries.

And the result was so successful that the entire sky is still resting on his shoulders.

### The Goddess Who Swallows the Sun

Nut had another important responsibility.

She didn't just represent the sky. She also played a role in the eternal cycle of the sun's movement.

According to one Egyptian tradition, Nut swallows the sun god every evening.

During the night, the sun travels through her body.

And in the morning, she gives birth to him again.

Imagine this scene through the eyes of an ancient Egyptian.

The sun sinks towards the horizon.

The world gradually falls into darkness.

But the light doesn't disappear forever.

It begins a journey inside the sky goddess, only to return at dawn.

A new day is a new birth.

And this happens every single day.

No weekends, sick leave, or holidays.

Nut keeps the celestial cycle running without interruption.

### Why Was Nut Depicted on Coffins?

At first glance, the connection between a sky goddess and burial rituals might seem surprising.

But remember the sun.

Every evening, it disappears.

Every morning, it is born again.

For the Egyptians, this image was closely connected to the hope of rebirth after death.

That's why images of Nut appear on coffins, funerary papyri, and inside tombs.

The goddess who gives birth to the sun could symbolically protect the deceased and help them enter a new form of existence.

In this belief system, death didn't necessarily mean an absolute end.

It could be part of a larger cycle of transformation and renewal.

Later, we'll discover just how far the Egyptians developed this idea.

But for now, let's return to Geb and Nut.

Their story doesn't end with their separation.

They have children.

Four deities who will play an enormous role in Egyptian mythology.

And while the first generations of gods were busy creating the world, the next generation will be busy dealing with family relationships.

**Considering what happens next, creating the universe was probably the easier part.**

## Chapter 4. Nine Gods. The Family Drama Begins

---

![The nine gods of the Great Ennead](/visuals/egypt/m01/04-great-ennead-1448.webp)

---

So far, we have Atum, his children Shu and Tefnut, and his grandchildren Geb and Nut.

Five deities.

The world is gradually taking shape. There is space between earth and sky, and the sun has begun its daily journey.

You might think this would be a good moment to stop and enjoy the results.

But no.

Geb and Nut have four children.

And from this moment on, the history of the Egyptian gods starts looking increasingly like a family drama.

### Osiris. Future Ruler of the World of the Dead

Our first new character is **Osiris**.

In Egyptian religion, he would become one of the most important gods associated with death, rebirth, and the afterlife.

He is often depicted as a mummified ruler holding symbols of royal authority.

Why this particular appearance?

Because the central story of Osiris involves death and a new form of existence.

But let's not get ahead of ourselves.

For now, let's just say that Osiris will become a central figure in a conflict that will make it impossible to explain Egyptian beliefs about death without mentioning his name.

### Isis. The Goddess Who Refused to Give Up

**Isis** is the sister and wife of Osiris, and one of the best-known goddesses of Ancient Egypt.

She was associated with magic, motherhood, protection, and royal power.

In mythological stories, Isis shows remarkable resourcefulness, determination, and the ability to act under the most difficult circumstances.

She doesn't simply wait for the other gods to solve the problem.

She gets involved.

And as we'll discover in the next module, her determination sometimes changes the entire course of events.

### Set. A Character with a Complicated Reputation

**Set** is associated with the desert, storms, power, and dangerous forces.

In the famous myth of Osiris, he becomes his own brother's destructive rival.

It would be very convenient to label him the main villain right away.

But Egyptian mythology is more complicated than that.

In different periods, Set could be seen not only as a dangerous deity but also as a protector of the sun god Ra against his enemies.

In other words, the same god can threaten order in one story and defend it in another.

This isn't a mistake made by the writers.

Egyptian gods weren't divided into two clearly defined teams of "good guys" and "bad guys."

Their images developed over centuries, and their roles depended on religious traditions.

So let's hold off on making any final judgments about Set.

Although some of his future actions will be genuinely difficult to defend.

### Nephthys. The Goddess Everyone Seems to Forget

The fourth child is **Nephthys**.

She was associated with protecting the dead, funerary rituals, and magical assistance.

In well-known myths about Osiris, she works alongside Isis and helps her.

Nephthys played an important role in Egyptian religious beliefs, although modern popular culture gives her far less attention.

Apparently, being a powerful goddess isn't enough to get a decent part in a film.

You also need to constantly betray someone, cast a curse, or make a dramatic entrance through a sandstorm.

### The Great Ennead

Now let's count the members of this divine family.

Atum.

Shu and Tefnut.

Geb and Nut.

Osiris, Isis, Set, and Nephthys.

**Nine gods in total.**

In the classical Heliopolitan tradition, they form the **Great Ennead** — a sacred group of nine deities.

Egyptologists use the word *Ennead*, which comes from the Greek word for a group of nine.

These divine groupings helped express relationships between the gods and represent the structure of the universe.

However, we shouldn't treat this group of nine as the only possible or permanently fixed collection of deities. Egyptian texts describe other divine groupings too, and their membership could vary.

In the Heliopolitan version we've just explored, the family tree looks like this:

**Atum → Shu and Tefnut → Geb and Nut → Osiris, Isis, Set, and Nephthys.**

Notice that Horus, the son of Osiris and Isis, isn't included in this classical list of nine deities. His story belongs to the next generation.

And now a question arises.

If the Egyptians had already developed a creation story and built an entire divine family tree, why did they need other versions of the universe's origins?

Because Ancient Egypt existed for thousands of years.

Different cities worshipped different gods.

And the creation of the universe was far too important a subject to be explained in just one way.

## Chapter 5. Three Cities. Three Ways to Create the Universe

---

![Heliopolis, Memphis and Hermopolis: three images of creation](/visuals/egypt/m01/05-three-creation-traditions-1448.webp)

---

Imagine travelling through Ancient Egypt.

In one city, priests tell you that the world was created by Atum.

You travel to another city — and discover that a different divine creator brought everything into existence through thought and speech.

In a third city, you're told that eight primeval deities existed before the world began.

And now you have a perfectly reasonable question:

**So who actually created the universe?**

From a modern perspective, we might demand a single correct answer.

But in ancient Egyptian religion, different creation traditions could coexist and complement one another.

They didn't necessarily describe exactly the same event. Instead, they could explain different aspects of the world's birth and structure.

### Heliopolis. The World as One Big Divine Family

You already know the first tradition.

Heliopolis, known to the Egyptians as Iunu, was one of the most important religious centres devoted to the sun god.

Here, Atum and the generations of gods who emerged after him played a central role.

The primeval waters.

The first mound.

The creator god.

Shu and Tefnut.

Geb and Nut.

And then four children who will eventually change the fate of the divine world.

This is an explanation of creation through the gradual emergence of new forces.

A kind of family tree of the universe.

Except that instead of family photographs, it contains entire forces of nature.

### Memphis. The God Who Only Had to Speak

In the religious tradition of Memphis, an important role belonged to the god **Ptah**.

He was associated with craftsmanship, skilled work, and creation.

In what is known as Memphite Theology, the creation of the world is described through the deity's heart and tongue.

For the Egyptians, the heart wasn't simply associated with emotions. It was also the centre of thought and intention.

The god conceives what is to exist in his heart and expresses his intention through speech.

In this version, the world comes into existence through divine thought and words.

First, an idea.

Then, the spoken word.

And finally, its realisation.

It's a remarkably abstract image of creation.

Ptah doesn't necessarily have to begin with an entire divine dynasty. Creative power lies in thought and its expression.

Of course, if an ordinary person says, "Let there be a palace," a palace probably won't appear.

But according to this religious tradition, Ptah had rather more impressive abilities.

### Hermopolis. Eight Forces Before the World Began

The third tradition is associated with the city of Khemenu, later known by its Greek name, Hermopolis.

The Egyptian name Khemenu is connected with the number eight.

Here, particular importance was given to the **Ogdoad** — a group of eight primeval deities.

They were arranged into four pairs.

In one common version, these were:

- Nun and Naunet — the primeval waters.
- Heh and Hehet — infinity.
- Kek and Kauket — darkness.
- Amun and Amaunet — hiddenness and invisibility.

In earlier texts, another pair, Tenem and Tenemet, appears instead of Amun and Amaunet. They are associated with the absence of a fixed direction.

These deities represented conditions that existed before the ordered world appeared.

It's important not to imagine eight characters sitting around a table, assigning responsibilities, and voting on whether to create the universe.

The Ogdoad expressed the very nature of the primeval state.

Water.

Darkness.

Infinity.

The hidden possibilities of existence.

In Egyptian art, the male deities of this group could appear with frogs' heads, while the female deities could have snakes' heads.

These images might seem unusual to us. But in Egyptian symbolism, they could express connections with water, life, and birth.

And the beliefs surrounding the Ogdoad didn't necessarily contradict stories about the emergence of the sun god.

They could describe an earlier state of the universe, from which the creator later emerged.

### So Which Version Is Correct?

This is where we need to let go of the familiar idea that mythology must provide one final answer.

Ancient Egyptian religion developed over thousands of years. Different cities preserved their own traditions, while priests could connect local gods with broader ideas about the creation of the world.

One story explained the universe through successive generations of gods.

Another emphasised the power of divine intention.

A third described the primeval conditions that existed before the ordered cosmos emerged.

That's why we shouldn't imagine Egyptian mythology as a single book containing the final, officially approved version of every event.

Instead, we find a collection of interconnected beliefs that existed in different periods.

And this helps us understand an important feature of ancient Egyptian thought:

**The world can be explained through different images when each one reveals something essential about its existence.**

But there's still one question we've barely touched on.

The gods have appeared.

The sky and earth have taken their places.

The sun has begun its journey.

But what about humans?

It seems someone forgot to add humanity.

## Chapter 6. Humans and Ma'at. A Universe Without a Warranty

---

![Khnum shapes a human on the potter's wheel, Ma'at with her feather beside him](/visuals/egypt/m01/06-khnum-and-maat-1448.webp)

---

So, let's see what we've got.

The world emerged from the primeval waters.

The gods appeared.

The sky separated from the earth.

The sun began its daily journey.

And several different explanations of how all this happened came into existence.

Everything looks fairly well organised.

But there's one small problem.

**Where are the humans?**

Did the gods really create an entire universe and then simply forget about its future inhabitants?

Of course not.

The Egyptians had different ideas about the origins of humanity. For example, in some texts, humans emerge from the tears of the sun god. The Egyptian words for people and weeping were even connected through a play on similar sounds.

But there's another story, and it's particularly beautiful.

The story of a god who created people in almost the same way a craftsman makes a clay vessel.

### Khnum. The God with a Potter's Wheel

Meet **Khnum**.

He was often depicted as a man with the head of a ram.

Khnum was associated with creation, fertility, and the life-giving forces of the Nile.

According to well-known religious beliefs, he shaped humans from clay on a potter's wheel.

Imagine a workshop.

A potter's wheel turns in the centre.

A lump of wet clay rests upon it.

The deity shapes the clay, gradually forming a human body.

Unlike the story of Atum, where the first gods emerge through the creator's own power, this form of creation resembles the work of a skilled craftsman.

It requires material.

It requires form.

It requires skill.

And the result depends on a creative act.

It's a very Egyptian image: the creation of life can be explained through a craft people knew from everyday experience.

The human body, in this story, becomes a kind of masterpiece of divine craftsmanship.

Of course, the idea that you personally might have been shaped by a god with a ram's head takes a little getting used to.

On the bright side, at least now you know whom to blame for the shape of your nose.

### Creating Humans Is Only Half the Job

But the appearance of humans doesn't automatically mean they'll be able to live.

They need water, food, land, sunlight, and a stable order.

For people living in the Nile Valley, prosperity depended on recurring natural cycles.

The sun had to return.

The river had to keep flooding.

The fields had to produce crops.

And this brings us to one of the most important concepts in all of ancient Egyptian culture.

**Ma'at.**

### Ma'at. Not the Goddess of Good Vibes

Ma'at was both a goddess and a fundamental idea about the proper order of the world.

The concept was associated with truth, justice, order, and harmony.

In Egyptian art, the goddess Ma'at was often depicted as a woman wearing an ostrich feather on her head.

The feather was her symbol, and it would later become one of the most important elements in beliefs about judgment after death.

But let's not get ahead of ourselves.

For now, we're interested in the idea itself.

Imagine a perfectly designed home.

Everything works. There's electricity, running water, comfortable furniture, beautiful windows, and a wonderful view.

You move in and decide that everything will stay this way forever.

But time passes.

Dust starts collecting.

A light bulb burns out.

A door breaks.

And suddenly you discover that even the most beautiful home requires constant care.

The ancient Egyptians had a similar idea about the universe.

**Creating the world isn't enough. Its order must be maintained.**

Ma'at expressed the principle that allowed the world to preserve its proper structure.

And this wasn't just about natural events.

For the Egyptians, moral behaviour and cosmic order were connected.

Truthfulness, justice, fulfilling responsibilities, and proper relationships between people mattered not only to society but also to the wider harmony of the universe.

### What Happens When Order Breaks Down?

The opposite of Ma'at was the concept of **isfet**.

It was associated with chaos, falsehood, injustice, violence, and the destruction of proper order.

Isfet wasn't a separate evil god living somewhere underground and constantly plotting against the world.

It was the principle of disorder.

If Ma'at maintained the proper structure of the world, isfet represented the forces and actions that worked against it.

For the ancient Egyptians, the struggle between order and its disruption was enormously important.

It affected everyday life, royal power, and religious rituals.

### Pharaoh. A Job with Cosmic Responsibilities

According to ancient Egyptian royal ideology, the pharaoh was responsible for maintaining Ma'at.

He didn't simply rule the country, manage resources, and build monumental structures.

In religious thought, he was responsible for preserving proper order and maintaining relationships between humans and the gods.

Temple reliefs sometimes show the pharaoh offering a small figure of Ma'at to the gods.

In other words, he symbolically presents them with the very order he is supposed to uphold.

It sounds very solemn.

But if we simplified the pharaoh's job description, it might look something like this:

Govern the country.

Maintain justice.

Ensure that religious rituals are performed.

Preserve relationships with the gods.

And, ideally, prevent the collapse of the cosmic order.

Just an ordinary Tuesday at work.

Of course, royal ideology doesn't mean that real pharaohs always behaved justly. Egyptian history is full of conflicts, wars, and political crises.

But that's exactly why the concept of Ma'at mattered so much.

Order wasn't seen as something guaranteed forever. It was a condition that had to be preserved and restored.

### Why Must the Sun Rise Tomorrow?

Let's return to the goddess Nut.

Every evening, she swallows the sun.

Every morning, she gives birth to it again.

Today, we explain day and night through the Earth's rotation. But for ancient Egyptians, the sun's movement was part of a far more complex religious picture.

Every day, the sun god travelled along his path and confronted forces that tried to disrupt the established order.

Later, we'll meet the giant serpent Apep — one of the sun god Ra's greatest enemies.

His story will show just how seriously the Egyptians took the threat of chaos.

Each sunrise could be understood as confirmation that the solar order had once again survived.

That's why repetition and renewal were so important in Egyptian mythology.

The flooding of the Nile.

The cycle of day and night.

The return of the sun.

Life, death, and rebirth.

Creation wasn't simply a distant event that happened once at the beginning of time.

Images of creation continued to appear in religious rituals and beliefs about the constant renewal of the existing order.

### Now Look at Egypt Differently

Think of the most recognisable images of ancient Egyptian civilisation.

Enormous pyramids.

Temples with towering columns.

Pharaohs wearing ceremonial headdresses.

The sky goddess with stars covering her body.

The solar barque.

The ostrich feather of Ma'at.

These no longer look like a random collection of mysterious symbols.

Behind them lies an entire system of beliefs.

The world emerged from the primeval waters.

It took shape through divine forces.

Its existence depends on constant renewal.

And order must be maintained.

And here's what makes all this especially interesting.

The ancient Egyptians weren't only trying to explain **how the world began, but also what was necessary for it to keep existing**.

The gods created the universe.

But that was only the beginning.

Because ahead of them lie love, rivalry, power, betrayal, and death.

And one god named Osiris is about to discover that having a brother doesn't always guarantee family support.

But that's a story for the next module.

## Conclusion. First Came Chaos. Now It Has a Story.

---

You've just visited a world where the sky is a goddess, the earth is her husband, and the air keeps them apart.

You've met the primeval waters of Nun, the creator god Atum, the first divine family, and several different explanations of how the universe began.

You've discovered why the Egyptians associated creation with water, why Shu had to separate Geb and Nut, and how the sky goddess brought the sun back every day.

Most importantly, you've learned about Ma'at, one of the central principles of the ancient Egyptian worldview.

You now know that, for the Egyptians, the world wasn't a machine that only needed to be switched on once.

It required constant renewal and the preservation of order.

**But if the gods created order, why did they break it so often themselves?**

In the next module, we'll meet Osiris, Isis, and Set.

And discover how one divine family turned a struggle for power into a story that transformed Egyptian beliefs about death and eternity.

C. It referred only to a successful flooding of the Nile.  

**Answer:** B.

Ma'at brought together ideas of truth, justice, and the proper order of the world. The Egyptians associated maintaining Ma'at with both the well-being of society and the stability of the cosmos.
