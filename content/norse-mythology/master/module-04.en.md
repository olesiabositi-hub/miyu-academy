# Module 4

# Thor. When a Hammer Is the Answer to Everything.

*The story of Asgard's strongest god, who defended the world against giants, once lost his own weapon, went on the worst fishing trip in history, and discovered that even a hammer powerful enough to shatter mountains is useless against old age. Thor, naturally, refused to accept this.*

**Time:** about 30–38 minutes

---

## Chapter 1. Thor. Hit First. Ask Questions Later.

---

![Thor. Hit First. Ask Questions Later.](/visuals/norse/m04/01-stormbound-norse-warrior-over-the-fjord-1448.webp)

---

Last time, we met Odin.

He sacrificed an eye for knowledge, spent nine nights hanging from the World Tree, learned the secrets of the runes, and devoted a considerable portion of his existence to discovering what the future had in store.

Now imagine a very different god.

Enormous. Red-bearded. Incredibly strong.

A god who rarely begins a dangerous encounter with a philosophical question.

His name is **Thor**.

And he has a hammer.

Perhaps you're already picturing the tall, blond hero from the Marvel films, elegantly swinging his weapon while delivering heroic speeches.

But the Thor of Norse mythology was rather different.

In the myths, he's often portrayed as fearsome, quick-tempered, and extraordinarily strong, closely associated with thunder and the protection of the world.

His famous red beard is one of the most recognisable features of his traditional image.

Thor is the son of Odin and Jörð, a goddess or personification of the Earth.

His wife is **Sif**.

She has magnificent hair, which will soon become the cause of a deeply unfortunate incident.

But Thor's defining quality is his strength.

He fights giants, ventures into dangerous territories, and protects Asgard and Midgard from beings that threaten the established order.

And here's something important to remember.

In Norse mythology, giants aren't simply enormous villains.

Among the jötnar are wise beings, powerful women, allies, and even relatives of the gods.

Odin's own mother was a giantess.

But some giants are genuinely dangerous.

And when negotiations fail, Thor is often the one who turns up.

His lands are called **Thrúðvangar**, and his enormous hall is known as **Bilskirnir**.

According to *Grímnismál*, Bilskirnir contains 540 rooms.

Plenty of space for a family gathering.

Although given the relationships between Norse gods, inviting everyone at once would probably be unwise.

Thor possesses several particularly valuable objects.

The first is **Megingjörð**, a belt that increases his strength.

The second is a pair of iron gloves associated with wielding his hammer.

And, of course, there's **Mjölnir** itself.

Thor's most famous weapon.

A hammer capable of crushing enemies and returning to its owner after being thrown.

But Mjölnir isn't used exclusively for destruction.

In the ancient myths, it can also consecrate things. Thor uses it to bless objects and, on occasion, even restore life.

That's an important detail.

Thor's strength isn't simply about killing.

He protects the existing world and helps maintain its order.

For the ancient Norse, Thor wasn't merely a character in entertaining stories. He occupied an important place in religious life.

Archaeologists have discovered small hammer-shaped pendants associated with his worship.

And while Odin often appears as a god of secret knowledge, rulers, and warriors, Thor is especially recognisable as a protector of ordinary people.

He could be invoked when people needed strength, safety, or prosperity.

But there's one problem.

Even the mightiest protector can't always tell the difference between a genuine enemy and a spectacularly bad idea.

And, as we're about to discover, some of Thor's most unfortunate adventures begin with entirely ordinary events.

For example, someone deciding to cut his wife's hair.

Without asking.

And cutting off every last strand.

## Chapter 2. Mjölnir. The Greatest Hammer Ever Made. Minor Manufacturing Defect.

---

![Mjölnir. The Greatest Hammer Ever Made. Minor Manufacturing Defect.](/visuals/norse/m04/02-mjolnir-forged-in-the-norse-flame-1448.webp)

---

Sif had beautiful hair.

Long, golden, and so famous that the ancient myths make a point of mentioning it.

One day, Loki decided to cut it off.

Why?

The *Prose Edda* tells us he did it out of mischief.

No elaborate political intrigue.

No ancient prophecy.

No grand purpose.

Loki simply saw somebody else's hair and decided the situation required his personal involvement.

When Thor discovered what had happened, his reaction was entirely predictable.

He grabbed Loki and threatened to break every bone in his body.

Loki immediately promised to put things right.

He announced that he would find craftsmen capable of making Sif new hair out of real gold.

Hair that would grow just like the original.

Thor agreed.

And so began the story of how some of the greatest treasures of the gods were created.

Loki went to a group of dwarven craftsmen known as **the Sons of Ivaldi**.

They made Sif's golden hair.

But they didn't stop there.

They also created Skíðblaðnir, the magical ship associated with Freyr, and Gungnir, the spear that would belong to Odin.

Three extraordinary treasures.

You might think the matter was settled.

Time to return to Asgard and stop annoying everyone.

But this is Loki we're talking about.

He decided to start a competition.

Loki made a wager with a dwarf named **Brokkr**, claiming that Brokkr's brother, the master smith Sindri, couldn't make three treasures equal to those already produced.

Loki wagered his own head.

A remarkably high price to pay for the pleasure of proving a point.

Sindri began working in the forge.

First, he created Gullinbursti, a boar with golden bristles.

Next came Draupnir, a golden ring capable of producing more rings.

The third treasure would be a hammer.

But Loki had absolutely no intention of losing.

So he transformed into a fly.

And began interfering with the work.

He bit Brokkr, who was operating the bellows to keep the forge burning.

First on the hand.

Then on the neck.

But Brokkr kept working.

While the final treasure was being forged, however, the fly bit him near the eye so fiercely that blood obscured his vision.

For a moment, Brokkr stopped.

And that was enough.

When Sindri removed the finished hammer from the forge, its handle turned out to be unusually short.

**Mjölnir had been created.**

Thor's most fearsome weapon.

With a minor manufacturing defect.

The dwarves presented their creations to the gods.

Odin received Draupnir.

Freyr received Gullinbursti.

And Thor received the hammer.

The gods decided that Mjölnir was particularly valuable because it could defend Asgard against giants.

Despite its short handle, they judged it the greatest of the treasures.

Brokkr had won the wager.

He was now entitled to Loki's head.

And this is where Loki suddenly demonstrated a remarkable talent for legal interpretation.

He explained that he had indeed promised his head.

But not his neck.

And removing one without interfering with the other presented certain practical difficulties.

Brokkr couldn't claim his prize in quite the way he'd intended.

So instead of cutting off Loki's head, he sewed his mouth shut.

Temporarily.

As we'll discover, hoping this would solve the problem permanently would have been wildly optimistic.

But something else mattered more.

Thor now possessed Mjölnir.

The weapon that would accompany him on nearly all his most famous adventures.

And consider how unexpectedly it had come into existence.

Loki did something stupid.

Tried to fix it.

Started a wager.

Lost.

And accidentally provided Asgard with its most important defensive weapon.

Sometimes catastrophically bad decisions produce surprisingly good results.

Unfortunately, the reverse is also true.

And now Thor is about to set off on a journey that begins with dinner.

## Chapter 3. Two Goats. One Dinner. Serious Consequences.

---

![Two Goats. One Dinner. Serious Consequences.](/visuals/norse/m04/03-norse-heroes-before-the-giants-glove-1448.webp)

---

Thor had a rather unusual means of transport.

A chariot pulled by two goats.

Their names were **Tanngrisnir** and **Tanngnjóstr**.

Their names are generally associated with the grinding or gnashing of teeth.

When Thor travelled through the sky, the rumbling of his chariot was associated with thunder.

But the goats were remarkable for more than their strength.

They had one particular quality that would make travelling considerably more convenient.

You could eat them.

And then bring them back to life.

One day, Thor and Loki set off on a journey and stopped for the night at a farmer's house.

The family welcomed the travellers.

Evening came.

Thor decided to prepare dinner.

He slaughtered both goats, skinned them, and cooked the meat.

Then he invited the family to share the meal.

Nothing particularly surprising, unless you happen to know that these are the thunder god's beloved animals.

Before dinner, Thor gave one important instruction.

Every bone had to be carefully placed on the goatskins.

**Not a single bone was to be broken.**

The family agreed.

But the farmer had two children — a boy named Thjálfi and his sister Röskva.

Thjálfi decided he wanted some bone marrow.

He took one of the goat's thigh bones.

And split it open with a knife.

You can imagine how insignificant this seemed at the time.

Just one bone.

Just a little marrow.

The following morning, Thor woke up, took Mjölnir, and consecrated the goatskins.

The goats came back to life.

They stood up.

And one of them began to limp.

Thor immediately understood what had happened.

A thigh bone had been damaged.

The thunder god was furious.

He gripped his hammer until his knuckles turned white.

The farmer's family was terrified.

And not without reason.

When an angry thunder god discovers that his resurrected transport has suffered structural damage in your house, the situation calls for extremely careful explanations.

The family begged for forgiveness.

They offered everything they owned.

Thor saw their fear and calmed down.

As compensation, he took Thjálfi and Röskva into his service.

From then on, they accompanied him as servants and companions.

And that's how one unfortunate dinner led to some rather unexpected changes in the divine expedition's personnel.

The journey continued.

Ahead lay the lands of the giants.

Thor and his companions travelled east through wild forests and enormous open spaces.

One night, they found a strange shelter.

It was enormous, dark, and divided into several compartments.

The travellers settled down to sleep.

But during the night, the ground began to shake.

There was a terrible rumbling.

It sounded as though an earthquake was happening nearby.

In the morning, they discovered that the cause was a giant named **Skrýmir**.

He had been sleeping nearby.

And snoring.

The shelter where the travellers had spent the night was actually his glove.

Yes.

They had slept inside a giant's glove.

Admittedly, it was spacious enough for group accommodation.

Skrýmir suggested that they travel together.

Unfortunately, his company became increasingly irritating.

During the night, Thor struck the sleeping giant's head several times with Mjölnir.

But Skrýmir merely woke up and asked whether a leaf, an acorn, or a twig had fallen on him.

Thor was baffled.

His hammer could crush monsters.

And this giant barely considered the disturbance worth waking up for.

Eventually, the travellers parted ways with Skrýmir and reached an enormous fortress.

It belonged to a being named **Útgarða-Loki**.

And although the name sounds familiar, this wasn't our Loki from Asgard.

A completely different character.

Útgarða-Loki invited his guests inside.

Then offered them a chance to prove whether they were really as powerful as everyone claimed.

For Thor, this should have been the perfect opportunity to demonstrate his strength.

Instead, he was about to experience one of the most humiliating days of his life.

## Chapter 4. Visiting the Giants. Humiliation Included.

---

![Visiting the Giants. Humiliation Included.](/visuals/norse/m04/04-thor-struggles-with-the-endless-cat-1448.webp)

---

An enormous hall was filled with giants.

Útgarða-Loki studied the visitors carefully.

He wanted to know what talents they possessed.

Loki went first.

He claimed he could eat faster than anyone else.

A contest was arranged.

Opposite him sat a being named **Logi**.

A long wooden trough filled with meat was placed between them.

They began to eat.

Both moved towards the middle, devouring everything in their path.

But when they met, it became clear that Loki had eaten only the meat.

Logi had consumed the meat, the bones, and the trough itself.

Loki lost.

Thjálfi went next.

He was an extraordinarily fast runner.

His opponent was a young man named **Hugi**.

They raced.

Hugi won.

They tried again.

And again.

The result never changed.

Thjálfi couldn't come close to catching his opponent.

Now it was Thor's turn.

Útgarða-Loki suggested a drinking contest.

A servant brought out a long drinking horn.

The host explained that a good drinker could empty it in one attempt.

A less accomplished drinker might need two.

But surely everyone could manage it in three.

Thor took the horn.

And began to drink.

He drank for a very long time.

Finally, he stopped to catch his breath.

He looked inside.

The water level had barely changed.

Útgarða-Loki expressed his disappointment.

Thor tried again.

Even harder this time.

But the level scarcely moved.

After the third attempt, he was forced to admit defeat.

For someone capable of crushing mountains, this was a deeply unpleasant result.

So they offered him another challenge.

**Lift a cat.**

An ordinary grey cat that wandered into the hall.

Thor looked at the animal.

He probably assumed they were making fun of him.

He reached beneath its belly and began lifting.

But the cat arched its back.

The higher Thor raised his arms, the more the cat stretched.

Eventually, he managed to lift just one paw from the floor.

And that was all.

Thor had been humiliated.

The giant observed that this wasn't a bad result for such a small and weak visitor.

Thor grew furious and demanded someone to wrestle.

Útgarða-Loki replied that none of his warriors would want to fight someone so feeble.

But perhaps they could summon his elderly nurse.

Her name was **Elli**.

An old woman entered the hall.

Thor prepared to wrestle.

He grabbed her.

Tried to throw her to the ground.

But the harder he struggled, the more firmly she stood.

She began forcing him backwards.

And eventually made him drop to one knee.

The contest was stopped.

The god of thunder had lost.

To an elderly woman.

After all these failures, the visitors were allowed to spend the night.

The next morning, Útgarða-Loki escorted them beyond the fortress walls.

And finally admitted the truth.

Everything had been an illusion.

Skrýmir, whom they'd met in the forest, and Útgarða-Loki were the same being.

When Thor struck the sleeping giant with his hammer, the blows had actually landed on a mountain, leaving enormous dents in the rock.

Then the host explained the other challenges.

**Logi**, Loki's opponent in the eating contest, was fire itself.

Nothing can eat faster than something that consumes everything.

**Hugi**, Thjálfi's opponent, represented thought.

Even the fastest runner cannot outrun a thought.

Thor's drinking horn was connected to the sea.

And the thunder god had lowered the level of the ocean with his enormous gulps.

The cat wasn't a cat at all.

It was **Jörmungandr**, the World Serpent that encircles Midgard.

Thor had managed to lift part of a creature that stretches around the entire earth.

And Elli?

The old woman was **Old Age** itself.

A force that sooner or later brings everyone to their knees.

Even gods.

And Thor had resisted her far longer than anyone could have expected.

Útgarða-Loki admitted that the visitors' extraordinary abilities had terrified him.

And he'd very much prefer never to see them again.

When Thor realised he'd been deceived, he reached for Mjölnir.

But the giant disappeared.

And so did his fortress.

Only an empty plain remained.

What's extraordinary about this story is that every one of Thor's defeats turns out to be evidence of his immense power.

He couldn't drink the sea.

But he made it recede.

He couldn't lift a cat.

But nearly lifted the World Serpent.

He couldn't defeat an old woman.

But he managed to resist old age.

**Sometimes losing doesn't mean you're weak. It means you didn't understand what you were up against.**

Thor learned that lesson the hard way.

Although it's difficult to imagine him enjoying the experience.

## Quick check

Why did Mjölnir end up with an unusually short handle?

A. Thor specifically ordered a compact travel edition.  
B. Odin asked for a smaller hammer so it would fit in his pocket.  
C. Loki, disguised as a fly, disrupted Brokkr's work at the forge.  
D. The dwarves ran out of iron because the project went over budget.  

**Answer:** C.

Loki distracted Brokkr during the forging process. And so Asgard's greatest weapon was born with a manufacturing defect. The quality-control department is still looking for that fly.

## Chapter 5. Wedding of the Century. The Bride Has a Hammer.

---

![Wedding of the Century. The Bride Has a Hammer.](/visuals/norse/m04/05-thors-perilous-viking-wedding-feast-1448.webp)

---

One morning, Thor woke up and discovered that Mjölnir was missing.

His most important weapon.

The hammer that protected Asgard and Midgard against dangerous beings.

Gone.

Thor immediately suspected that something terrible had happened.

And went to find Loki.

Which, to be fair, is a perfectly reasonable place to start.

Loki listened and agreed to help.

Together, they visited Freyja and asked to borrow her magical feather cloak, which allowed its wearer to fly in the form of a bird.

Freyja agreed.

Loki transformed into a bird and flew to Jötunheim, the land of the giants.

There he met **Thrym**, a powerful leader among the giants.

Thrym was sitting on a mound, minding his own business.

When Loki asked about the hammer, the giant calmly admitted he'd stolen it.

In fact, he'd hidden it eight miles beneath the earth.

And he had absolutely no intention of returning it.

Unless certain conditions were met.

**He wanted to marry Freyja.**

Just like that.

The gods' most important defensive weapon had become a bargaining chip in a marriage negotiation.

Loki returned to Asgard with the demand.

Thor and Loki went to Freyja.

They suggested she put on a wedding dress and travel to Thrym.

Freyja became so furious that the halls of the gods shook and her famous necklace, Brísingamen, broke apart.

The proposal had been rejected.

Quite emphatically.

The gods called a council.

They needed the hammer back.

But Thrym wasn't going to surrender it.

Then Heimdall suggested a plan.

**Dress Thor as the bride.**

Put him in a wedding gown.

Cover him in jewellery.

Hide his face behind a veil.

And send him to the giant in Freyja's place.

Thor was outraged.

He was worried that all the other gods would laugh at him.

Which is understandable.

Here stood Asgard's greatest protector.

The god of thunder.

Master of lightning.

Being asked to attend a wedding as the bride.

But Loki pointed out that without Mjölnir, the giants might conquer Asgard.

Thor agreed.

Reluctantly.

Very reluctantly.

Loki disguised himself as a maidservant and accompanied him.

And so began one of the most famous comedies in Norse mythology.

When the visitors arrived in Jötunheim, Thrym was delighted.

He ordered a wedding feast.

The giant eagerly anticipated meeting his beautiful bride.

But soon there was a problem.

The bride was exceptionally hungry.

According to *Þrymskviða*, or *The Lay of Thrym*, Thor ate an entire ox.

Eight salmon.

And all the delicacies prepared for the women.

Then he drank three enormous vessels of mead.

Thrym was astonished.

He'd never seen a bride with an appetite like this.

Fortunately, the maidservant was nearby.

Loki immediately explained that Freyja hadn't eaten for eight nights.

She'd been so desperate to reach Jötunheim.

The giant was reassured.

Briefly.

When he tried to lift the veil and kiss his bride, he recoiled in horror.

Freyja's eyes were burning with fury.

Thrym had never seen such a terrifying expression.

Loki stepped in again.

Freyja, he explained, hadn't slept for eight nights.

She'd been so excited about the wedding.

It's difficult to know how convincing these explanations actually sounded.

But Thrym continued to believe them.

Then came the decisive moment.

According to the wedding custom in the poem, Mjölnir was to be brought in to consecrate the marriage.

Thrym ordered the hammer placed on the bride's lap.

And finally, Thor had exactly what he'd endured this entire performance to obtain.

His hammer was back in his hands.

The ancient poem tells us that Thor's heart rejoiced.

The wedding then took a very different turn.

Thor killed Thrym.

Then he killed the other giants gathered for the celebration.

Thrym's sister, who had hoped to receive a bridal gift, also died beneath the hammer.

The story ends with Mjölnir safely returned to its rightful owner.

Not exactly a romantic conclusion.

But the weapon had been recovered.

And perhaps the real hero of this particular story wasn't Thor alone.

It was Loki too.

He found the thief.

Helped organise the deception.

Played a convincing maidservant.

And successfully explained why the bride had eaten an entire ox.

That last achievement requires a very particular kind of talent.

But the story isn't only funny.

It also shows that Thor can set aside his pride to protect the world.

He's willing to look ridiculous because he understands how important the mission is.

Still, once the hammer is back in his hands, he immediately returns to his preferred method of conflict resolution.

No marriage.

No surviving wedding guests.

Weapon secured.

The wedding planners probably wouldn't recommend the experience to other couples.

## Chapter 6. Fishing with Thor. Should Have Stayed Home.

---

![Fishing with Thor. Should Have Stayed Home.](/visuals/norse/m04/06-thor-battles-the-sea-serpent-1448.webp)

---

If you think attending a wedding with Thor is the most dangerous thing you could possibly do, you clearly haven't heard about his fishing trip.

One day, Thor found himself visiting a giant named **Hymir**.

In *Hymiskviða*, or *The Lay of Hymir*, the story is connected with a search for an enormous cauldron the gods needed to brew ale for a feast hosted by Ægir.

In that account, Týr accompanies Thor on the journey.

But the fishing trip becomes the most famous part of the adventure.

The *Prose Edda* tells the fishing story separately, with some different details.

So remember: we have several surviving versions of the same myth.

Thor decided to go fishing with Hymir.

The giant wasn't particularly enthusiastic about his companion.

He suspected Thor might get cold, become exhausted, or simply prove too weak for serious fishing.

A rather bold assumption.

Thor asked for bait.

Hymir told him to find his own.

So Thor went to the giant's herd of cattle.

He selected the largest ox, named **Himinhrjot**.

Cut off its head.

And decided to use it as bait.

It's worth noting that the fishing trip stopped being ordinary at roughly this point.

Thor and Hymir got into the boat.

They began rowing.

First, they reached the usual fishing grounds.

Hymir suggested they stop.

Thor wanted to go farther.

They rowed on.

The giant began to worry.

Something lived in these waters that nobody should ever want to encounter.

**Jörmungandr.**

The World Serpent.

An enormous monster lying in the ocean around Midgard.

According to the myth, its body is so vast that it encircles the entire human world and bites its own tail.

This was the same creature Thor had tried to lift in Útgarða-Loki's fortress.

But then, the serpent had been hidden by an illusion.

Now Thor was about to face it for real.

He prepared an enormous hook.

Attached the ox's head.

And lowered the line into the depths.

The bait reached the seabed.

And Jörmungandr swallowed it.

Thor pulled.

The serpent fought back.

The water churned.

The boat rocked violently.

Thor braced his feet so fiercely that, according to the *Prose Edda*, they broke through the bottom of the boat, leaving him standing on the seabed itself.

He kept pulling.

And finally, the serpent's enormous head emerged from the water.

Imagine the scene.

A tiny boat in the middle of the vast northern sea.

Inside, the thunder god holding an enormous fishing line.

Beside him, a giant who is clearly starting to regret his plans for the day.

And rising from the water, a monster large enough to encircle the world.

Thor's eyes meet Jörmungandr's.

The serpent spits venom.

Thor prepares to strike with Mjölnir.

And then Hymir loses his nerve.

Terrified, he cuts the line.

Jörmungandr disappears back into the depths.

Some surviving versions differ in the details of what happens next, but the most important point remains the same.

The World Serpent survives.

Thor doesn't finish the battle.

And their final confrontation is postponed until Ragnarök.

One day, they'll meet again.

And both will die.

But for now, Thor returns from his fishing trip.

Without the serpent.

Without his intended trophy.

And with the deeply unpleasant feeling of unfinished business.

Although, if someone suggests using an ox's head as bait for a creature the size of the world, it's probably best to decline the invitation before leaving the shore.

Thor's adventures, however, are far from over.

Another giant is waiting for him.

A giant with a heart made of stone.

And a level of confidence in his own strength that bears a remarkable resemblance to Thor's.

## Chapter 7. A Heart of Stone. A Hammer of Iron.

---

![A Heart of Stone. A Hammer of Iron.](/visuals/norse/m04/07-thors-hammer-against-the-stone-giant-1448.webp)

---

A giant named **Hrungnir** was one of Thor's most formidable opponents.

According to the *Prose Edda*, it all began with an encounter between Hrungnir and Odin.

Odin was riding Sleipnir, his eight-legged horse.

The giant saw him and began arguing about whose horse was faster.

Hrungnir owned a magnificent stallion named Gullfaxi.

The argument turned into a race.

Odin galloped towards Asgard.

Hrungnir followed.

So enthusiastically that he rode straight into the gods' territory.

The Æsir invited him to a feast.

The giant started drinking.

Then drank some more.

And eventually became so drunk that he threatened to destroy Asgard, abduct the goddesses, and kill every god.

Not ideal behaviour for an invited guest.

The gods summoned Thor.

When Thor appeared with Mjölnir, Hrungnir protested that attacking an unarmed guest was beneath a great warrior.

He proposed a formal duel at an agreed location.

Thor accepted.

Hrungnir returned home to prepare.

His heart was made of stone.

According to the myth, it even had three sharp corners.

His head was stone too.

As was his shield.

For a weapon, he carried an enormous whetstone.

The giants decided to help their champion.

They made a gigantic being out of clay.

They named it **Mökkurkálfi**.

According to the *Prose Edda*, it was enormous but exceptionally cowardly.

Even the specially manufactured combat assistant was emotionally unprepared for the battle.

Thjálfi accompanied Thor to the duel.

The very same boy who had once broken the goat's bone.

He was now joining the thunder god in one of his most dangerous fights.

Thjálfi tricked Hrungnir.

He told the giant that Thor intended to attack from below the ground.

Hrungnir placed his stone shield beneath his feet.

Leaving himself to face Thor with only the whetstone in his hands.

Thor approached amid the roar of thunder.

Lightning flashed between the opponents.

Hrungnir hurled his enormous whetstone.

Thor threw Mjölnir.

The weapons collided in mid-air.

The hammer shattered the stone.

One piece fell to the ground.

Another struck Thor in the head.

A fragment lodged in his forehead.

But Mjölnir continued on its course.

And crushed Hrungnir's skull.

The giant was dead.

Unfortunately, as he fell, he landed directly on Thor.

One of his enormous legs pinned the thunder god beneath him.

Thor was trapped under the body of his defeated opponent.

The other Æsir tried to free him.

They couldn't.

Then **Magni**, Thor's son, appeared.

According to the *Prose Edda*, he was just three nights old.

Yet he possessed such extraordinary strength that he managed to lift the giant's leg and release his father.

Thor was saved.

As a reward, Magni received Gullfaxi, the horse that had belonged to Hrungnir.

There's something particularly interesting about this story.

Thor won.

He destroyed a formidable opponent.

But then found himself helpless beneath the giant's body.

And a child had to rescue him.

An admittedly rather unusual child.

**Even the greatest strength sometimes needs a helping hand.**

But Thor still had another problem.

The fragment of whetstone remained lodged in his forehead.

Later, a seeress named Gróa attempted to remove it through magical spells.

Thor was so pleased with her efforts that he began telling her good news about her husband.

Gróa became so delighted that she forgot to continue the spell.

And the fragment remained where it was.

A remarkably fitting ending.

Thor had defeated a giant with a heart of stone.

But couldn't remove a tiny piece of stone from his own head.

Sometimes a problem really is too small for Mjölnir.

## Chapter 8. The Strongest. Not the All-Powerful.

By now, we know Thor considerably better.

At the beginning of this module, he was the mighty god of thunder.

Protector of Asgard and Midgard.

A warrior armed with Mjölnir, a belt of strength, and iron gloves.

What could possibly go wrong?

As it turns out, almost everything.

First, we learned that his greatest weapon existed because of one of Loki's stupid jokes.

Then we met the goats that could be resurrected after dinner.

And the boy whose appetite for bone marrow had rather serious consequences.

We travelled into the lands of giants.

Watched Thor attempt to lift a cat, drink the sea, and defeat old age.

Discovered how he had to dress as a bride to recover his own hammer.

Joined him on a fishing trip where the bait was an ox's head and the intended catch was a monster the size of the world.

And finally watched him defeat a giant with a heart of stone, only to become trapped beneath the giant's body.

If Asgard had a performance-review system, Thor's report would be rather mixed.

Giants defeated: numerous.

Worlds protected: regularly.

Health and safety violations: too many to count.

But behind the comedy lies a much more serious figure.

**Thor is a god who protects.**

He doesn't seek to know absolutely everything, like Odin.

He doesn't constantly try to trick everyone around him, like Loki.

His strength is directed against threats that might destroy the world's established order.

And although Thor is quick-tempered, occasionally arrogant, and not always particularly cautious, he repeatedly sets out to face danger.

Not because he has nothing to lose.

But because danger exists, and someone has to confront it.

Yet the myths never allow us to see him as invincible.

Thor cannot drink the sea.

He cannot defeat old age.

He doesn't always recognise deception.

And even the greatest weapon cannot protect him from the consequences of his own mistakes.

In this respect, he's surprisingly similar to Odin.

Odin tries to overcome uncertainty through knowledge.

Thor tries to overcome danger through strength.

Both are powerful.

Both possess extraordinary abilities.

And both discover that there are limits to what they can change.

But there's something else.

In almost all of Thor's most ridiculous adventures, one familiar character seems to be nearby.

Loki.

He cut off Sif's hair.

Started the wager that led to Mjölnir's creation.

Accompanied Thor on dangerous journeys.

Helped recover the stolen hammer.

Occasionally saved the day.

And sometimes caused the problem in the first place.

But Loki is more than an irritating travelling companion.

His role in the fate of the gods is far more complicated.

He can change his shape.

Outwit almost anyone.

Escape punishment.

And transform an entirely ordinary incident into a catastrophe on a cosmic scale.

But one day, his jokes will stop being funny.

And the consequences will be so devastating that not even Thor can fix them with his hammer.

**In the next module, we'll meet Loki.**

And discover why some problems are better left unsolved.

Especially his way.

# Self-check

---

## 1. How did Thor recover Mjölnir after the giant Thrym stole it?

A. Odin bought it back by sacrificing his remaining eye.  
B. Freyja defeated Thrym in a magical contest.  
C. Thor challenged Thrym to an honourable duel and won the hammer back.  
D. Thor dressed as a bride, while Loki accompanied him disguised as a maidservant.  

**Answer:** D.

Asgard's greatest protector recovered his weapon in a wedding dress. Thrym got the bride he asked for. Just not quite the bride he expected.

---

## 2. What did Thor use as bait on his fishing trip with Hymir?

A. The head of an enormous ox.  
B. The golden ring Draupnir.  
C. One of his magical goats.  
D. Loki. He was responsible for so many problems anyway.  

**Answer:** A.

Thor used an ox's head and caught Jörmungandr. After that, ordinary fishing with a rod seems rather lacking in ambition.

---

## 3. Who freed Thor when the dead Hrungnir fell on top of him?

A. Odin, using an ancient spell.  
B. Magni, Thor's very young son.  
C. Loki, after transforming into a giant.  
D. Thjálfi, who called Asgard's emergency rescue team.  

**Answer:** B.

Magni was only three nights old, but he managed to free his father. Sometimes even the strongest god needs a child's help. Admittedly, this wasn't an ordinary child.
