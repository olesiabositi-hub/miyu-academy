# Module 2

# Osiris. Family Relationships Taken Way Too Far.

**Time:** about 20–25 minutes

---

## Introduction. The Most Dangerous Family Gathering in Egyptian History

---

Picture this: a celebration at the palace. Lamps are burning, guests are laughing, wine is flowing, and in the middle of the hall stands a magnificent wooden chest. The host proposes an innocent little game: whoever fits inside it perfectly gets to keep it.

One by one, the guests try it out. Too short for one. Too narrow for another. A third guest really shouldn't have accepted that extra helping.

Finally, the king lies down inside.

The chest fits perfectly.

The lid slams shut.

And suddenly everyone realizes this was never a competition.

That, according to one of the most famous later retellings, is how the murder of Osiris begins. His own brother, Set, will kill him. His wife, Isis, will set out to find his body. Their son, Horus, will defend his right to the throne. And Osiris himself will gain power over the place every human being will eventually reach.

It sounds like a family saga that even the Egyptian papyrus industry would struggle to keep up with. But behind the betrayal, magic, and fight over the inheritance lies a much more interesting question: **what do you do when death destroys something that seemed eternal?**

There's one important thing to understand. There is no single ancient Egyptian “script” of this myth, written down from beginning to end. Different parts of the story are known from texts belonging to different periods. The detailed plot involving the chest and the journey to Byblos was made especially famous by the Greek author Plutarch, who wrote in the Roman period. We'll follow a clear narrative, but we won't pretend all its details are equally ancient.

Now let's go back to the time when Osiris was still alive and the problems with the succession were only just beginning.

## Chapter 1. The Perfect Ruler. Suspicious Already.

---

![Osiris as king of the fertile Nile valley](/visuals/egypt/m02/01-osiris-king-of-the-nile-1448.webp)

---

If you remember the previous module, the world of the Egyptian gods had already acquired a sky, an earth, a sun, and some fairly complicated family connections. Now we're going to meet the family in which those connections will turn into a political crisis.

In one of the major traditions, Osiris's father is Geb, god of the earth. His mother is Nut, goddess of the sky. His brother and sisters are Set, Isis, and Nephthys.

Yes, Isis is both Osiris's sister and his wife. Set is Osiris's brother and the husband of Nephthys, their sister. Among the ancient Egyptian gods, marriages like these were not intended to be sensational plot twists: they helped explain the relationships between divine powers and kingship. We shouldn't apply ordinary human family rules directly to this world.

Osiris receives royal power. Don't go looking for his coronation date in a list of historical pharaohs: we're dealing with a **mythical ruler**, not a person whose reign can be verified archaeologically.

But what kind of ruler did people imagine him to be?

In Plutarch's later account, Osiris doesn't simply wear a crown. He teaches people to grow grain, live by laws, perform rituals, and stop treating brute force as the universal solution to every problem. Before him, people supposedly lived in an almost lawless state; with him comes an ordered way of life.

He represents the ideal of a good king who does more than issue commands. He transforms a place where people can only survive into a world where they can build a future.

For Egypt, that image is especially powerful. Imagine the narrow green valley of the Nile. Desert stretches away on either side. Without water and fertile soil, there will be no harvest; without a harvest, there will be no cities, no temples, and no state. Osiris was associated with fertility and the renewal of life—not because he personally went out into the fields with a hoe every spring, but because his story expressed life's ability to return.

The Egyptians had a concept called **ma'at**: right order, truth, justice, and the harmonious functioning of the world. It's not quite the same as “following the rules,” nor does it simply mean “being a good person.” Ma'at is what keeps the cosmos from collapsing back into destructive disorder, makes kingship legitimate, and holds society in balance.

Osiris, in his role as a just king, naturally belongs to this world of order.

And then Set appears.

He's often portrayed as simply a “god of evil,” but that's far too convenient. Set is associated with the desert, storms, foreign lands, strength, and danger. Yet in some Egyptian traditions he protects the sun god Ra by fighting the monstrous serpent Apep. At first glance, this creates a strange picture: the very same god can defend cosmic order against chaos—and commit the crime that shatters that order.

Here's the important point: the Egyptian gods don't always fall neatly into two columns marked “good” and “bad.” Their roles changed between myths, places, and periods. Set is a dangerous force, but he's not a cardboard villain wearing a sign that says “MAIN ANTAGONIST.”

That makes what he does all the more powerful.

Because the conflict doesn't begin as a struggle between good and some unmistakable absolute evil. It begins **inside a family and inside the divine order itself**.

Osiris is king. Set is his brother. Power, intimacy, jealousy, and the right to inherit the throne all exist within the same palace.

And when family and state are almost the same thing, a family quarrel can easily become a catastrophe on a national scale.

So here we have a prosperous world, a legitimate ruler, and a brother whose strength cannot bear to come second. All that's left is to invite everyone to a party.

## Chapter 2. A Chest That Fit a Little Too Perfectly

---

![Set's banquet and the chest in the middle of the hall](/visuals/egypt/m02/02-set-banquet-and-the-chest-1448.webp)

---

The murder of Osiris is one of the central stories of Egyptian religion. But different traditions described the manner of his death in different ways. Egyptian sources include motifs of violent death, drowning, and the search for his body. The particularly dramatic scene involving the chest, however, is known to us primarily through Plutarch's *On Isis and Osiris*.

So let's be clear: what we're watching now is like **a later, highly detailed screen adaptation of a much older myth**.

Plutarch tells us that Set—whom he calls by the Greek name Typhon—prepares a conspiracy. He finds out the exact measurements of Osiris's body and commissions a magnificent chest made precisely to those dimensions.

That alone might have raised suspicions. When a relative starts taking an unusually meticulous interest in your measurements, it may not be because they're buying you a birthday present.

At the feast, Set shows the guests the chest and promises to give it to whoever fits it perfectly. Everyone has a go. But the chest is either too large or too small.

Finally, Osiris lies down inside.

And everything matches: the length, the width, the proportions.

One of those rare occasions when a perfect fit is not good news.

The conspirators immediately slam the lid shut, seal the chest, and throw it into the Nile. The king is trapped inside; the river carries him away.

Notice the cruelty of the idea itself. Set doesn't challenge Osiris to an honest duel. He turns trust into a trap. An invitation becomes a weapon; an object of luxury becomes a coffin; a party game becomes an execution.

And there's another detail: the Nile, the source of Egypt's life, becomes the road to death. The river that feeds the country carries its king away. In the ancient Egyptian imagination, water could signify not only fertility but also danger, transition, and the unknown.

Why does Set do it?

The best-known explanation is ambition and rivalry for power. But the sources also offer other motives: in some later traditions, for example, a marital conflict involving Nephthys enters the picture. If we tried to put every version into one official case file, we'd end up not with an investigation but with an endless dispute between witnesses.

So for our story, the essential point is this: **Set removes the legitimate king, calling the previous order and the right to rule into question**.

Osiris vanishes. The palace receives no ordinary explanation of where its ruler has gone. No honest announcement of a transfer of power. No guarantees for those who remained loyal to him.

And Isis is left with the most terrible knowledge: the man she loves will not come back on his own.

This is where the myth could have ended as a tragedy. Brother murders brother, the world loses its king, and force defeats justice.

But at this exact point, the story changes its main character.

From now on, everything depends not on who is stronger, but on **who refuses to accept destruction as the final word**.

And that person—or, more precisely, that goddess—will be Isis.

---

## Chapter 3. Isis. Missing Husband. Potential Consequences for the Entire Cosmos.

---

![Isis finds the chest hidden in the palace column at Byblos](/visuals/egypt/m02/03-isis-search-in-byblos-1448.webp)

---

It's easy to imagine Isis as a grieving widow whose role is to cry and wait. In the Egyptian myth, it's almost the other way around.

Yes, Isis mourns Osiris. But then she takes action.

She searches for her husband's body. She travels along the water, asks questions, follows traces, and refuses to let his disappearance become final. In ancient Egyptian texts, Isis is a powerful goddess of magic and protection. Her strength lies not in undoing what happened with a single gesture, but in **finding what was lost and restoring connections**.

In Plutarch's detailed version, the chest containing Osiris's body drifts far beyond Egypt—to Byblos, a city on the coast of present-day Lebanon. It washes ashore. A tree grows around it, gradually enclosing the chest within its trunk.

The local king sees the magnificent tree, orders it cut down, and has its trunk turned into a pillar supporting the palace roof.

It's an extraordinary image. Somewhere in a palace, right at the heart of everyday life, stands a column. People walk past it, talk, eat, and go about their business. And no one knows that a dead god lies hidden inside the wood.

Isis discovers where she needs to look. She makes her way to Byblos, enters the circle of the royal family, and eventually gains access to the column. Plutarch's account contains many more details at this point: a queen, a child, the goddess's mysterious actions, and the moment when Isis reveals her true identity. We won't turn this lesson into a retelling of every scene, but we will preserve the central event.

**Isis retrieves the chest containing Osiris.**

It seems the hardest part is over.

She has found the one she was looking for. She has completed a journey that seemed impossible in itself. Now she can go home.

But the myth doesn't let us feel relief quite so easily.

According to Plutarch, after the chest is brought back to Egypt, Set discovers Osiris's body once again. This time he dismembers it and scatters the pieces across the country.

Why such cruelty? The first murder took Osiris's life. The second assault seems designed to take away even the possibility of his being whole, recognizable, and properly buried.

You'll sometimes hear that there were fourteen pieces. **The number fourteen belongs to Plutarch's particular later version; it is not a single rule governing all Egyptian mythology.** Different local traditions offered their own explanations for where parts of the body were found and why particular shrines were associated with Osiris.

What's particularly striking is the way the scale of the story changes. At first, there was one body inside one chest. Now the traces of the tragedy are scattered throughout the land. Egypt itself almost becomes a map of loss.

Isis sets out again.

You might reasonably ask, “How many more times will she have to do the same job?”

The myth's answer is harsh: as many as it takes.

This isn't a story in which magic solves the problem on the first try. It's a story about persistence in the face of something that cannot be repaired by ordinary means.

There's also an important historical nuance. Egyptian traditions concerning the search for Osiris, the mourning of him, and his restoration are considerably older than Plutarch. But **the continuous sequence “feast — chest — Byblos — return — dismemberment”** comes specifically from his later narrative. We shouldn't assume that Egyptians spent thousands of years telling the story in precisely this order.

What lies ahead for Isis is no longer a search operation.

It's a task for which humanity has never devised an ordinary solution: to put a dead god back together and give him the possibility of continuing to exist.

## Chapter 4. Reassembling a God. Instructions Not Included.

---

![Isis and Nephthys guard Osiris while Anubis assists](/visuals/egypt/m02/04-isis-nephthys-anubis-restoration-1448.webp)

---

Imagine the silence after a long search. Now Isis knows where Osiris is. Or rather, she knows where what remains of him is.

And this is where the Egyptian myth stops being simply a detective story about a murdered king. It becomes a story about how people came to understand death not only as disappearance, but also as **a rupture that might somehow be overcome**.

Isis is not alone.

Her most important ally is Nephthys. Yes, Set's wife. Yes, the sister of Isis and Osiris. And yes, the family situation has reached a level of complexity at which a quick family tree is no longer much help.

But Nephthys's role is crucial: she mourns Osiris alongside Isis and helps protect the dead. In Egyptian funerary imagery, the two goddesses often stand on either side of a body, stretch out their wings, and guard and support the person who has died.

These are not just beautiful gestures. Mourning the dead and caring for their bodies formed part of religious restoration. Naming the deceased, addressing them, reassembling their body, protecting them from further destruction—all of this mattered.

This is where Anubis also enters the story: a god often depicted as a jackal or as a man with a jackal's head. He is associated with embalming, funerary rituals, and the protection of the dead. In images and texts, he helps prepare Osiris's body for a new state of existence.

If you've encountered the phrase “Anubis is the son of Osiris and Nephthys,” it's worth knowing that this parentage does indeed appear in some versions. But **it is not universal across all Egyptian sources**, and we shouldn't turn it into yet another indisputable entry in the family tree.

Isis and Nephthys bring together what has been separated. The body is protected and made whole again. Anubis takes part in its funerary restoration. Isis uses magical words and the power of her wings to restore Osiris's capacity for life.

Notice: this is **not** like an ordinary resurrection story in which the hero stretches, gets up from his bed, and walks back to the palace to personally sign an order for Set's arrest.

Osiris does not return to his former earthly life. Magic enables him to continue existing—but in another way.

And there's another twist.

In a widely attested Egyptian strand of the myth, it is after Osiris's restoration that Isis conceives their son, Horus. The god whose life was violently ended becomes the father of an heir. In Plutarch's later account, some details of the family chronology work differently; for this scene, we are drawing primarily on the Egyptian tradition of conception after death.

The episode is strange, solemn, and extremely important all at once. Osiris has already died. No one has “undone” his death. Yet it has not destroyed the possibility of a future.

Horus is not simply a child. He is the answer to Set's attempt to cut off the legitimate line of succession.

But the child still needs to be kept safe. Isis hides and raises Horus among the marshes of the Nile Delta, far from Set. In some stories, the young Horus is threatened by illness, venomous creatures, and attacks, and once again Isis's magic becomes his protection. The mother who managed to restore her dead husband's wholeness now protects her living son.

The image of Isis thus brings two things together: grief and action. She doesn't pretend the tragedy never happened. She deals with its consequences.

We can also see here something of the meaning of the Egyptian approach to mummification. Preserving a body was not merely about slowing the decomposition of tissue. In religious thought, the deceased had to regain the ability to be themselves: recognizable, connected to their loved ones, the gods, and the world.

In other words, after death, the point wasn't simply to “preserve the shape.” It was to make a person **whole** again.

But bodily wholeness alone is not enough for Osiris. His royal dignity, his rights, and his place in the order of the world have also been taken away. Until Set's crime is answered, the restoration remains unfinished.

Which means that the next part of the story belongs to his son.

## Chapter 5. Horus vs. Set. A Family Dispute the Gods Had to Settle.

---

![Horus and Set before the assembly of the gods](/visuals/egypt/m02/05-horus-and-set-divine-tribunal-1448.webp)

---

Horus grows up. And with him grows a question the Egyptian world cannot possibly avoid answering:

**Who has the right to rule after Osiris?**

On one side is Set: influential, powerful, dangerous, and not remotely inclined to give up power voluntarily.

On the other is Horus, the son of Osiris and Isis. The heir of the murdered ruler.

It's a familiar situation, even without the pyramids: one claimant relies on force; the other, on descent and legitimacy. But for the Egyptians, this was more than a quarrel between two ambitious relatives. Its outcome would determine **what makes a king legitimate**.

Stories about the conflict between Horus and Set survive in different forms. A particularly vivid literary version is preserved in the Chester Beatty I Papyrus, from the New Kingdom. There, the gods don't solve the problem with one lightning-fast verdict. They argue, hold hearings, change their minds, and put the claimants through trials.

At one point, the whole thing starts to resemble an exceptionally drawn-out board meeting. Except that the participants are immortal, the agenda concerns the entire kingship of Egypt, and the rules of procedure periodically give way to magic and fighting.

Isis does not stand aside. She defends her son's claim, resorts to cunning, and tries to make the gods recognize the injustice of the situation. Set, meanwhile, fights to have his own claims recognized. Some episodes of this story are surprisingly bold, comic, and even awkward for the solemn “mythology from a school textbook” that we might expect.

It's a useful reminder: ancient Egyptian texts were not always sacred and ceremonial in tone. They knew how to portray gods as argumentative, offended, stubborn, and far from flawless.

In other versions of the conflict, Horus and Set engage in direct combat. Horus loses or injures an eye; later it is restored—with the god Thoth playing an important role in several traditions. **The Eye of Horus**, known as the *wedjat*, becomes a symbol of healing, protection, and restored wholeness. It's not merely a “logo with spectacular eyeliner,” but an image of something damaged and made whole again.

Now look at how this story echoes the fate of Osiris.

The father's body was taken apart—and restored. The son's eye was damaged—and healed. The legitimate order was shattered—and now it must be brought back.

Again and again, Egyptian mythology returns us to the same idea: **what has been broken should have a chance to become whole**.

Ultimately, Horus's right to kingship is affirmed. Set does not receive Osiris's throne. His subsequent fate differs from one version to another; what matters is that the dispute over legitimate succession is resolved in favor of the son.

That doesn't mean Set disappears from Egyptian religion forever. Remember how we discussed his ambiguous role? In some texts, he is Osiris's murderer; in others, he is a formidable force who helps protect the solar barque. The Egyptians didn't need to erase one tradition for another to exist.

Horus, meanwhile, becomes the living king—an image of legitimate power on earth. That is why a reigning pharaoh could be understood and represented through the image of Horus, while a dead king could be linked to Osiris.

This is why the son's victory in the divine court also matters to the father. Osiris has received more than care for his body: his cause has been vindicated, the violation of order has been acknowledged, and his legitimate place in the divine world has been restored.

The heir has defended the name of the dead.

But one final question remains. If Osiris no longer rules Egypt, if his earthly reign is not restored—what does he receive instead?

The answer will radically change the way we think about death.

## Chapter 6. Osiris's Second Crown. His Kingdom Is Now Beyond Death.

---

![Osiris enthroned as ruler of the dead](/visuals/egypt/m02/06-osiris-afterlife-judgment-1448.webp)

---

Osiris does not return to his old palace.

He gains another kingdom.

He becomes lord of the dead—one of the most important gods in Egyptian ideas about existence after death. Horus rules in the world of the living; Osiris rules in the place the living cannot yet enter.

At first glance, this might seem like a sad compromise: the earthly throne is lost, so he's issued a different one. But in the Egyptian worldview, this is not a demotion. It is a magnificent transformation.

**Osiris is the first great model of the idea that death does not necessarily mean total annihilation.**

He has passed through murder, disintegration, restoration, and vindication. This means there is a path, somewhere in the story of the world, from destruction to another form of life. And that path matters to more than gods alone.

Think of how Egyptian artists depicted Osiris: his body often tightly wrapped like a mummy, his hands crossed, holding the royal emblems of crook and flail, and a tall feathered crown on his head. His skin is frequently green or black.

Those colors are no accident. Green could be associated with vegetation and renewal; black, with the fertile soil of the Nile and rebirth. Here is a dead god portrayed through the signs of continuing life.

In some periods, rituals involved making figures of Osiris out of earth sown with grain. They were watered, and shoots emerged from their surfaces. It might seem like a simple effect: a seed germinates. But that was precisely the power of the image. Something that looks motionless and buried is capable of new growth.

These ritual “germinating Osirises” are not proof that Egyptians treated the myth as a manual of agriculture. They are a vivid religious statement: renewal is possible even where everything seems to point to an ending.

But how did a human being enter Osiris's realm, and what happened there?

Egyptian beliefs about the afterlife changed over the centuries. We can't describe them as though every Egyptian, in every period, followed exactly the same itinerary after death. Yet one of the most famous images, especially familiar from the *Book of the Dead*, is **the weighing of the heart**.

Imagine a judgment hall. The dead person stands before the divine order. Their heart is placed on one pan of a balance; on the other is a feather associated with Ma'at, truth and justice. Anubis takes part in checking the scales. Thoth, god of writing and knowledge, records the result. Osiris sits enthroned as lord and judge of the afterlife. If the test ends in failure, the deceased faces Ammit—a creature combining the features of dangerous animals, who devours those who do not pass judgment.

This is not a quiz about the names of the gods, nor an opportunity to talk the system into agreeing with you through a beautiful presentation. Here, the heart is connected with a person's life, actions, and the truth about them.

Of course, this is a religious image, not a report of an actual event. But it expresses a striking idea: **continued existence depends not only on how a person was buried, but also on what kind of person they were**.

Death stops being just a doorway into darkness. Beyond it are relationships, duties, judgment, memory, the possibility of vindication, and admission into a community of divine beings.

One further clarification is worth making. People sometimes say, “An Egyptian became Osiris after death.” In funerary formulas, the deceased could indeed be called “Osiris so-and-so.” But that did not always mean literally becoming the one and only god Osiris. Rather, the dead person acquired a special connection with Osiris, followed his path of restoration, and hoped to enter the world of the blessed dead. Scholars stress how important that distinction is.

And here the entire story comes together.

Set destroyed Osiris's body—Isis and her allies restored its wholeness. Set deprived him of his place—Horus secured recognition of the legitimate succession. Death ended his life—and the possibility of another form of existence emerged.

So the myth works on several levels at once: it explains the divine foundations of kingship, gives meaning to funerary rituals, links fertility with renewal, and shapes the human fear of death into a story in which the end is not the only possible answer.

That is why Osiris is much more than “Egyptian god of the dead” in a short reference table.

He is the god whose power begins where everything seemed to have ended.

## Conclusion. What Remained After Osiris

---

We began with a chest snapping shut at a festive banquet.

Let's end with another image: Osiris sitting on a throne in the realm of the dead. Gods stand nearby. Before him, judgment continues, as do the memory of the dead and the hopes of those who wish to move beyond.

In the first scene, everything depended on one brother's deception. In the last, an entire world is trying to answer the question of what might be stronger than destruction.

Isis did not undo death. Horus did not restore his father's former life. Not even the gods pretended the tragedy had never happened.

Instead, they restored what could still be restored: the body, the name, justice, continuity, and a connection to the future.

**The central idea of this module:** Osiris became a symbol not of simple “resurrection,” but of the return of wholeness and meaning after destruction—for a god, for royal power, and for a human being facing death.

And now that we've met the ruler of the world of the dead, it's time to ask a question the ancient Egyptians took remarkably seriously: **how exactly do you prepare for the journey there?**

Spoiler: bandages alone won't be enough.
