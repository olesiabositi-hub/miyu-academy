# Module 6

# Goddesses, Witches, and a Fate That Couldn't Care Less About Your Plans.

*The story of women who could see the future, communicate with invisible powers, choose the warriors who fell in battle, and shape human destinies. One managed to outwit Odin himself. Another commanded magic and claimed half the battle-dead. And a third discovered that even a love strong enough to survive death couldn't necessarily overcome fate.*

**Time:** about 34–44 minutes

---

## Chapter 1. Frigg. Knows Everything. Doesn't Have to Explain.

---

![Frigg. Knows Everything. Doesn't Have to Explain.](/visuals/norse/m06/01-frigg-1448.webp)

---

In our previous adventures, we met three rather extraordinary residents of Asgard.

Odin was willing to sacrifice his own eye for wisdom.

Thor preferred solving problems with a hammer.

And Loki could turn an entirely ordinary day into a catastrophe requiring the attention of every other god.

Now it's time to meet those who rarely needed such dramatic displays of strength.

**The goddesses.**

Let's begin with Frigg.

Odin's wife.

Baldr's mother.

One of the most important goddesses in the Norse world.

We've already encountered her in the tragic story of Baldr's death. It was Frigg who tried to protect her son by securing promises from everything in existence.

But there's much more to Frigg than the image of a grieving mother.

She is associated with marriage, family relationships, high status among the gods, and knowledge of fate.

In *Lokasenna*, there's an intriguing suggestion that Frigg knows people's destinies but chooses not to reveal them.

That doesn't necessarily mean she literally knows every detail of the future. But her connection with special knowledge is an important part of her character.

And there's one story that demonstrates something else about Frigg.

She doesn't just know how to keep secrets.

She knows how to win.

Even when her opponent is Odin himself.

The story survives in **Grímnismál**, usually translated as *The Sayings of Grímnir*.

One day, Odin and Frigg were sitting on Hliðskjálf, the high seat from which they could survey the worlds.

They were watching the fortunes of two brothers.

Long ago, the boys had become lost and spent a winter in the home of an elderly couple.

The couple had taken them in and cared for them.

In reality, their hosts were Odin and Frigg, disguised as ordinary people.

Odin looked after the younger brother, Geirröd.

Frigg cared for the elder, Agnar.

But the brothers' lives eventually took very different directions.

Geirröd managed to return home, seize power, and become a king.

Agnar remained far from his homeland.

Odin pointed this out to Frigg with considerable satisfaction.

His fosterling was ruling a kingdom.

Frigg's fosterling was living in a cave with a giantess.

You can almost imagine Odin's expression as he said it.

Frigg, however, wasn't prepared to admit defeat.

She declared that Geirröd was a terrible ruler.

So miserly and inhospitable, she claimed, that he tortured guests whenever he thought they were eating too much.

Odin objected.

The poem makes clear that the accusation was originally false.

But the two gods made a wager.

And Frigg decided to influence the outcome.

She sent her attendant, Fulla, to Geirröd.

Fulla warned the king that a dangerous magician would soon enter his lands.

He could be recognised by one unusual sign.

Even the fiercest dogs would refuse to attack him.

Before long, a stranger arrived in the kingdom.

He wore a dark blue cloak.

And called himself **Grímnir**.

The dogs didn't touch him.

The king immediately remembered the warning.

And ordered the stranger arrested.

Grímnir refused to explain who he was.

So Geirröd had him placed between two enormous fires.

To force him to speak.

For **eight nights**, the stranger remained between the flames.

Nobody brought him food.

Nobody helped.

Only the king's young son, also named Agnar, approached the prisoner and offered him a drink.

The boy said his father was wrong to torture an innocent guest.

And then the stranger began to speak.

Grímnir described the gods, the worlds, their halls, and the secrets of the universe.

He revealed things no ordinary human could possibly know.

And gradually, it became clear who was sitting between the fires.

**It was Odin.**

The Allfather.

The chief of the gods.

In person.

When Geirröd realised he'd been torturing Odin for eight nights, he rushed to free him.

But disaster struck.

The king fell onto his own sword.

And died.

His son Agnar inherited the throne.

And Frigg, apparently, won the wager.

Although she'd achieved it by creating the very circumstances that made her original accusation come true.

An ingenious strategy.

And a somewhat questionable approach to fair competition.

But this story demonstrates something important.

Frigg doesn't simply occupy an honoured place beside Odin.

She can act independently.

Use information.

Manipulate events.

And defeat the being generally regarded as the wisest of the gods.

Without a hammer, an army, or nine nights hanging from the World Tree.

Although Odin did end up spending eight nights between two fires.

Perhaps the couple were a little more careful when choosing subjects for future marital disagreements.

Now let's meet another goddess.

She had a famous golden necklace, a chariot pulled by cats, and authority that extended considerably further than love.

## Chapter 2. Freyja. Love, Gold, and Some Very Serious Responsibilities.

---

![Freyja. Love, Gold, and Some Very Serious Responsibilities.](/visuals/norse/m06/02-freyja-1448.webp)

---

Imagine a majestic woman wearing magnificent golden jewellery.

Around her neck is a precious necklace.

She travels in a chariot drawn by two cats.

At first glance, it seems we're about to meet another goddess of beauty and love.

This is **Freyja**.

But don't jump to conclusions.

In Norse mythology, beautiful jewellery and romantic interests sometimes come with responsibilities that even experienced warriors would prefer to avoid.

Freyja comes from the Vanir.

Her father is Njord, a god associated with the sea and wealth.

Her brother is Freyr, associated with fertility and prosperity.

After the war between the Æsir and Vanir, Freyja came to live among the gods of Asgard.

And became one of the most significant figures in their world.

She is associated with love, desire, wealth, and magic.

But also with war and death.

Yes, death.

In *Grímnismál*, we're told that Freyja chooses half of those who fall in battle.

Odin receives the other half.

Freyja rules over a place called **Fólkvangr**.

There stands her magnificent hall, **Sessrúmnir**.

So not every warrior killed in battle automatically ends up with Odin in Valhalla.

Some belong to Freyja.

The ancient sources don't provide a detailed explanation of exactly how the dead are divided or what criteria determine the selection.

So it's probably best not to imagine the gods sitting with neatly organised lists and a scoring system.

Although the idea of divine recruitment is rather entertaining.

Especially considering the candidates are already dead.

But Freyja is famous for much more than this.

One of her greatest treasures is a necklace called **Brísingamen**.

Ancient texts mention it as Freyja's precious ornament, although the stories surrounding its origins differ between traditions.

Later narratives offer more elaborate, sometimes scandalous explanations.

We shouldn't automatically treat them as one unified Viking Age myth.

For our purposes, something else matters.

Brísingamen symbolises the goddess's beauty, wealth, and elevated status.

It's so closely associated with her that when she becomes furious, even the necklace can become part of the disaster.

Remember when the giant Thrym demanded her hand in marriage in exchange for Thor's stolen hammer?

Freyja became so angry that Brísingamen broke apart.

An entirely understandable response to an exceptionally unwelcome marriage proposal.

But Freyja has a love story of her own.

Her husband is named **Óðr**.

The *Prose Edda* tells us that Óðr departed on long journeys.

Freyja travelled among different peoples searching for him.

And she wept.

But her tears were no ordinary tears.

They turned into red gold.

Other imagery in the tradition also connects her tears with precious substances such as amber.

Freyja mourns her absent husband.

And even her grief becomes something valuable.

It's a beautiful and melancholy image.

But it would be a mistake to reduce Freyja entirely to love and longing.

She possesses knowledge of a form of magic called **seiðr**.

According to *Ynglinga Saga*, she introduced the Æsir to this practice.

We'll soon explore what that magic involved, and why modern reconstructions of ancient rituals need to be treated with care.

For now, remember the essential point.

Freyja is connected with powers that ordinary weapons cannot command.

She is associated with desire, fate, magic, and death.

And she combines qualities that modern stories often divide among several different characters.

A lover.

A sorceress.

A mistress of wealth.

And a chooser of fallen warriors.

She also owns a chariot pulled by cats.

The *Prose Edda* really does describe Freyja travelling in a chariot drawn by two cats.

The ancient text doesn't tell us their names.

Or much about their personalities.

So we can't confidently say they were ordinary domestic cats.

But we can certainly imagine Thor's expression.

Especially if his own goats had recently caused another transport-related incident.

Charming as the cats may be, though, they aren't the most interesting part of Freyja's story.

Her connection with magic is.

Because in the ancient North, people believed in practices that could reveal hidden knowledge, influence events, and approach powers beyond ordinary human reach.

And those who possessed such abilities could command enormous respect.

Along with a considerable amount of fear.

## Chapter 3. Seiðr. Magic Without a Safety Setting.

---

![Seiðr. Magic Without a Safety Setting.](/visuals/norse/m06/03-seidr-1448.webp)

---

We've already encountered magic in Norse mythology.

Odin used spells to preserve Mimir's head.

Loki changed his shape.

Freyja possessed secret knowledge.

But now we're going to explore a particular practice that occupied an important place in the beliefs of the ancient North.

**Seiðr.**

The term is associated with magical practices, divination, and attempts to influence hidden forces.

But there's something we need to establish immediately.

We don't possess a complete, universally accepted manual explaining how every ancient seiðr ritual was performed.

Our sources were created at different times.

Some describe mythology.

Others tell stories in sagas.

Still others reflect religious practices that their authors may already have viewed from a historical distance.

So we can't reduce seiðr to one neat, universal form of "Viking magic".

But some fascinating details survive.

For example, *The Saga of Erik the Red* tells us about a seeress named **Thorbjorg**, known as the Little Völva.

The story takes place in Greenland.

The community is suffering through difficult times.

Bad weather, illness, and food shortages threaten the settlement.

Nobody knows when things will improve.

So they invite a seeress.

Not for entertainment.

Not because everyone is in the mood for a mysterious prediction.

But because uncertainty has become dangerous.

Thorbjorg arrives as an honoured guest.

The saga describes her in considerable detail.

She wears special clothing.

She carries an ornamented staff.

A particular seat is prepared for her.

And her arrival transforms an ordinary gathering into an occasion on which people hope to receive answers.

Songs play an important role in the account.

They're associated with calling upon spiritual helpers.

A woman named Gudrid knows the necessary song.

Although she is Christian and initially reluctant to take part in a pagan ceremony.

Eventually, she agrees to help.

After the song has been performed, the seeress announces that the community's difficulties will soon end.

She also predicts aspects of Gudrid's future.

And here's what's especially interesting.

People aren't asking Thorbjorg about cosmic catastrophes in the distant future.

They're concerned about practical problems.

When will the famine end?

What will happen to our families?

Will we survive the winter?

Do we have a future?

In this sense, the seeress plays an important social role.

She offers people a way of making sense of events.

And hope that the unknown can, at least partly, be understood.

But how much evidence do we have that practices like these existed in real life?

This is where archaeology becomes useful.

Viking Age graves sometimes contain unusual objects.

For example, iron staffs that some researchers associate with seeresses.

At Fyrkat, in Denmark, archaeologists discovered the grave of a woman buried with valuable possessions and an unusual collection of objects.

Among them was a metal staff.

Archaeologists have suggested that she may have been a völva.

But the word **"may"** is essential here.

We can't ask the woman buried in the grave what she did during her lifetime.

And a single object doesn't automatically prove that its owner performed magical rituals.

Archaeological interpretations must therefore be distinguished from direct evidence.

Nevertheless, these discoveries help us imagine a world in which certain women may have held significant religious and social positions.

Positions that didn't necessarily depend on physical strength.

Knowledge, prophecy, and ritual expertise could also confer authority.

But seiðr had a complicated reputation.

In some Old Norse sources, men who practised certain kinds of magic could face accusations that challenged established ideas of masculinity and honour.

Even Odin, who possessed magical knowledge himself, became a target of such insults.

Which gives us a rather peculiar situation.

The chief of the gods practises magic.

But some forms of that same magic are regarded as socially questionable for men.

Asgard apparently had some deeply inconsistent ideas about acceptable professional skills.

Nevertheless, magic remained an important part of this world.

Because knowing the future might at least allow people to prepare for it.

And sometimes they were willing to pay a very high price.

Even when the prophecy was something they might have preferred never to hear.

Which brings us to our next story.

## Chapter 4. The Völva. Spoilers for the End of the World.

---

![The Völva. Spoilers for the End of the World.](/visuals/norse/m06/04-volva-1448.webp)

---

Imagine a cold northern evening.

The wind howls outside.

A fire burns inside the house.

People gather around a woman holding a staff.

She is older than many of those present.

She speaks calmly.

And perhaps she knows something nobody else does.

This is one way we might imagine a **völva** — a seeress of the ancient North.

The word is commonly associated with a staff or wand.

A völva is a woman believed to possess special knowledge and the ability to foretell events.

But the most famous seeress in Norse mythology doesn't limit herself to predicting harvests or the weather.

She knows the history of the entire universe.

From its beginning to its destruction.

And one day, Odin himself seeks her knowledge.

The story lies at the heart of **Völuspá — The Prophecy of the Seeress**.

We first encountered the poem in Module One, when we explored the creation of the world.

Now let's approach it from another direction.

Imagine that Odin stands before a being who can describe the past and future of the universe.

The Allfather has spent his existence searching for wisdom.

He sacrificed an eye.

Hung for nine nights from the World Tree.

Sought the secrets of ancient giants.

And now he has the opportunity to hear the entire story of existence.

The seeress begins to speak.

She remembers the oldest times.

Ginnungagap.

The first beings.

The creation of the earth.

The arrival of the gods and humans.

She describes Yggdrasil, the World Tree.

The Well of Urd.

The Norns.

The war between the Æsir and the Vanir.

The ancient conflicts whose consequences continue to shape the world.

At first, it sounds almost like a history lesson.

But gradually, the story turns towards the future.

And the atmosphere changes.

The seeress speaks of Baldr's death.

Of Loki's punishment.

Of the collapse of family bonds.

Of cruelty and betrayal.

Of a time when brothers will turn against one another.

When the familiar order will break down.

When the world enters its final age.

And then comes **Ragnarök**.

The great destruction of the gods.

Wolves will catch the heavenly lights.

Jörmungandr will rise from the ocean.

Fenrir will break free.

Loki will escape his chains.

Odin will face the monstrous wolf.

Thor will confront the World Serpent.

The world will be consumed by destruction.

And if you remember our previous modules, you'll already recognise some of those destined to take part.

For now, they're alive.

Arguing.

Travelling.

Making mistakes.

Yet the prophecy already describes their end.

For Odin, this knowledge is both priceless and terrifying.

He learns what awaits the world.

But receives no simple explanation of how to prevent it.

The poem, however, doesn't end with the destruction of everything.

After the catastrophe, the world rises again.

New land emerges from the sea.

Green.

Beautiful.

Some gods survive.

Others return.

Life continues.

But it is a different world.

We'll explore Ragnarök properly in Module Nine.

For now, something else matters.

The völva possesses knowledge that Odin himself desperately wants.

She doesn't fight giants.

She doesn't wield Mjölnir.

She doesn't command armies.

Yet her voice forces the chief of the gods to confront the most uncomfortable question of his existence.

**What do you do when you already know how everything ends?**

This is where Norse mythology becomes particularly fascinating.

Knowledge doesn't necessarily bring comfort.

Sometimes it destroys illusions.

Sometimes it reveals the limits of even the greatest authority.

And sometimes it describes your future catastrophe in such extraordinary detail that you'd rather the seeress stopped talking.

Preferably before reaching the bit about your own death.

But the völva continues.

Because the future has no obligation to be pleasant merely because someone paid for a prediction.

And there are other beings whose authority over fate is particularly formidable.

They dwell beside the World Tree.

Near an ancient well.

And they don't even promise that everyone will receive a fair outcome.

## Chapter 5. The Norns. Your Plans Have Been Reviewed. Rejected.

---

![The Norns. Your Plans Have Been Reviewed. Rejected.](/visuals/norse/m06/05-norns-1448.webp)

---

Deep beneath the branches of Yggdrasil lies an extraordinary place.

Near one of the World Tree's roots is a sacred spring.

**The Well of Urd.**

Its waters possess remarkable properties.

The *Prose Edda* tells us that the gods gather nearby and that water from the well helps preserve the great tree's branches.

But today, we're interested in more than Yggdrasil.

Near the well are three mysterious women.

Their names are:

**Urðr.**

**Verðandi.**

**Skuld.**

They are the three most famous **Norns**.

According to *Völuspá*, they determine human destinies and establish the laws governing people's lives.

Imagine three women beside an ancient well.

Around them stretch the enormous roots of the World Tree.

Beneath their feet lie stone and cold water.

Above them rise the branches connecting the worlds.

And here, things beyond ordinary human control are decided.

The length of a life.

Opportunities.

Happiness.

Misfortune.

Events that can change everything.

Each Norn's name suggests a particular meaning.

**Urðr** is associated with fate and what has already come to pass.

**Verðandi** suggests becoming, or what is happening.

**Skuld** is connected with obligation and what is yet to come.

That's why modern retellings frequently present the three Norns as Past, Present, and Future.

It's a beautiful arrangement.

Three women.

Three aspects of time.

One destiny.

But there's a complication.

The ancient sources don't give us such a straightforward, universally accepted division of responsibilities.

We can't confidently claim that Urðr handled only the past, Verðandi dealt exclusively with the present, and Skuld managed everything yet to happen.

That's a familiar modern interpretation based partly on their names, not a precise job description preserved in an ancient manuscript.

And there weren't only three Norns.

The *Prose Edda* describes others as well.

They come to human beings at birth and shape their destinies.

Some are connected with the gods.

Others with elves.

And others with dwarves.

Nor are they all alike.

In *Gylfaginning*, a perfectly reasonable question is raised.

If the Norns determine human destiny, why do some people enjoy long and prosperous lives while others suffer poverty, misfortune, and early death?

The answer involves differences among the Norns themselves.

Good Norns bring favourable destinies.

Others can bring misfortune.

A wonderful explanation.

Although probably not especially comforting to someone who drew the short straw.

And here we reach one of the most difficult ideas in Norse mythology.

**Fate isn't necessarily fair.**

The strong may die before the weak.

Good people may suffer.

A mighty god can learn about his own death and still be unable to prevent it.

And that doesn't always mean somebody made a mistake.

Sometimes that's simply how the world works.

But we shouldn't reduce the Norse understanding of fate to the idea that nobody ever makes a meaningful choice.

Heroes make decisions.

Gods fight.

People display courage or cowardice.

Promises matter.

Actions have consequences.

Belief in destiny exists alongside belief in personal responsibility.

And a tension emerges between them.

If fate is already known, why act?

If the end is inevitable, why resist?

If the Norns have determined the outcome, why fight?

The ancient myths don't answer these questions with one universal philosophical rule.

Instead, they tell stories.

Stories of characters learning about prophecies.

Trying to avoid terrible events.

And continuing to act even when hope seems almost gone.

Perhaps that's why the Norns matter so much.

They don't merely represent the future.

They remind us that even the greatest hero exists in a world that cannot be completely controlled.

There's another important detail.

The Norns also care for Yggdrasil.

The *Prose Edda* describes them taking water and clay from the Well of Urd and applying them to the tree so that its branches won't dry out or decay.

It's a remarkable image.

Beings associated with fate are simultaneously helping preserve the World Tree.

They don't simply stand aside and watch events unfold.

They participate in maintaining the very structure of existence.

Their responsibilities are considerably more complicated than delivering one prophecy to every human.

Although, considering the number of inhabitants across the worlds, even that would require a fairly substantial workforce.

But in Norse mythology, the question of fate leads us towards another recurring subject.

Death in battle.

And the beings who choose the fallen.

## Chapter 6. Valkyries. Recruitment After the Battle.

---

![Valkyries. Recruitment After the Battle.](/visuals/norse/m06/06-valkyries-1448.webp)

---

Imagine a battlefield after the fighting has ended.

A cold wind.

Fallen banners.

Broken spears.

The silence that follows the shouting and the clash of weapons.

And above the battlefield — mounted women.

Wearing gleaming armour.

Carrying weapons.

They appear wherever warriors' fates are decided.

**The Valkyries.**

Their name is commonly translated as **"choosers of the slain."**

They aren't simply beautiful female warriors, as modern films and games often like to portray them — complete with flowing hair, winged helmets, and flawless makeup after three days of combat.

In the ancient myths, Valkyries are associated with war, death, the selection of fallen warriors, and Odin's will.

They may appear before battles.

Accompany warriors.

Influence the course of combat.

And determine who is destined to fall.

But their roles vary from one story to another.

Some Valkyries are closely associated with Odin.

Others possess wills of their own and appear in heroic legends.

Still others become the lovers of warriors.

Sometimes a single character combines several of these roles.

So we shouldn't imagine the Valkyries as an entirely uniform organisation with identical responsibilities.

The mythological figures are much more complicated than that.

But one thing remains particularly important.

Valkyries are connected with the boundary between life and death.

The *Prose Edda* tells us that Odin sends them to battlefields.

They select those who will fall and influence the outcomes of conflicts.

Some accompany fallen warriors to Valhalla.

There dwell the **einherjar** — warriors who will eventually take part in the final battle.

And this creates an interesting connection with Freyja.

Remember that she receives half of those who fall in battle?

The traditions surrounding Freyja and the Valkyries demonstrate that Norse beliefs about the fate of dead warriors were more complicated than the popular idea that every brave Viking automatically went to Odin.

The ancient sources don't provide one detailed, universally consistent system of allocation.

So we won't invent rules they don't contain.

But the idea itself is extraordinary.

A warrior may believe he's deciding his own fate on the battlefield.

He fights.

Shows courage.

Risks his life.

Yet somewhere above the fighting are beings associated with the choice of when his life will end.

Which raises an uncomfortable question.

Who really wins?

The strongest warrior?

The most skilled swordsman?

Or the one whose time to die hasn't yet arrived?

For the ancient Norse, warfare could involve more than a clash between human beings.

It could reflect decisions and forces invisible to ordinary eyes.

But Valkyries don't always remain anonymous servants of another's will.

They have names.

Stories.

Personalities.

And some make choices that lead to terrible consequences.

One example is Brynhild, the famous heroine of the legends surrounding Sigurd.

Her story involves love, oaths, deception, and tragedy.

But today, we'll meet another Valkyrie.

Her name is **Sigrún**.

And her story reveals that choosing the destinies of others doesn't necessarily mean being able to control your own.

First, though, there's something else worth remembering about Valkyries.

Popular culture often presents them exclusively as beautiful and noble celestial warriors.

But the ancient imagery can be much darker.

Some works associate them closely with bloodshed, death, and the terror of battle.

In *Darraðarljóð*, preserved in *Njáls Saga*, women resembling Valkyries weave the fabric of battle using human entrails, severed heads, and weapons.

A fairly disturbing description.

Especially if you were hoping for a peaceful afternoon of needlework.

But that's part of what makes Norse mythology distinctive.

Beauty doesn't exclude cruelty.

Power doesn't guarantee mercy.

And beings associated with the heavens may also be connected with the most terrible things happening on earth.

Yet sometimes, a Valkyrie becomes the heroine of a story not about death.

But about love.

Although, as we've already discovered, those two subjects rarely remain entirely separate in Norse mythology.

## Chapter 7. Sigrún. Love Is Stronger Than Death. Briefly.

---

![Sigrún. Love Is Stronger Than Death. Briefly.](/visuals/norse/m06/07-sigrun-helgi-1448.webp)

---

Her name was **Sigrún**.

She was the daughter of a powerful ruler named Högni.

And she was a Valkyrie.

The ancient poems describe Sigrún travelling through the air and over the sea alongside other Valkyries.

But one day, she met someone who changed her fate.

His name was **Helgi**.

He was a great warrior and the son of Sigmund.

His title was Hundingsbane — the Slayer of Hunding.

Songs celebrated his achievements.

But Sigrún loved him for more than his reputation in battle.

In *The Second Lay of Helgi Hundingsbane*, their encounter becomes the story of two people who recognise something important in each other.

Unfortunately, their happiness was threatened almost immediately.

Sigrún's father had arranged for her to marry another man.

Her intended husband was **Höðbroddr**, the son of a ruler named Granmar.

Sigrún didn't want this marriage.

She chose Helgi.

And went to find him.

Not to obediently announce her father's decision.

But to ask for help.

Helgi agreed to fight for her.

War followed.

On one side stood Helgi and his allies.

On the other were Höðbroddr's people and Sigrún's own relatives.

The battle was terrible.

Höðbroddr was killed.

But so were Sigrún's father, Högni, and her brother Bragi.

Helgi's victory brought Sigrún both the fulfilment of her wish and the destruction of part of her family.

She could finally be with the man she loved.

But the price was the lives of people close to her.

And she couldn't undo what had happened.

In the poem, Helgi tries to comfort her.

He speaks of fate and the impossibility of changing the outcome of a battle already fought.

But Sigrún grieves.

And her grief doesn't disappear simply because the war ended in victory.

Later, Helgi and Sigrún married.

They had children.

It seemed that the worst was finally behind them.

But Sigrún had another brother.

**Dagr.**

He had survived the battle in which their father was killed.

And although he had previously sworn loyalty, he couldn't forget Högni's death.

Dagr turned to Odin.

According to the prose narrative preserved alongside the poem, Odin gave him a spear.

Dagr then found Helgi.

And killed him.

Afterwards, Dagr went to Sigrún.

To tell her what had happened.

Imagine that moment.

A woman who once defied her father for love discovers that her own brother has murdered her husband.

Blood vengeance had completed what the first war failed to finish.

Sigrún was devastated.

She cursed Dagr.

He tried to offer her wealth and land as compensation.

But no amount of property could bring Helgi back.

The warrior was buried in a mound.

And it seemed the story had ended.

But one day, Sigrún's maidservant witnessed something extraordinary.

Helgi appeared near his burial mound.

A dead warrior.

Accompanied by his men.

He had returned from the world of the dead.

The maidservant was frightened.

But Helgi explained that he hadn't come to harm anyone.

She hurried to Sigrún.

And told her that her husband was at the burial mound.

Sigrún went there.

And saw Helgi for herself.

But he was no longer the man she remembered.

He belonged to the world of the dead.

His body was cold.

And his wounds still bore witness to what had happened.

Yet they were together again.

Sigrún embraced him.

She prepared a place for them to rest inside the mound.

And spent the night with him.

It's one of the most moving scenes in the entire *Poetic Edda*.

But there's a terrible detail.

Helgi tells Sigrún that her tears cause him pain.

Her grief hasn't remained in the world of the living.

It has reached even the dead.

And now that they're reunited, it becomes clear that the meeting cannot last.

Before dawn, Helgi must leave.

He must return to Valhalla before the cock announces the coming of a new day.

Sigrún is left alone.

But she hopes he'll return the following night.

She goes back to the mound.

And waits.

Evening falls.

Then night.

Then dawn.

But Helgi doesn't appear.

He never returns.

At the end of the poem, we're told that Sigrún died soon afterwards, overwhelmed by grief.

Then comes another unusual detail.

An ancient tradition held that Helgi and Sigrún were born again under different names.

But the surviving text itself notes that such beliefs in rebirth later came to be regarded as old-fashioned folklore.

We shouldn't turn this into a guaranteed happy ending.

Because within the story itself, happiness was painfully brief.

Sigrún fought for the right to choose her own life.

She refused an arranged marriage.

Chose the man she loved.

But couldn't prevent the war.

Couldn't save her father.

Couldn't protect her husband from revenge.

And couldn't keep him even when he returned from the dead for a single night.

**She could fight for her destiny. But she could never completely own it.**

Perhaps that's why this story feels so different from familiar tales of heroes.

There's no simple answer.

No victory followed by a lifetime of happiness.

There's love.

Choice.

And the price of defying the will of others.

In the end, only memory remains.

And one of the saddest images in Norse mythology.

A woman beside a burial mound, waiting for someone who has already returned from death once.

But will never return again.

## Chapter 8. Fate. A Contract You Cannot Cancel.

---

![Fate. A Contract You Cannot Cancel.](/visuals/norse/m06/08-fate-yggdrasil-1448.webp)

---

At the beginning of our journey, we met Frigg.

A goddess who can keep secrets and even outwit Odin.

Then we encountered Freyja.

A mistress of love, wealth, magic, and a share of the warriors who fall in battle.

We explored seiðr.

And women whose knowledge and prophetic abilities may have given them special positions in society.

We met a völva capable of telling the story of the universe from its creation to its destruction.

We encountered the Norns, who shape human destinies.

And the Valkyries, associated with the selection of the fallen.

Finally, we heard Sigrún's story.

A woman who refused to submit to a future chosen for her, but still couldn't save the person she loved.

And now we can return to the question we began with.

**Can fate be changed?**

At first, Norse mythology seems to offer a rather bleak answer.

Odin knows about Ragnarök.

But can't simply cancel it.

Frigg tries to protect Baldr.

But her son dies.

Sigrún chooses love.

But loses her husband.

The Norns shape destinies.

And Valkyries appear where warriors' lives come to an end.

It seems as though everything has already been decided.

But there's one important detail.

The characters in these myths keep acting.

Odin searches for knowledge.

Thor protects the world.

Frigg tries to save her son.

Freyja commands magic and exercises her own powers.

Sigrún chooses the person she loves.

Even if a prophesied future cannot be changed, a character's choices don't become meaningless.

They reveal who that character is.

What they fear.

What they believe is right.

What they're willing to fight for.

And what they're prepared to lose.

In Norse mythology, fate isn't simply the answer to how a story ends.

It's also a test of character.

Especially when victory is impossible.

Think of Sigrún.

She couldn't save Helgi from death.

But that didn't make her love any less real.

Think of Frigg.

She couldn't keep Baldr safe.

But her attempt to protect him reveals the strength of a mother's love.

Think of Odin.

He knows the world will be destroyed.

Yet he continues preparing for the final battle.

And here lies one of the most powerful ideas in the mythology of the North.

**You cannot change everything. But that doesn't mean nothing matters.**

Of course, we don't know whether every ancient Scandinavian understood fate in exactly the same way.

Different poems, sagas, and myths were composed at different times.

They don't form one perfectly consistent philosophical system.

So we shouldn't claim that every person living during the Viking Age believed in precisely the same version of predestination.

But the surviving stories repeatedly return to the tension between choice and inevitability.

And that brings us to one more uncomfortable question.

What happens when someone discovers a terrible future and tries to prevent it?

Suppose the gods hear a prophecy about creatures destined to destroy their world.

The creatures haven't done anything terrible yet.

The final battle hasn't begun.

Entire ages still lie ahead.

What will the gods choose?

Will they negotiate?

Will they wait?

Or will they decide to eliminate the threat before it can become real?

We already know that the Norse gods don't always demonstrate remarkable foresight.

Especially when they believe they can outsmart fate.

Now think back to Loki.

And his three children with the giantess Angrboda.

Fenrir.

Jörmungandr.

And Hel.

We've mentioned their names before, but we've barely explored what happened to them.

One child will become an enormous wolf.

Another, a serpent encircling the world.

And the third will gain authority over a realm of the dead.

The gods will hear prophecies connected with their future.

And then make decisions that change the fate of every world.

Perhaps those very decisions will bring the catastrophe they fear a little closer.

But that's our next story.

And discover why some parents really can say that their children might one day destroy the entire world.

Although, to be fair, this family's problems began long before the children were born.

Valkyries are associated with battles and the selection of the slain. Some accompany warriors to Valhalla. By human standards, the recruitment process is rather severe.

Helgi returned for just one night. Sigrún waited for him again, but in vain. Not every return becomes a new beginning.
