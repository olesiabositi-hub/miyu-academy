# Module 7

# Monsters. Family Problems on a Cosmic Scale.

*The story of Loki's three children, who were prophesied to threaten the gods. One was thrown into the ocean. Another was sent to rule the dead. The third was deceived and bound to a rock forever. The gods hoped to prevent disaster. But perhaps their attempts to escape the prophecy helped bring it closer.*

**Time:** about 32–41 minutes

---

## Chapter 1. Angrboda. Congratulations. Three Children and a Prophecy.

---

![Angrboda. Congratulations. Three Children and a Prophecy.](/visuals/norse/m07/01-angrboda-and-her-children-1448.webp)

---

In our previous module, we explored fate.

The Norns, who shape human destinies.

The Valkyries, who choose the fallen.

Odin, who knows about his own death but continues preparing for the final battle.

And the uncomfortable fact that knowing the future doesn't necessarily make it any easier to change.

Now it's time to meet three beings whose lives were shaped by prophecy almost from the beginning.

But first, let's remember Loki.

We left him bound to rocks beneath a venomous serpent.

His wife, Sigyn, catches the dripping poison in a bowl.

The earth shakes whenever a drop reaches his face.

A rather grim ending.

Unfortunately, Loki's problems didn't end with his personal fate.

He had children.

And not just Sleipnir, Odin's eight-legged horse, whose family history has already forced us to reconsider several conventional ideas about Norse parenthood.

In Jötunheim lived a giantess named **Angrboda**.

Her name is often interpreted as "Bringer of Grief" or "She Who Foretells Sorrow".

Not the most optimistic name.

Especially if you're planning to start a family.

According to the *Prose Edda*, Angrboda and Loki had three children.

**Fenrir.**

**Jörmungandr.**

**Hel.**

One was a wolf.

Another was a serpent.

The third was a being whose appearance seemed to belong to both life and death.

An unusual family, even by Asgard's standards.

The children grew up in Jötunheim, among the giants.

The ancient text tells us almost nothing about their early lives.

We don't know how Angrboda cared for them.

We don't know whether they played together.

And we don't know what they thought of their famous father.

So we won't invent details the sources don't provide.

But one day, the gods learned that the children existed.

And, more importantly, they learned what had been prophesied about them.

The prophecies warned that these three beings would bring terrible misfortune.

Great calamities would come upon the gods because of them.

The Æsir had never been entirely comfortable with the descendants of giants.

But now their fears had a specific explanation.

The future looked dangerous.

And Angrboda's children were at the centre of it.

Odin decided to act.

He sent the gods to Jötunheim.

Their orders were to take the three children and bring them before him.

What did Angrboda feel when they were taken?

The myth doesn't tell us.

We hear no protest from her.

We don't know whether she resisted.

We can't even say precisely how the children were seized.

The *Prose Edda* describes it much more simply.

The gods learned about the prophecy.

The gods were afraid.

The gods took the children.

Remarkably efficient decision-making.

No family consultation.

No discussion of alternatives.

And apparently no attempt to establish whether the children had actually done anything wrong.

Once the three siblings were brought before Odin, he decided their fates.

The serpent would go to the sea.

Hel would be sent to a distant realm of the dead.

And the wolf would remain among the Æsir.

Each was assigned a place.

And each would live a life shaped by someone else's fear of the future.

There's an important distinction to make here.

**The prophecy of future danger belongs to the ancient myth itself.**

Whether the gods treated the children fairly is a question we're asking as readers.

Perhaps Odin's concerns were justified.

Perhaps the creatures really did pose a terrible threat.

But an uncomfortable question remains.

If someone told you that a child would one day become your enemy, what would you do?

Would you try to raise that child differently?

Or would you treat them as an enemy from the very beginning?

Now imagine that the child eventually grew strong enough to demand an explanation.

The gods had time to consider this.

Instead, they distributed the threats across different parts of the universe.

We'll begin with the one they kept closest.

A wolf named Fenrir.

## Chapter 2. Fenrir. Good Boy. While He's Small.

---

![Fenrir. Good Boy. While He's Small.](/visuals/norse/m07/02-fenrir-and-tyr-1448.webp)

---

Of Loki's three children, Fenrir caused the gods particular concern.

He was also known as **the Fenris Wolf**.

He wasn't an ordinary forest animal.

Nor merely an unusually large predator.

According to prophecy, this wolf would play a terrifying role in the world's future.

But for now, he was living in Asgard.

The gods had decided to raise him there.

Imagine how strange his presence must have seemed among the magnificent halls.

Gods with weapons and precious jewellery.

Great buildings.

Councils.

Feasts.

And somewhere nearby, a wolf that everyone feared a little more with every passing day.

Fenrir grew enormous.

Very quickly.

Day by day, his body increased in size.

His strength multiplied.

His teeth became more frightening.

The gods watched him and remembered the prophecies.

One day, this creature might bring about their destruction.

And fewer and fewer of them were willing to approach him.

The *Prose Edda* explicitly tells us that only one god dared bring Fenrir food.

**Týr.**

We've met him before in Asgard.

A god associated with war, courage, and obligations.

Modern films don't tend to give him quite as much attention as Odin or Thor.

But in this story, Týr becomes one of the most important figures.

Imagine the enormous wolf.

He lies near the gods' halls.

The others prefer to keep their distance.

But Týr approaches.

And feeds him.

Again.

And again.

The source doesn't tell us that they formed a tender friendship.

Nor does it say that Týr raised Fenrir like his own son.

Those might make a touching story, but they'd be invented details.

Yet the fact remains significant.

**Týr was the only god who regularly approached a creature the others feared.**

Why?

Perhaps because he was exceptionally brave.

Perhaps because he felt some responsibility towards the wolf the Æsir had chosen to keep among them.

The myth doesn't explain his motives.

But later, this detail will become terribly important.

Because Týr will be the one to demonstrate just how much trust can cost.

For now, Fenrir keeps growing.

And the larger he becomes, the more afraid the gods grow.

There's an interesting pattern here.

Fenrir hasn't yet done what the prophecy says he will do.

But the Æsir are already looking at him as a future enemy.

They're judging him not just by what he's doing.

But by what he's expected to become.

That doesn't mean their fear is entirely irrational.

An extraordinarily powerful wolf really is growing among them.

And the prophecy promises disaster.

For beings responsible for Asgard's safety, this is a serious problem.

A very serious problem.

But the gods have no reliable way of discovering what might happen if they simply leave Fenrir alone.

And no guarantee that interfering will make things better.

As we've already discovered, however, a lack of guarantees rarely prevents Asgard from making decisions on a cosmic scale.

The gods begin discussing how to limit the wolf's power.

They don't dare kill him.

The *Prose Edda* explains that they don't want to defile their sacred place with his blood.

So they choose another solution.

Bind him.

Immobilise him.

Make sure he can no longer threaten them.

At first glance, it sounds reasonable.

Especially from the gods' perspective.

But there's one problem.

Fenrir is a living being.

And he's unlikely to volunteer to spend the rest of his existence chained to a rock.

Which means the gods will have to deceive him.

First, however, let's discover what happened to his brother and sister.

Both were sent far away from Asgard.

And Odin dealt with each of them very differently.

---

## Chapter 3. Hel. She Got Her Own Kingdom. Nobody to Celebrate With.

---

![Hel. She Got Her Own Kingdom. Nobody to Celebrate With.](/visuals/norse/m07/03-hel-queen-of-the-dead-1448.webp)

---

Fenrir's sister was named **Hel**.

Unlike her brothers, what made her extraordinary wasn't her enormous size.

It was her appearance.

According to the *Prose Edda*, one half of her body was blue-black.

The other was the colour of living flesh.

She was described as grim and formidable.

Her appearance seemed to bring together two different conditions.

Life.

And death.

But let's clear up a common misconception.

Hel isn't simply the Norse equivalent of the Christian Devil.

And her realm shouldn't automatically be treated as an exact version of Christian Hell.

Ancient Norse ideas about death developed differently and can't be reduced to one universal system of eternal punishment for sinners.

Hel is primarily associated with a realm of the dead.

And she rules over those who enter it.

When Odin received Angrboda's three children, he sent Hel to **Niflheim**.

A cold, dark region of the cosmos.

But he didn't merely exile her.

He also granted her authority over the dwellings of the dead who came under her rule.

The *Prose Edda* specifically mentions those who died of sickness or old age.

That doesn't mean every ancient Norse belief about the afterlife followed one rigid rule.

We already know about Valhalla and Fólkvangr.

And in the next module, we'll explore why the world of the dead was far more complicated than popular retellings suggest.

For now, let's return to Hel.

Her realm has high walls.

Enormous gates.

Her hall bears a name often translated as **Sleet-Cold**.

Even the names of her possessions and servants are grim.

Her dish is associated with Hunger.

Her knife with Famine.

Her bed with Sickness.

Her threshold with Stumbling.

Even her servants have names associated with slowness.

One suspects hospitality here was rather different from the festive traditions of Asgard.

But Hel isn't merely a frightening decoration in a gloomy landscape.

She possesses genuine authority.

And we've already seen it in Baldr's story.

Remember when Hermod travelled to the realm of the dead to bring Odin's son back?

He found Hel.

And asked her to release Baldr.

She didn't agree simply because the request had come from the Allfather's messenger.

Instead, she set a condition.

Everything in existence must weep for Baldr.

And when one giantess refused, Baldr remained among the dead.

This is one of the most important details about Hel.

She isn't someone who simply obeys the wishes of the other gods.

She has rules of her own.

And even Odin cannot just order her to release his son.

Consider that kind of power.

In Asgard, Odin carries the title of Allfather.

Thor can crush giants.

Freyja chooses a share of the fallen warriors.

But in Hel's realm, the decisions belong to Hel.

Loki's daughter.

The sister of a wolf and a World Serpent.

A being the gods had decided was dangerous before her future had even unfolded.

And whom they sent away.

It's a rather unusual outcome.

Odin tried to protect the world by removing Hel from Asgard.

But in doing so, he also made her the ruler of her own domain.

This isn't necessarily a story about a rejected girl who inevitably becomes a villain.

The ancient sources give us no reason to simplify her character that way.

But there's an intriguing contrast.

Hel has no place among the Æsir.

Yet she possesses a form of authority they themselves must respect.

And if you ever find yourself standing outside her gates, remember one thing.

**Even the mightiest gods can't always overrule her decisions.**

Now let's meet Angrboda's other son.

He was sent even farther away.

Not to a realm of the dead.

But into the depths of the ocean.

Perhaps the gods thought that would keep the problem safely out of everyone's way.

As it turned out, the ocean wasn't quite big enough.

---

## Chapter 4. Jörmungandr. Thrown into the Sea. Now He's the Size of the World.

---

![Jörmungandr. Thrown into the Sea. Now He's the Size of the World.](/visuals/norse/m07/04-jormungandr-world-serpent-1448.webp)

---

When Odin saw Loki's second child, he was looking at a serpent.

**Jörmungandr.**

He's also known as **the World Serpent** or **the Midgard Serpent**.

Odin didn't keep him in Asgard.

And he didn't give him a kingdom to rule.

He threw Jörmungandr into the deep ocean surrounding the lands of humankind.

A sensible decision, you might think.

If a giant serpent is destined to threaten the gods one day, it's probably best to keep him far away.

Preferably somewhere nobody is likely to encounter him by accident.

But in Norse mythology, getting rid of a threat rarely makes it disappear.

Jörmungandr began to grow.

Larger.

And larger.

Until eventually, his body was so enormous that it encircled Midgard.

The *Prose Edda* describes the serpent lying in the ocean around the lands, biting his own tail.

Imagine a creature so vast that no one could see him in his entirety.

Somewhere far beneath the waves, a body stretches across unimaginable distances.

His scales are hidden by the water.

His coils lie in the depths.

His head and tail meet somewhere no ordinary person could ever hope to reach.

The ocean seems endless.

Yet it has become the home of a single serpent.

It was Jörmungandr whom Thor encountered on the most famous fishing trip in Norse mythology.

Remember?

The giant Hymir.

A tiny boat.

An enormous ox's head for bait.

And the thunder god deciding that an ordinary fish wasn't nearly ambitious enough.

In Module Four, we witnessed that adventure from Thor's perspective.

Now let's look at it differently.

Jörmungandr lies in the ocean depths.

Suddenly, bait appears.

He swallows it.

And finds himself connected to one of his greatest future enemies.

Thor pulls the line.

The serpent fights back.

The sea churns.

And two beings destined to kill each other find themselves face to face in an extraordinary confrontation.

In the *Prose Edda*, Hymir cuts the line in terror.

Jörmungandr disappears into the depths.

But the conflict isn't over.

The prophecies connect their eventual deaths at Ragnarök.

Thor will kill the World Serpent.

But he'll die from its venom.

For now, however, both remain alive.

One among the gods.

The other in the sea.

And between them lies a battle the future has already foretold.

There's something important to note here.

Modern stories sometimes portray Jörmungandr as a creature literally holding the world together with his body.

But the ancient account doesn't give us good grounds for treating him as a conscious guardian of cosmic balance.

We know that he encircles the lands.

And that he'll take part in the final catastrophe.

Anything beyond that needs to be treated carefully.

Yet the image is extraordinary without adding anything.

A serpent exiled from the world of the gods grows large enough to encircle the world of humans.

So let's return to our original question.

Did Odin's decision work?

Jörmungandr really was kept far away from Asgard.

But he didn't vanish.

He didn't become less powerful.

And he didn't stop being part of the future the gods feared.

He simply grew.

And one day, not even the enormous ocean will be able to contain him.

*Sometimes a problem thrown overboard comes back.*

*Especially if it's had enough time to grow around the entire world.*

But things would become far more personal with Loki's third child.

Because Fenrir was still living among the gods.

And they were about to break a promise to his face.

## Chapter 5. Fenrir. Two Chains. Zero Results.

---

![Fenrir. Two Chains. Zero Results.](/visuals/norse/m07/05-fenrir-breaks-the-chains-1448.webp)

---

Let's return to Asgard.

Fenrir is still growing.

The wolf the gods once brought from Jötunheim now possesses extraordinary strength.

And the stronger he becomes, the less interested the gods are in discovering whether the prophecies might come true.

They make a decision.

**Fenrir must be bound.**

But how do you persuade an enormous creature to voluntarily let someone put chains around him?

An attack could end in disaster.

Trying to overpower him might be even worse.

So the Æsir come up with a clever idea.

They propose a competition.

Just a small test of strength.

Nothing dangerous.

Naturally.

The gods make an enormous iron chain.

It's called **Læðingr**.

The chain is heavy, strong, and impressive.

They show it to Fenrir.

And invite him to try breaking it.

If he succeeds, everyone will see just how powerful he really is.

Fenrir examines the chain.

Considers it.

And decides the challenge is well within his abilities.

He allows the gods to bind him.

The Æsir step back.

Fenrir tenses.

His muscles begin to move.

The iron stretches.

And the chain breaks.

Without much difficulty.

Imagine the gods' expressions.

They've devoted considerable effort to creating something that should protect Asgard.

And Fenrir destroys it almost immediately.

But the Æsir aren't ready to give up.

They construct a second chain.

**Drómi.**

Much stronger than the first.

According to the *Prose Edda*, it's twice as strong.

Once again, the gods approach Fenrir.

And once again, they offer a test.

They tell the wolf that if he can break such an extraordinary chain, his reputation will become truly magnificent.

Fenrir thinks about it.

This chain looks considerably more serious.

He understands that the challenge will be harder.

But he remembers how much stronger he's become.

And decides to take the risk.

After all, what's the point of being the strongest wolf in the world if you never get to demonstrate it?

He allows himself to be bound again.

The Æsir prepare.

Fenrir tightens his body.

Pulls.

Strikes the chain against the ground.

And keeps struggling until the iron bursts into pieces.

Drómi is destroyed.

The gods have failed a second time.

And now the situation is even worse.

They've discovered that ordinary restraints are useless against Fenrir.

But they've also demonstrated to the wolf that he can overcome increasingly powerful chains.

**The gods grow more afraid.**

**Fenrir grows more confident in his strength.**

An excellent outcome.

If the goal was to make the entire situation even more tense.

In Old Norse tradition, the names of these chains even became part of expressions describing the overcoming of extraordinary difficulties.

But the gods are no longer impressed.

They realise that if they keep forging heavier chains, Fenrir may eventually grow strong enough to break those too.

They need a different solution.

Not stronger iron.

Not another enormous chain.

Something Fenrir has never seen before.

Odin sends **Skírnir**, Freyr's messenger, to a group of skilled dwarves.

He orders a special restraint.

And so begins the story of one of the strangest objects in Norse mythology.

**Gleipnir.**

If Mjölnir came into existence because Loki interfered with some dwarven craftsmanship, Gleipnir is the result of a far more disturbing commission.

The gods need to create something capable of restraining a creature that can shatter iron.

The dwarves agree.

But to accomplish this, they'll need materials that can't be found in any ordinary forge.

## Chapter 6. Gleipnir. The Thinnest Chain. The Greatest Betrayal.

---

![Gleipnir. The Thinnest Chain. The Greatest Betrayal.](/visuals/norse/m07/06-tyr-gleipnir-and-betrayal-1448.webp)

---

The dwarves make Gleipnir from six extraordinary ingredients.

And once again, Norse mythology demonstrates its remarkable imagination.

The ingredients are:

- The sound of a cat's footsteps.
- A woman's beard.
- The roots of a mountain.
- A bear's sinews.
- A fish's breath.
- A bird's spittle.

Yes.

Those ingredients.

The *Prose Edda* even explains why some of them are impossible to find in the ordinary world.

Cats walk silently.

Mountains have no roots in the usual sense.

And the remaining ingredients are equally unusual.

The dwarves produce a restraint that looks completely harmless.

Not an enormous chain.

Not heavy iron links.

Not an intimidating piece of machinery.

**A thin, soft ribbon.**

Almost like silk.

But impossible to break.

The gods receive Gleipnir.

And travel to Lake **Ámsvartnir**, to an island called **Lyngvi**.

They bring Fenrir with them.

Imagine a cold lake.

Dark water.

A low sky.

An island surrounded by a motionless surface.

And an enormous creature standing among the gods.

One of them is holding a thin ribbon.

The Æsir propose another challenge.

Look, they say.

You've broken enormous chains.

Now let's see what you can do with this little thing.

But Fenrir is no longer so trusting.

He examines Gleipnir.

And notices something strange.

If the ribbon really is weak, breaking it will be easy.

But there'll be no glory in such a feat.

If it's been made through trickery, then this is a trap.

Fenrir suspects deception.

And he's absolutely right.

He refuses to be bound without a guarantee.

The gods insist that the test is safe.

If he can't break the ribbon, they promise to release him.

A wonderful promise.

Especially considering they have absolutely no intention of keeping it.

But Fenrir demands proof.

**One of the gods must place a hand inside his mouth.**

And keep it there throughout the test.

If the Æsir genuinely intend to release him, there's nothing to fear.

But if they're lying, someone will pay for the betrayal.

The gods look at one another.

Suddenly, the plan seems considerably less appealing.

Nobody wants to risk a hand.

Nobody is particularly enthusiastic about demonstrating the honesty of their own promises.

And then **Týr** steps forward.

The god who used to bring Fenrir food.

The only one who regularly dared approach him.

Týr stretches out his right hand.

And places it inside the wolf's mouth.

This is where we should stop for a moment.

We don't know exactly what Týr was feeling.

We don't know whether Fenrir trusted him personally, or simply considered the pledge sufficient.

The ancient text doesn't reveal their thoughts.

But it tells us what happened.

The gods place Gleipnir around Fenrir.

The wolf tries to escape.

He tenses.

Pulls.

But the ribbon doesn't break.

He tries again.

And discovers that the harder he struggles, the tighter the restraint becomes.

Fenrir understands.

He's been deceived.

The gods have no intention of releasing him.

And the promise that persuaded him to submit was a lie.

Then Fenrir closes his jaws.

**And bites off Týr's hand.**

There's an almost unbearable detail in the *Prose Edda*.

When the gods see that the wolf is securely bound, they laugh.

**All except Týr.**

He's just lost his hand.

And Fenrir has lost his freedom.

It's one of the most powerful moments in Norse mythology.

Not because good has defeated evil.

Or evil has defeated good.

But because victory and betrayal have become inseparable.

The gods have achieved their goal.

They've protected themselves against an immediate threat.

But they've done it by breaking their own promise.

Týr has shown extraordinary courage.

And paid the price for the gods' decision.

Fenrir is now bound.

And his distrust has been completely justified.

The Æsir secure the restraint to enormous stones.

When the wolf tries to bite them, they force a sword into his mouth.

Its hilt rests against his lower jaw, its point against the upper.

Fenrir can no longer close his jaws.

Saliva flows from his mouth.

In the ancient myth, this becomes a river called **Ván**.

Fenrir remains bound.

And he'll stay there until Ragnarök.

The gods believe they've eliminated the danger.

But the prophecy hasn't disappeared.

It still says that one day the wolf will break free.

And kill Odin.

Now comes the central question.

**Was Fenrir a monster before the gods bound him?**

The ancient myth describes his monstrous strength and the threat foretold by prophecy.

But it doesn't provide a detailed catalogue of evil acts committed by Fenrir before his imprisonment.

That doesn't prove he was harmless.

Yet it invites us to think about what happened between the gods' fear and their decision.

They were trying to prevent a foretold disaster.

And created a creature who now had very real reasons to hate them.

That's one way we can read the story today.

Not as a claim that the ancient author necessarily intended this exact psychological drama.

But as a question the myth leaves open.

**What's more frightening: a monster everyone fears, or a betrayal committed out of fear of that monster?**

This time, we won't make a joke.

Because on that cold island remains a wolf nobody intends to release.

And Týr returns to Asgard without his right hand.

Neither of them has truly won.

## Chapter 7. Other Monsters. The Universe Was Dangerous Before Loki Had Children.

---

![Other Monsters. The Universe Was Dangerous Before Loki Had Children.](/visuals/norse/m07/07-yggdrasil-and-its-creatures-1448.webp)

---

By now, we know what happened to Angrboda's three children.

Hel received authority over a realm of the dead.

Jörmungandr was cast into the ocean.

Fenrir was bound until the final battle.

It seems the gods have managed to distribute the major threats across different parts of existence.

Perhaps now they can finally feel safe.

But the Norse universe has one rather inconvenient feature.

**There are considerably more than three dangerous creatures living in it.**

Let's begin with the sky.

In Module One, we met the Sun and Moon.

They travel through the heavens.

And wolves pursue them.

One is named **Sköll**.

The other is **Hati**.

In the *Prose Edda*, Sköll chases the Sun.

Hati pursues the Moon.

And one day, the chase will end in catastrophe.

The heavenly lights will be swallowed.

The familiar order of the world will break down.

The surviving texts contain complicated and not entirely consistent accounts of the celestial wolves' ancestry.

So we won't confidently add Sköll and Hati to the list of Loki's children.

The ancient sources don't support such a simple family tree.

But the wolves themselves are an essential part of the mythological North.

Every day and every night, the heavenly lights continue their journeys.

And behind them runs a threat that never disappears.

Now let's leave the sky and descend to Yggdrasil's roots.

There we encounter a creature named **Níðhöggr**.

He's commonly imagined as a dragon or serpent-like monster.

He gnaws at one of the World Tree's roots.

And this isn't simply an unpleasant problem for an unusually large plant.

Yggdrasil connects the worlds.

Its existence is tied to the structure of the cosmos itself.

So whatever happens to its roots matters to everyone.

Níðhöggr isn't the only creature troubling the tree.

*Grímnismál* describes animals feeding on its branches.

Numerous serpents beneath its roots.

And suffering that exceeds what human beings can imagine.

It's a remarkable image.

The World Tree rises above existence.

Its branches stretch between the realms.

Yet something is constantly chewing on it.

From above.

From below.

From practically every direction.

Maintaining cosmic infrastructure in the ancient North was apparently a rather thankless occupation.

And now let's meet a creature who looks completely harmless.

**Ratatoskr.**

A squirrel.

An apparently ordinary squirrel that runs along the trunk of Yggdrasil.

But Ratatoskr has a special job.

High in the branches sits an enormous eagle.

Far below, near the roots, lives Níðhöggr.

And between them runs Ratatoskr.

Delivering hostile messages.

Insults.

Unpleasant remarks.

Whatever else the two have to say about one another.

Up and down.

Down and up.

So while a dragon gnaws at the World Tree and an eagle watches from above, a squirrel is busy maintaining their mutual hostility.

A remarkably modern communication model.

Although these days you don't usually need to run up and down a tree to accomplish it.

But Ratatoskr isn't merely a comic character.

Its presence reveals how much tension exists within Yggdrasil's world, even when no great battle is taking place.

There are opposing forces.

Hostility.

Rivalry.

Constant threats.

And none of them ever disappears entirely.

There are also dragons, giants, dead beings, and monsters appearing throughout other northern legends.

Some guard treasures.

Others inhabit distant lands.

Still others become the enemies of heroes.

And they don't all belong to one neat category.

"Monster" is a convenient word for a modern storyteller.

But the ancient mythological world doesn't always separate humans, gods, and terrifying creatures as neatly as we'd like.

Giants can be wise.

Gods can be cruel.

A wolf can be betrayed.

And a squirrel can specialise in conflict escalation.

Although with the squirrel, at least, things are fairly straightforward.

If you need to know why the eagle and the dragon have fallen out again, Ratatoskr is probably the first place to start your investigation.

But beneath these strange and sometimes amusing images lies an important idea.

**The world wasn't safe even before Ragnarök.**

Yggdrasil suffers.

Wolves pursue the Sun and Moon.

The World Serpent lies beneath the ocean.

A dragon dwells near the roots of the tree.

And somewhere on an island, an enormous wolf awaits his release.

The catastrophe hasn't happened yet.

But its possibility is already everywhere.

And that's what makes the gods' actions towards Fenrir even more troubling.

They tried to eliminate one threat.

In a world where threats could never be completely eliminated.

## Chapter 8. Monsters. Or the Consequences of Someone Else's Fear?

---

![Monsters. Or the Consequences of Someone Else's Fear?](/visuals/norse/m07/08-fenrir-hel-jormungandr-prophecy-1448.webp)

---

Let's return to the beginning of our story.

In Jötunheim lived Angrboda.

She had three children with Loki.

Fenrir.

Jörmungandr.

Hel.

And one day, the gods learned that these beings were destined to cause terrible events.

Odin decided to act early.

The children were taken.

Separated.

And sent to different parts of the universe.

Hel was sent to a realm of the dead.

Jörmungandr was thrown into the ocean.

Fenrir remained in Asgard, until the gods eventually bound him.

At first glance, the gods behaved like responsible protectors of the world.

They discovered a danger.

And tried to prevent it.

But now that we know the details, things become much more complicated.

Hel didn't simply disappear.

She became a powerful ruler whose decisions even the gods had to respect.

Jörmungandr didn't perish in the ocean.

He grew until his body encircled the world.

And Fenrir didn't merely grow stronger.

He was deceived by the very beings among whom he'd lived.

Lost his freedom.

And was left waiting for the final battle.

One moment in Fenrir's story is particularly important.

**He demanded a guarantee of honesty.**

Which means he understood that deception was possible.

But he agreed to the test anyway.

Týr placed his hand in the wolf's mouth.

The gods fastened Gleipnir.

And broke their promise.

Of course, we cannot claim that Fenrir was originally a gentle and completely harmless creature.

The myth doesn't tell us that.

And the prophecies about his future role are real within the story.

But we can ask a question.

If a creature prophesied to become the gods' enemy is imprisoned through deception before that future arrives, might the gods themselves be helping to bring the conflict closer?

In other words:

**Can an attempt to prevent a prophecy become part of the reason it comes true?**

This is our interpretation of the story, not a direct answer provided by the ancient text.

But questions like these are precisely what make the myths so compelling today.

There are no entirely flawless heroes.

Odin wants to protect the world.

But makes cruel decisions.

Týr demonstrates courage.

But takes part in a deception.

Fenrir poses a terrifying threat.

But also becomes a victim of betrayal.

And Hel, who might seem like a frightening ruler of the dead, exercises authority granted to her by Odin himself.

The boundary between protector and destroyer isn't always where we expect it to be.

Sometimes good intentions lead to cruelty.

Sometimes fear produces injustice.

Sometimes the being called a monster has perfectly understandable reasons to distrust everyone around them.

But the reverse matters too.

We can't assume that every dangerous creature is an innocent victim simply because it's been treated cruelly.

Norse mythology doesn't offer such easy answers.

It's far more interesting than that.

Now imagine, for a moment, what will happen at the end of the world.

Fenrir will break free.

Jörmungandr will rise from the ocean.

The living and the dead will become part of a great catastrophe.

And the gods will confront the threats they tried to isolate long before the final battle.

But we'll save the complete story of Ragnarök for Module Nine.

For now, one mystery remains.

We've already spoken several times about Valhalla.

Fólkvangr.

Hel.

Fallen warriors.

People who died of old age or sickness.

And the possibility of returning from the realm of the dead, as Baldr's family once tried to arrange.

But what did the ancient Norse actually believe happened after death?

Did every brave warrior go to Odin?

Was Hel a place of punishment?

What happened to people who didn't die in battle?

And was there ever one universal vision of the afterlife?

To find out, we'll have to travel somewhere people don't normally return from.

Ahead lie Valhalla, Fólkvangr, the realm of Hel, and the other ways the ancient Norse imagined existence after death.

And perhaps the strangest part of this journey is that there really were different possibilities.

You just didn't necessarily get to choose yours in advance.
