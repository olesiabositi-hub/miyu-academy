# Module 9

# Ragnarök. The End of the World Is Scheduled. No Cancellations.

*Nine modules. Nine worlds. And one catastrophe the gods knew was coming but could never prevent.*

*This is a story about the Great Winter, the release of monsters, the final battle of the gods, and the destruction of the world. About why all his wisdom couldn't save Odin, why Thor took only nine steps after his greatest victory, and why the end of everything turned out to be less final than expected. And about the green earth that rose from the sea, and the life that began again.*

**Time:** about 30–38 minutes

---

## Chapter 1. Fimbulwinter. Three Winters. Not a Single Spring.

---

![Fimbulwinter. Three Winters. Not a Single Spring.](/visuals/norse/m09/01-fimbulwinter-1448.webp)

---

Once, there was nothing.

No earth.

No sky.

No gods.

No humans.

Only an enormous emptiness called Ginnungagap.

That's where our journey began in Module One.

Then came the first beings.

The world was created from Ymir's body.

Yggdrasil grew.

The gods built Asgard.

Humans settled in Midgard.

Odin began his search for wisdom.

Thor received Mjölnir.

And Loki started causing problems that gradually became less and less amusing.

We met goddesses, Valkyries, giants, monsters, and the dead.

We discovered why some things cannot be prevented, even when you know they're going to happen.

Now we return to the völva's prophecy.

The very story Odin once demanded to hear.

She knew how everything began.

And how everything would end.

But the end doesn't begin with fire.

**It begins with cold.**

**Fimbulwinter** arrives.

The Great Winter.

Not an ordinary northern winter, when snow covers the ground and people wait for spring.

Not even a particularly severe year when rivers freeze and feeding animals becomes difficult.

The *Prose Edda* describes three consecutive winters.

Without a summer between them.

Three years of cold.

Three years without the relief the earth has always known.

Imagine the first year.

The snow refuses to melt.

The ground remains frozen.

Food runs out faster than expected.

Animals die.

Families struggle to preserve their supplies.

Then the second winter comes.

Nothing improves.

No harvest.

No real warmth.

No opportunity to replace what's been lost.

Then the third winter begins.

And everyone understands.

This isn't simply bad weather anymore.

**The world has stopped working as it should.**

Modern tourism companies might attempt to advertise such conditions as an "authentic Nordic experience".

But even the most determined Scandinavians would probably prefer to see the sun occasionally.

Yet the cold is only the beginning.

In *Völuspá*, human relationships collapse.

Brothers turn their weapons against one another.

Family bonds lose their meaning.

Oaths are broken.

Violence spreads.

And here's what makes the prophecy especially frightening.

The destruction isn't happening only among the gods.

It's happening between humans.

Inside homes.

Inside families.

Between people who once trusted one another.

We've encountered such stories before.

Baldr's death shattered relationships among the gods.

Dagr's revenge killed Helgi.

Fear of prophecy drove the Æsir to deceive Fenrir.

Now that same collapse of trust spreads everywhere.

As though the world is repeating the mistakes of its creators.

Then the familiar light disappears.

In Norse mythology, wolves pursue the Sun and Moon.

We met them in Module Seven.

Sköll.

Hati.

In accounts of the final days, the heavenly lights are overtaken and swallowed.

The surviving sources don't always agree about which wolves are associated with the destruction of the Sun and Moon.

*Vafþrúðnismál*, for instance, mentions Fenrir in this context.

So we shouldn't force different traditions into one perfectly consistent account.

But the central image is clear.

The Sun disappears.

The Moon disappears.

Stars fall from the sky.

And the darkness grows deeper.

Imagine standing in a frozen field.

Snow beneath your feet.

Not a green plant in sight.

People have long since stopped expecting spring.

You look up.

And the sky no longer resembles the one you've known your entire life.

Then comes the most terrible realisation.

This isn't Ragnarök yet.

**This is only the preparation.**

You still have to survive long enough to witness the final battle.

And deep beneath the earth, in the ocean, and on distant islands, the creatures the gods tried to contain begin to feel the old order breaking apart.

For now, the chains remain.

But the world in which they were forged is already trembling.

Soon, we'll discover just how effective Odin's solutions really were.

## Chapter 2. The Chains Are Broken. Everyone You Feared Is Free.

---

![The Chains Are Broken. Everyone You Feared Is Free.](/visuals/norse/m09/02-monsters-unbound-1448.webp)

---

Remember the island of Lyngvi?

The cold waters of Lake Ámsvartnir.

The thin ribbon called Gleipnir.

And the enormous wolf who agreed to be bound only after Týr placed his hand inside the wolf's mouth.

Fenrir discovered the deception.

Bit off Týr's hand.

And remained bound to a rock.

The gods believed they were safe.

But now the earth shakes.

Mountains crack.

Ancient boundaries collapse.

And **Fenrir breaks free**.

Imagine him rising.

After countless years of imprisonment.

After the betrayal.

After time beyond any human measure.

Gleipnir can no longer hold him.

Before the wolf lies the world from which he was excluded.

And ahead lies his prophecy.

The *Prose Edda* describes Fenrir becoming unimaginably enormous during the final battle.

His lower jaw reaches towards the earth.

His upper jaw towards the sky.

Fire pours from his eyes and nostrils.

He no longer resembles the wolf Týr once fed.

He's a force capable of destroying even the chief of the gods.

But Fenrir isn't the only one to break free.

Remember **Jörmungandr**.

Odin once threw him into the ocean surrounding Midgard.

The serpent grew.

Encircled the human world.

And remained in the depths.

Now he begins to move.

The waters rise.

Enormous waves crash against the shore.

The sea breaks beyond its familiar boundaries.

Jörmungandr's vast body rises above the ocean.

Poison fills the air.

The entire sea seems to move with him.

If Fenrir represents the threat the gods bound, Jörmungandr represents the threat they threw as far away as possible.

Both containment strategies fail at the same time.

A deeply unfortunate outcome for Asgard's long-term risk management programme.

There's another prisoner, though.

And we know him very well.

**Loki.**

We left him bound to rocks after Baldr's death.

Sigyn collected the serpent's poison in a bowl.

Whenever she left to empty it, drops fell onto Loki's face.

He writhed in pain.

And the earth shook.

But now his chains are broken.

Loki is free again.

This time, however, he doesn't approach the gods with another questionable proposal.

He marches against them.

In accounts of Ragnarök, enormous forces gather for the last battle.

Giants arrive.

Hostile armies advance.

And among the most disturbing images is a ship called **Naglfar**.

According to the *Prose Edda*, it's made from the nails of dead people.

Yes.

An entire ship.

Made of fingernails and toenails.

Norse mythology has finally discovered a construction material that makes ordinary timber look like an extraordinary achievement in good taste.

The medieval account even preserves the idea that nails should be trimmed from the dead so as not to contribute to Naglfar's construction.

But we shouldn't automatically treat that explanation as proof of a universal Viking Age funeral custom.

First and foremost, it's a mythological image.

The ship is associated with the forces of destruction.

And its arrival signals the approaching end.

Different descriptions of the armies and their leaders contain variations, but in the *Prose Edda*, the giant Hrym steers Naglfar.

From another direction comes **Surtr**.

A fiery giant from Muspelheim.

His name is associated with darkness, yet his weapon shines brighter than the Sun.

He carries a flaming sword.

And the forces of fire march behind him.

The world is freezing.

The air is poisoned.

The earth trembles beneath monstrous footsteps.

And now a fire capable of ending everything approaches.

Our course began with two opposing regions.

Icy Niflheim.

And fiery Muspelheim.

Nine modules later, cold and fire are once again at the heart of the story.

But this time, they aren't helping create a world.

They're helping destroy it.

When Surtr's forces advance, **Bifröst**, the rainbow bridge connecting the worlds, breaks beneath their weight.

The bridge the gods once used to travel between Asgard and the lands of humankind.

Another symbol of the old order disappears.

Imagine that bridge collapsing into chaos.

Fire behind it.

The final battlefield ahead.

Every threat the gods tried to isolate has returned.

And for the first time, the Æsir have nowhere left to send the problem.

**There are no other worlds in which to hide the end of the world.**

## Chapter 3. Gjallarhorn. Final Announcement for All Worlds.

---

![Gjallarhorn. Final Announcement for All Worlds.](/visuals/norse/m09/03-heimdall-gjallarhorn-1448.webp)

---

At the edge of Asgard stands Heimdall.

We met him in Module Two.

The guardian of Bifröst.

A god with extraordinary hearing and vision.

He can hear grass growing.

And detect events at enormous distances.

For ages, his duty has been to guard the gods' world.

Watch for threats.

And warn the others in time.

Now the moment he's spent his existence preparing for has finally arrived.

Heimdall lifts **Gjallarhorn**.

His mighty horn.

And sounds it.

The call travels through the worlds.

This isn't an ordinary alarm.

Not a warning that another giant is approaching Asgard.

Not a request for Thor to solve a problem with his hammer.

**It's the announcement that the final battle has begun.**

Imagine the sound reaching the furthest corners of existence.

The frozen lands.

The dark forests.

The halls of the gods.

The places where humans still survive.

All living beings learn that the time has come.

The gods assemble for a council.

In the *Prose Edda*, Odin rides to Mimir's well in search of counsel.

And there's something almost unbearably ironic about that.

In Module Three, we told the story of Odin surrendering an eye for wisdom.

Of hanging from Yggdrasil.

Of searching for the secrets of existence.

He spent his life trying to understand more.

And now, as the world approaches its end, he seeks wisdom once again.

But he already knows the most important thing.

He knows what's coming.

He knows the name of his killer.

He knows that even the mightiest god cannot simply cancel a prophecy.

Still, he goes to Mimir.

Not necessarily because he expects to find a way to escape death.

Perhaps because knowledge is the one weapon Odin has never stopped seeking.

The sources don't preserve a detailed speech delivered at this final council.

So we won't invent one and pretend it's ancient literature.

But we can imagine the weight of the moment.

The gods have gathered countless times before.

Discussed wars.

Argued.

Feasted.

Tried to repair the consequences of Loki's latest disaster.

Now they'll confront the forces they always feared.

Meanwhile, the doors of **Valhalla** open.

The einherjar assemble for battle.

Remember their daily training?

Endless fighting.

Wounds.

Resurrection.

Feasting.

And fighting again.

In Module Eight, we joked that even death hadn't released these warriors from military obligations.

Now we understand what all that training was for.

This day.

One battle after which there will be no familiar evening feast.

And probably no announcement about tomorrow's training schedule.

The warriors march out.

The gods take up their weapons.

Thor raises Mjölnir.

Odin wears his golden helmet and carries the spear Gungnir.

Freyr prepares to fight.

Týr will join the battle.

And Heimdall, who has just sounded the alarm, will soon confront the being destined to become his final opponent.

Loki.

The battlefield is called **Vígríðr**.

The *Prose Edda* describes an enormous plain stretching a hundred leagues in every direction.

The ancient description conveys scale rather than providing precise geography.

Here, the fate of the old world will be decided.

On one side stand the gods, trying to preserve the existing order.

On the other are the forces capable of destroying it.

But we already know that the boundary between good and evil is rarely simple in Norse mythology.

Fenrir was once bound through deception.

Loki helped the gods before helping bring about Baldr's death.

Odin wanted to protect the world but made cruel decisions of his own.

Now they must all meet.

And nobody can escape the consequences.

A moment arrives when words no longer change anything.

The armies draw closer.

Weapons rise.

And the first great tragedy awaits the god who spent his life trying to foresee it.

Odin.

## Chapter 4. Odin and Fenrir. Wisdom Against Teeth.

---

![Odin and Fenrir. Wisdom Against Teeth.](/visuals/norse/m09/04-odin-fenrir-1448.webp)

---

Odin steps onto the battlefield of Vígríðr.

The Allfather.

The ruler of Asgard.

The god who sacrificed his eye for wisdom.

Who hung nine nights from the World Tree.

Who searched for knowledge among gods, giants, and the dead.

Before him stands Fenrir.

An enormous wolf.

The son of Loki and Angrboda.

The creature the gods once took from Jötunheim.

Whom Týr used to feed.

Whom they challenged to break increasingly powerful chains.

Whom they deceived with Gleipnir.

Now their entire history converges in one moment.

Perhaps this is the most terrible encounter in all Norse mythology.

Because both are bound to what comes next.

Fenrir is destined to kill Odin.

Odin knows the identity of his killer.

There will be no more negotiations.

No more clever schemes.

No chance to ask the dwarves to forge one final chain.

No way to postpone the inevitable.

Odin advances towards the wolf.

Thor, who might otherwise have helped his father, is already fighting Jörmungandr.

The Allfather faces the monster.

Gungnir in his hand.

Before a mouth so enormous that, according to the *Prose Edda*, its upper jaw reaches the sky.

Imagine the final moment.

A strange silence within the roar of battle.

Odin, who has looked into the future so many times.

And the being who has become part of that future.

**Fenrir devours Odin.**

The Allfather dies.

Neither wisdom.

Nor his spear.

Nor his trained army.

Nor countless years of trying to understand fate can change the outcome.

In Norse mythology, this isn't a triumph of justice.

Nor the victory of evil.

It's the fulfilment of a prophecy.

But the story doesn't end with Odin's death.

His son **Víðarr** stands on the battlefield.

One of the gods destined to survive Ragnarök.

Víðarr approaches Fenrir.

According to the *Prose Edda*, he wears an extraordinarily strong shoe.

Its sole is made from leather scraps that people have collected for this purpose.

The ancient account even explains why those scraps should be saved.

For the first time in quite a while, it appears that collecting seemingly useless materials might genuinely prove useful at the end of the world.

But this isn't a moment for jokes.

Víðarr places his foot against the wolf's lower jaw.

Grasps the upper jaw with his hand.

And tears his mouth apart.

**Fenrir dies.**

Another ancient account describes Víðarr's revenge somewhat differently, involving a weapon. There's no reason to pretend every detail from different sources belongs to one completely consistent version.

But the essential outcome remains.

Odin is dead.

Fenrir is dead.

Víðarr has avenged his father.

And the world continues to fall apart.

Now remember Týr.

His hand.

And the moment when the Æsir laughed after binding the wolf.

Back then, they believed they'd won.

Now, it turns out, they merely delayed the inevitable.

Perhaps no other scene reveals the gods' central tragedy quite so clearly.

They possessed immense power.

But they couldn't control everything.

They knew danger was approaching.

Yet became part of the events they feared.

Even the wisest of them couldn't escape death.

Odin's search for knowledge wasn't meaningless.

His life was filled with discoveries, decisions, mistakes, and attempts to understand existence.

But knowledge didn't make him immortal.

**Sometimes wisdom allows you to see the end. But not to prevent it.**

Meanwhile, not far from Odin's death, Thor is fighting.

He has a prophecy of his own.

And just nine steps left to take.

## Chapter 5. Thor. Nine Steps After Victory.

---

![Thor. Nine Steps After Victory.](/visuals/norse/m09/05-thor-jormungandr-1448.webp)

---

If you asked the Norse gods to choose one of their number for a final battle, many would probably name Thor.

Powerful.

Direct.

Experienced.

Armed with a hammer capable of destroying giants.

We got to know him much better in Module Four.

Discovered how he lost Mjölnir and was forced to disguise himself as a bride.

How he travelled to Útgarðr.

How he attempted to drink an ocean.

How he nearly pulled the World Serpent out of the sea.

And how frequently he considered a hammer blow sufficient to settle any disagreement.

But now comes a battle in which all his strength will be both enough and not enough.

Before Thor rises **Jörmungandr**.

The World Serpent.

Loki's son.

The creature Odin once threw into the ocean.

Unlike their earlier meeting, when Thor sat in a boat with the giant Hymir, nothing separates the two opponents now.

No fishing line.

No bait.

No frightened companion ready to cut the rope.

Before Thor is a monster whose body once encircled all of Midgard.

Imagine an enormous head rising above the battlefield.

Scales reflecting the flames.

Poisonous breath.

And the eyes of a being who spent ages beneath the sea while the gods continued their ordinary existence.

The ocean no longer contains him.

Thor raises Mjölnir.

The hammer the dwarves forged long ago.

The weapon with which he defended Asgard.

With which he battled giants.

Which returned to his hand after being thrown.

Everything we know about Thor seems to lead to this moment.

Thunder confronts the ocean.

The hammer meets venom.

The god who defended the human world faces a creature capable of destroying it.

The battle begins.

Thor strikes.

Jörmungandr fights back.

Catastrophe rages around them.

And finally Thor wins.

**The World Serpent dies.**

One of Midgard's greatest threats has been destroyed.

Thor has accomplished what he's risked his life trying to do for so long.

Surely this should be the moment of victory.

He might raise Mjölnir.

Look at the fallen monster.

And return to the other gods.

But none of that happens.

Because Jörmungandr has poisoned him.

Thor takes one step.

Then another.

And another.

Just **nine steps**.

After the ninth, he falls.

And dies.

That's how the *Prose Edda* describes his end.

Nine steps after victory.

Not nine heroic achievements.

Not nine more battles.

But nine final movements by someone who has already defeated his enemy and still cannot save himself.

For our course, the number nine has a special resonance.

Nine worlds.

Nine modules.

Nine steps.

But that's **a literary connection we're making today**, not a proven ancient explanation of why Thor walks precisely nine steps.

In the story itself, the meaning is simpler and more terrible.

Thor dies almost immediately after his greatest victory.

In Module Four, we laughed at his character.

His temper.

His faith in the hammer.

The absurd situations he found himself in.

But now we should remember something else.

Thor protected people.

He fought the forces threatening Midgard.

Again and again, he faced danger so that the rest of the world could continue to exist.

And he does it one final time.

Even though the outcome may cost him his life.

There's a particular cruelty in the fate of a hero who genuinely wins.

But never gets to see what becomes of the world after his victory.

Thor lies on the battlefield of Vígríðr.

Jörmungandr is dead nearby.

And for a moment, all that remains of them is a story.

Later, however, we'll learn something important.

Mjölnir doesn't disappear with its owner.

Thor has sons.

And they'll survive the catastrophe.

For now, the battle continues.

Odin is dead.

Thor is dead.

And the last confrontations are still to come.

Along with some deeply unpleasant news for anyone hoping that Loki might finally manage to avoid another conflict.

## Chapter 6. Loki. The Final Family Reunion Went Badly.

---

![Loki. The Final Family Reunion Went Badly.](/visuals/norse/m09/06-loki-surtr-final-battle-1448.webp)

---

Loki returns.

But this time, he isn't arriving for a feast.

He isn't proposing a suspicious deal.

He isn't trying to repair another disaster of his own creation.

He's marching into the final battle against the gods.

In Module Five, we explored Loki's story.

How he helped the gods and deceived them.

Recovered their stolen treasures.

Kidnapped Idunn and then rescued her.

Brought about Baldr's death.

And was eventually bound beneath a venomous serpent.

Loki has been one of the most complicated characters of our journey.

He wasn't always the gods' enemy.

Nor was he always their friend.

But now his role in the coming catastrophe is clear.

He belongs to the forces opposing Asgard.

His opponent is **Heimdall**.

The guardian of Bifröst.

The one who noticed the approaching final battle.

Raised Gjallarhorn.

And warned everyone that the end had come.

The two have clashed before.

In later traditions surrounding Brísingamen, Loki and Heimdall are connected with a struggle over Freyja's necklace.

But here, their conflict takes its final form.

They fight.

And **kill one another**.

Neither returns to Asgard victorious.

Loki, who escaped so many impossible situations, finds no way out this time.

Heimdall, who guarded the boundaries of the gods' world, dies in the last battle for that world.

But they aren't the only ones.

**Týr** faces the monstrous hound **Garmr**.

The *Prose Edda* describes Garmr breaking free from his bonds near a cave called Gnipahellir.

He fights Týr.

And both perish.

Remember Týr from Module Seven.

The god who placed his hand inside Fenrir's mouth.

Who lost that hand when the gods deceived the wolf.

And who still goes into the final battle.

His story doesn't end with another confrontation with Fenrir.

But with a battle against a different monster.

Next comes **Freyr**.

Freyja's brother.

A god associated with fertility, prosperity, and peaceful life.

Earlier in the course, we met him as one of the most important Vanir living among the Æsir.

But Freyr has a problem.

Long ago, he gave his remarkable sword to his messenger, Skírnir.

In the story of the giantess Gerðr, that loss becomes an important detail.

Now, as Freyr faces Surtr, he no longer has the weapon capable of fighting on its own.

And he dies.

There's something almost tragically ironic about it.

Odin sacrificed an eye for knowledge.

Týr lost a hand in the binding of a wolf.

And Freyr once gave up a magnificent sword in pursuit of love.

At the end of the world, it turns out that some personal decisions have extremely long-term consequences.

But Freyr is dead now.

As are many others.

Surtr remains among the forces of destruction.

His flaming sword shines.

And the final stage of Ragnarök begins.

Fire sweeps across the world.

The lands burn.

Stars fall.

Familiar boundaries collapse.

The sky and earth change beyond recognition.

In *Völuspá*, the earth sinks into the sea.

Fire reaches towards heaven.

The old world disappears.

Not just Asgard.

Not only the halls of the gods.

Not one country.

**The entire familiar order of existence.**

Imagine the place where magnificent buildings once stood.

Where the gods held their feasts.

Where Thor argued with Loki.

Where Odin surveyed the worlds from his high seat.

Now there's nothing.

No familiar roads.

No bridges.

No living sky.

No Asgard as we knew it.

All the gods' efforts, wars, bargains, and schemes to keep the world from destruction have ended here.

The catastrophe foretold by the völva has happened.

The prophecy has been fulfilled.

And if our course ended at this moment, Norse mythology would seem extraordinarily cruel.

A story about how even gods are powerless against death.

But there's one detail.

**The völva hasn't finished speaking.**

## Chapter 7. The World Died. The Earth Disagreed.

---

![The World Died. The Earth Disagreed.](/visuals/norse/m09/07-world-reborn-1448.webp)

---

After the fire comes silence.

No fighting.

No monstrous footsteps.

No sound of Gjallarhorn.

Nobody arguing around a feasting table.

No laughter from Loki.

No thunder of Mjölnir.

No approaching storms of Thor.

The world that existed for so long has vanished.

But something passes for which we have no suitable measure.

And land begins to rise from the sea.

First, the peaks appear.

Then hills.

Coastlines.

Valleys.

Fields.

Green grass grows across the new earth.

It's beautiful.

And life returns.

In *Völuspá*, the seeress describes the earth rising once again from the ocean.

It's one of the most extraordinary turns in all Norse mythology.

Because after such a detailed account of the world's destruction, we suddenly see something entirely different.

Not fire.

But green fields.

Not destruction.

But the possibility of beginning again.

Not death.

But life.

Yet who will inhabit this new world?

So many gods have died.

Odin.

Thor.

Freyr.

Týr.

Heimdall.

Loki.

And still, some have survived Ragnarök.

Among them is **Víðarr**.

The god who avenged Odin's death by killing Fenrir.

And **Váli**, another of the Allfather's sons.

Thor's sons have survived too.

**Magni and Móði.**

They inherit Mjölnir.

The hammer with which their father protected the world.

Consider the power of that image.

Thor is gone.

But his weapon remains.

And passes to another generation.

Not as a prize for winning the final battle.

But as part of a world that still has a future.

And there's an even more surprising return.

From Hel's realm come **Baldr and Höðr**.

Remember Baldr?

The beautiful god whom almost everything in existence had promised not to harm.

Everything except mistletoe.

Remember Höðr, whose blindness allowed him to become the instrument of his brother's death?

That tragedy changed the fate of Asgard.

Now both brothers appear among those who will live after Ragnarök.

Death once separated them.

And now they're together again.

The *Prose Edda* describes the surviving gods gathering on the plain of Iðavöllr, where Asgard once stood.

They talk.

Remember what happened.

Discuss the ancient stories.

The World Serpent.

Fenrir.

And among the grass they discover the golden gaming pieces that once belonged to the earlier gods.

Imagine the scene.

The world has only just been reborn.

Young green plants surround them.

The sky is clear.

And small golden objects lie in the grass.

They belonged to the old Asgard.

Its games.

Its conversations.

And those who are no longer there.

Suddenly, we understand that new life doesn't necessarily require forgetting everything that came before.

But what happened to humanity?

Ragnarök didn't affect only the gods.

It destroyed the familiar human world.

*Vafþrúðnismál* tells us that two people survived.

**Líf and Lífþrasir.**

They hid in a place called **Hoddmímir's Holt**.

And lived through the catastrophe.

According to the poem, morning dew provided their food.

And from them, new generations of humans would descend.

We can't reconstruct every detail of this mythological refuge.

But its meaning is beautiful.

While the world was being destroyed, life was preserved somewhere.

Not an enormous army.

Not a mighty god.

Just two people.

And that was enough for the human story to continue.

A new Sun will appear too.

*Vafþrúðnismál* tells us that the old Sun will have a daughter.

And she will follow her mother's path across the sky.

Imagine the first dawn of the new world.

After Fimbulwinter.

After the disappearance of the heavenly lights.

After the fire.

After the deaths of the gods.

Light appears on the horizon.

And a new day begins.

But even here, the ancient poetry doesn't offer an entirely uncomplicated happy ending.

In the final stanzas of *Völuspá*, **Níðhöggr** appears again.

The dark dragon.

He flies above the world carrying dead bodies.

The meaning of this final image is debated.

It's not entirely clear whether we should see it as a last reminder of evil, the beginning of another vision, or part of the complicated history of the surviving text.

So we won't claim that the new earth is guaranteed to be free of every danger forever.

But that uncertainty makes the ending more powerful.

**The new world isn't beautiful because darkness can never return.**

**It's beautiful because life exists again, despite everything that happened.**

## Chapter 8. After the End of the World. Stories Remain.

Ginnungagap.

A place where the familiar world didn't yet exist.

Gods.

Giants.

Humans.

His search for wisdom.

The eye he sacrificed to Mimir.

The runes he gained by sacrificing himself.

We met Thor.

His hammer.

His goats, which weren't the most reliable means of transport but at least knew how to come back to life.

We met Loki.

Whose jokes began with relatively harmless trouble and ended with the near-total destruction of the world.

We encountered Frigg and Freyja.

Now let's travel into our own time.

Imagine watching a Marvel film about Thor.

Once, you might have seen only an enormous hammer, Asgard, and gods with extraordinary powers.

Now you recognise the names and images the creators drew upon.

But you also know that **Marvel's Thor is a modern fictional character, not an exact reproduction of the ancient Norse god**.

And Ragnarök in the film *Thor: Ragnarok* is a creative interpretation, not a faithful retelling of the Eddas.

Or imagine playing *God of War Ragnarök*.

Thor.

Freyja.

Fenrir.

The World Serpent.

The ancient world becomes another layer of meaning.

You understand where the name comes from.

Why that character carries a particular weapon.

What stories existed long before modern screens.

And how contemporary creators have transformed them.

The same is true of music.

In Module Eight, we listened to Wardruna's *Helvegen*.

A song associated with the final journey.

Now our journey will end with a different piece of music.

**Lyfjaberg.**

The name can be translated as "Healing Mountain".

The image of a mountain associated with healing appears in the medieval Icelandic poem *Fjölsvinnsmál*.

But Wardruna's song is a modern composition.

It isn't a recovered ceremonial chant from the Viking Age.

Einar Selvik draws on ancient cultural imagery to explore overcoming difficulties and the journey towards healing.

And that's why the song belongs at the end of this course.

After Ragnarök, the world doesn't return to exactly what it was.

Odin doesn't come back.

Thor doesn't receive another chance.

And nothing can undo everything that's happened.

But new life begins.

And beginning again isn't the same as forgetting.

Remember the surviving gods on the plain of Iðavöllr.

And remember the old world.

Those small objects contain something much greater than gold.

**Memory.**

Of what existed before.

Of those who are gone.

Of the decisions that led to disaster.

And of the need to continue living.

This is where our journey through Norse mythology should end.

Not with an explosion.

Not with another battle.

But with a road.

A mountain path.

Along which someone climbs towards the light.

# Course final exam

## 1. What was Ginnungagap?

A. The main hall of Asgard where the gods gathered.  
B. The primordial void between the realms of cold and fire.  
C. An underground workshop where dwarves forged weapons.  
D. Scandinavia's first coffee shop. Unfortunately, coffee hadn't been invented.  

**Answer:** B.

Ginnungagap was the primordial emptiness associated with the beginning of existence. The world emerged not from a convenient, ready-made space, but through the interaction of opposing forces.

---

## 2. According to Norse mythology, how was the world created from Ymir's body?

A. Ymir voluntarily transformed himself into a planet.  
B. Thor struck him with Mjölnir, creating the Nine Worlds.  
C. Loki persuaded Ymir to donate his body for the greater good.  
D. The gods used the primordial being's body to create the earth, sea, mountains, and sky.  

**Answer:** D.

The gods created the world from Ymir's body. Not the most peaceful creation story. But we learned fairly early that the Norse gods weren't always committed to peaceful solutions.*

### Module 02 — Welcome to Asgard

---

## 3. How did the war between the Æsir and Vanir end?

A. With reconciliation and an exchange of hostages as part of the peace settlement.  
B. With the complete destruction of the Vanir.  
C. With Loki becoming ruler of both groups.  
D. With the creation of a joint committee for the prevention of family disputes.  

**Answer:** A.

The Æsir and Vanir made peace. Important consequences included the exchange of hostages and members of both groups living among one another. A rare example of relatively constructive conflict resolution among the gods.

---

## 4. What is one of Heimdall's principal duties?

A. Guarding Idunn's golden apples.  
B. Controlling the weather in Midgard.  
C. Guarding Bifröst and warning the gods about threats.  
D. Checking everyone's tickets before entering Asgard, particularly if they're giants.  

**Answer:** C.

Heimdall guards Bifröst. During Ragnarök, he's the one who sounds Gjallarhorn to announce the final battle.*

### Module 03 — Odin. Knowledge Comes at a Price

---

## 5. What did Odin sacrifice in exchange for a drink from Mimir's well of wisdom?

A. He defeated Mimir in a duel.  
B. He traded his eight-legged horse for the water.  
C. He gave up one eye.  
D. He purchased a lifetime wisdom subscription. No refunds.  

**Answer:** C.

Odin sacrificed an eye for wisdom. Knowledge was never free for him — and we're not talking about tuition fees.

## 6. What happened during Odin's nine nights on Yggdrasil?

A. He underwent a sacrificial ordeal through which he gained knowledge of the runes.  
B. He prepared for Freyja's wedding.

**Answer:** A.

Odin endured a terrible ordeal on the World Tree to gain knowledge of the runes. Another example of how far he was willing to go in pursuit of wisdom.*

### Module 04 — Thor. When a Hammer Is the Answer to Everything

---

## 7. Which weapon is Thor's most important symbol?

A. Gungnir.  
B. Gleipnir.  
C. Brísingamen.  
D. Mjölnir.  

**Answer:** D.

Mjölnir is Thor's hammer, forged by dwarves. Gungnir belongs to Odin, Gleipnir bound Fenrir, and Brísingamen is Freyja's necklace. Asgard wasn't always well organised, but mixing up personal belongings is still best avoided.

## 8. What bait did Thor use when he tried to catch Jörmungandr on his famous fishing trip?

A. Freyja's gold.

C. Fenrir, whom he invited to help.  
D. A complimentary tour of Asgard, followed by immediate elimination.  

**Answer:** B.

Thor went fishing with the giant Hymir and used an ox's head for bait. An ordinary fishing trip rapidly became a rehearsal for the final battle.*

### Module 05 — Loki. Some Problems Are Better Left Unsolved

---

## 9. Why was Idunn's kidnapping dangerous for the gods?

A. She guarded Bifröst.

**Answer:** C.

Idunn guarded the apples associated with the gods' youth. When she was kidnapped, the Æsir began to age. Loki had to repair the consequences of his own actions.

**Answer:** A.

Frigg secured promises from almost everything in existence, but mistletoe was excluded. Loki exploited that weakness, and Baldr died.*

## 11. How did Frigg win her wager with Odin over King Geirröd?

A. She persuaded the Norns to rewrite the king's fate.

Frigg created the circumstances that made her accusation come true. Odin spent eight nights between two fires. Losing a marital disagreement could be extremely expensive in Asgard.

A. Three Valkyries guarding Valhalla.

Urðr, Verðandi, and Skuld are Norns. Other Norns also appear in the sources, and the popular division into Past, Present, and Future shouldn't be treated as an unquestionable ancient system.*

## 13. Why was Gleipnir more effective than enormous iron chains?

**Answer:** A.

Gleipnir looked like a thin ribbon, yet possessed extraordinary strength. It was created from six impossible things, including the sound of a cat's footsteps.

## 14. Why did Fenrir bite off Týr's hand?

A. Týr tried to take his food away.

**Answer:** C.

The gods deceived Fenrir, and Týr paid for it with his hand. It's one of Norse mythology's most tragic stories about trust and betrayal.*

## 15. What do the sources say about Freyja and fallen warriors?

**Answer:** D.

Valhalla wasn't the only mythological destination associated with fallen warriors. Freyja received a portion of the slain and was associated with Fólkvangr.

## 16. How should we understand the popular image of a Viking funeral on a burning ship?

A. It was compulsory for every Scandinavian.

D. Every deceased person received a ship as long as their relatives submitted the application before sunset.  

**Answer:** B.

Archaeology reveals a wide range of funeral practices. The familiar cinematic scene wasn't a universal historical tradition.*

Fenrir fulfils the prophecy of Odin's death. Víðarr avenges his father. It's one of the defining scenes of the final battle.

Ragnarök doesn't end with destruction alone. The earth returns, surviving and returning gods gather, and human life continues. The end of one world becomes the beginning of another.
