# Module 2

# Welcome to Asgard. The Leadership Is Questionable.

*How the gods divided power, started the first war, negotiated a deeply questionable peace treaty, and discovered that running the universe is considerably harder than creating it.*

**Time:** about 23–29 minutes

---

## Chapter 1. Asgard. Excellent Address. Difficult Neighbours.

---

![Asgard. Excellent Address. Difficult Neighbours.](/visuals/norse/m02/01-norse-stronghold-beneath-the-winter-sun-1448.webp)

---

Last time, we left the universe in a rather unusual state.

The earth had been made from a dead giant. The sky was being held up by four dwarves. The Sun and Moon were fleeing from wolves. The first humans had been given life, but absolutely no explanation of what was going on.

And somewhere above it all stood Yggdrasil, the enormous tree connecting the different realms of existence.

You might think it was time for someone to take control of the situation.

Enter the gods.

Not merely powerful beings who could command thunder, control the seas, or influence fate. An entire community with its own laws, assemblies, alliances, obligations, and conflicts.

They were particularly good at the last one.

The principal home of many of these gods was **Asgard** — the realm of the Æsir.

Imagine magnificent halls, towering walls, feasting chambers, bridges, and breathtaking views of the heavens.

Just don't try to find Asgard on Google Maps.

The Old Norse sources don't all describe it in the same way. This isn't a city with an officially approved master plan, but a mythological home of the gods, revealed differently from one story to another.

Here stood their halls and gathering places. Here, decisions were made, agreements negotiated, feasts held, and arguments started that could affect the fate of other worlds.

One god held a particularly important position.

**Odin.**

He was known as the Allfather. He was associated with power, wisdom, war, poetry, magic, and the warriors who died in battle.

In the *Prose Edda*, Odin is presented as the eldest and greatest of the Æsir.

But don't imagine him as an absolute monarch who only has to raise an eyebrow for everyone else to obey.

The gods hold councils. They argue. They offer one another advice, occasionally with catastrophic results. And not every decision Odin makes is automatically the final word.

Even in Asgard, authority doesn't eliminate the need for negotiation.

Beside Odin stands **Frigg**, his wife, a goddess who knows the fates of human beings but is in no hurry to reveal what she knows.

Other gods live here too, each with their own powers, personalities, and ideas about how things should be done.

They have halls, weapons, wealth, responsibilities, and a remarkable number of relatives.

Some of those relatives live outside Asgard.

Some pose a threat to it.

And some begin as enemies, become allies, and eventually join the same divine community.

Because Asgard isn't just a place.

It's an attempt to establish order among beings who aren't particularly fond of being told what to do.

And to understand why the leadership is so questionable, we need to establish one important fact.

**The gods of the North were never one big happy family.**

At least, not in the beginning.

---

## Chapter 2. Two Divine Families. One Universe.

---

![Two Divine Families. One Universe.](/visuals/norse/m02/02-norse-gods-fjordside-parley-1448.webp)

---

When we think of Norse gods, the names Odin, Thor, and Loki usually come to mind first.

But Old Norse tradition describes two major families of gods.

**The Æsir.**

And **the Vanir.**

The Æsir include figures such as Odin, Thor, Frigg, and Týr. Their myths often involve power, war, the defence of order, knowledge, and relationships between the gods and other beings.

The best-known Vanir are Njord and his children, Freyr and Freyja. They are especially associated with the sea, prosperity, fertility, wealth, and certain forms of magic.

But let's avoid making the distinction too neat.

It would be wrong to say that the Æsir were simply in charge of war while the Vanir handled the harvest.

Odin practises magic. Freyja is connected not only with love and fertility, but also with warriors who die in battle. Divine powers don't fit into tidy departmental categories.

Ancient mythology rarely makes life easy for people trying to classify it.

Especially a thousand years later.

The Vanir had their own realm, **Vanaheim**.

Njord was associated with the sea, winds, and wealth. For people living along the northern coasts, those were matters of enormous importance.

The sea could provide food, transport, and opportunities for trade.

It could also destroy a ship in minutes.

Freyr was associated with fertility, peace, prosperity, and well-being. Entire communities depended on successful harvests and the changing seasons.

His sister Freyja was far more complicated than the familiar image of a goddess of love.

She was associated with desire, wealth, magic, and death. In some myths, she had the right to choose a share of the warriors killed in battle.

We'll get to know her much better later.

For now, remember that the Vanir possessed their own power and status. They had no intention of accepting the Æsir's superiority simply because the Æsir thought it would be a good idea.

Two groups of gods existed in the same universe.

Both were powerful.

Both expected respect.

And, as so often happens, both were convinced that their particular vision of how things ought to work deserved special consideration.

Eventually, their disagreements escalated into open conflict.

But what caused it?

This is where Norse mythology becomes especially fascinating.

In *Völuspá*, an ancient poem from the *Poetic Edda*, we hear about a mysterious event commonly associated with the beginning of the first war among the gods.

A woman named **Gullveig** appeared in Asgard.

And the welcome she received was rather less hospitable than she might have hoped.

---

## Chapter 3. Gullveig. The Woman Who Wouldn't Stay Burned.

---

![Gullveig. The Woman Who Wouldn't Stay Burned.](/visuals/norse/m02/03-gullveig-s-rebirth-in-the-longhall-1448.webp)

---

We know surprisingly little about Gullveig.

But what we do know is enough to make her story one of the most unsettling in Norse mythology.

Her name is associated with gold, although its exact meaning and significance remain subjects of debate.

She appears in *Völuspá*, a poem in which a seeress recounts the past and future of the world.

According to its verses, Gullveig was pierced with spears and burned in the hall of the gods.

Three times they committed her to the flames.

Three times she was reborn.

She survived.

The poem then introduces the name **Heiðr**, belonging to a wandering seeress who practised a form of magic known as seiðr.

These passages are commonly understood as describing Gullveig under another name, although interpretations differ.

Some scholars have suggested that Gullveig might actually have been Freyja, since both figures are associated with magic and gold.

But the ancient poem never explicitly says so.

And we're not going to present an attractive theory as an established fact.

What we can say is that Gullveig's story reveals just how threatening and incomprehensible certain powers could appear, even to the gods.

And that trying to destroy a problem doesn't always make it disappear.

Sometimes the problem comes back.

Three times in a row.

A deeply inconvenient outcome for anyone hoping for a permanent solution.

But something even more serious followed this disturbing episode.

*Völuspá* tells of the first war in the world.

Odin hurled his spear towards the enemy.

The conflict began.

The Vanir went to war against the Æsir.

Even the gods' defences proved vulnerable.

We don't know every detail. The poem is deliberately brief and mysterious. Other sources tell the story differently and don't provide a detailed explanation for the war's origins.

But the central fact remains.

**The gods went to war with one another.**

The very beings who were supposed to maintain order in the universe.

If cosmic management had been questionable before, things had now reached an entirely new level.

---

## Chapter 4. The First War. Exhaustion Wins.

---

![The First War. Exhaustion Wins.](/visuals/norse/m02/04-siege-of-the-stormbound-fortress-1448.webp)

---

Imagine a war between beings who possess powers ordinary humans can barely comprehend.

This isn't a battle between two human armies.

These are gods who can command forces of nature, practise magic, influence events, and interfere with the very workings of the world.

And neither side can secure a decisive victory.

In *Ynglinga Saga*, part of Snorri Sturluson's *Heimskringla*, Odin leads an army against the Vanir.

But the Vanir fight back.

They defend their territory and launch attacks of their own.

Both sides win battles.

Both suffer defeats.

Both invade the other's lands and inflict damage.

It's worth remembering that *Ynglinga Saga* presents the gods differently from the Eddic poems. Snorri also portrays them as ancient rulers and ancestors of royal dynasties.

So we're not going to force every surviving version into one perfectly consistent military history.

But the overall meaning is clear.

Even divine power doesn't guarantee victory over another divine people.

The mighty Æsir couldn't simply wipe out the Vanir.

And the Vanir couldn't completely subdue the Æsir.

The war continued.

Until, eventually, both sides grew tired of it.

Not necessarily because they had become wiser.

Perhaps they simply realised that continuing was pointless.

Victory brought no lasting peace.

Fresh battles solved none of the old disagreements.

And the damage kept growing.

So the gods decided to do something that turned out to be harder than fighting.

Negotiate.

They arranged talks and agreed to make peace.

But ancient peace treaties rarely depended on goodwill alone.

It wasn't enough to promise that they would stop attacking each other.

The agreement needed guarantees.

And the Æsir and Vanir chose a method that might strike a modern audience as rather alarming.

**They exchanged hostages.**

Although after the first war in the history of the universe, expecting a joint team-building retreat might have been a little optimistic.

## Quick check

B. Because the Æsir and Vanir were simply two names for the same family.  
C. Because the gods' powers and roles overlapped and didn't fit into strict categories.  
D. Because ancient sources mention only Odin.  

**Answer:** C.

Divine responsibilities didn't come in neatly labelled categories. Apparently, even the gods struggled with role definitions.

Why did the Æsir and Vanir eventually make peace?

B. Neither side could secure a decisive victory, and the war was causing increasing damage.

The gods discovered that endless warfare wasn't resolving their differences. A groundbreaking insight humanity still has to rediscover from time to time.

## Chapter 5. A Peace Treaty. Divine Hostages Included.

---

![A Peace Treaty. Divine Hostages Included.](/visuals/norse/m02/05-norse-delegations-at-the-sacred-table-1448.webp)

---

So, the war was over.

The two sides agreed to make peace and exchange hostages — representatives of their respective families who would serve as guarantees of the new arrangement.

The Vanir sent **Njord** and his son **Freyr** to the Æsir.

These weren't random prisoners.

Njord was a powerful god of the sea, winds, and wealth.

Freyr was associated with fertility, prosperity, and peace.

They were important figures whose presence among the Æsir demonstrated that the treaty was being taken seriously.

In return, the Æsir sent **Hœnir** and **Mimir**.

Hœnir looked like an excellent leader.

He was handsome, impressive, and seemed perfectly suited to a position of authority.

Accompanying him was Mimir, an exceptionally wise being whom others consulted for advice.

At first, the Vanir were delighted with the exchange.

Hœnir looked so convincing that they appointed him their leader.

But they soon noticed something strange.

Whenever Mimir was nearby, Hœnir performed his duties perfectly well.

Mimir provided the advice. Hœnir made the decisions.

But whenever his wise adviser was absent, the new leader seemed unable to decide anything important.

Asked for his opinion, he repeatedly gave essentially the same answer:

**"Let others decide."**

You can imagine how quickly the Vanir began to regret their choice.

Here was the perfect leader.

Handsome, impressive, confident — right up until someone asked him to make a decision on his own.

The Vanir concluded that the Æsir had deceived them.

And responded in the finest traditions of ancient diplomacy.

They cut off Mimir's head and sent it back to Odin.

Presumably, a formal complaint seemed insufficient.

But that wasn't the end of Mimir's story.

Odin treated the head with special herbs and recited spells that preserved it and gave it the ability to speak.

From then on, Odin could consult Mimir and learn hidden knowledge from him.

So even after death, Mimir continued working as an adviser.

Remarkable professional dedication.

The peace between the Æsir and Vanir nevertheless survived.

Njord and Freyr took honoured places among the gods. Freyja, Njord's daughter and Freyr's sister, also came to live among the Æsir and became one of the most important goddesses in their community.

According to *Ynglinga Saga*, it was Freyja who introduced the Æsir to seiðr, a form of magic practised among the Vanir.

The former enemies had begun exchanging more than hostages.

They were now sharing knowledge, customs, and ways of understanding the world.

And there was another story associated with their reconciliation.

The *Prose Edda* tells us that the Æsir and Vanir spat into a common vessel as a sign of peace.

Then, from this rather unusual symbol of unity, they created an extraordinarily wise being named **Kvasir**.

He was so intelligent that he could answer any question.

In *Ynglinga Saga*, however, Kvasir already exists when the hostage exchange takes place and is described as a wise member of the Vanir.

These are two different surviving accounts of his origins.

But both connect him with the reconciliation between the divine families.

You have to admit, it's quite an unusual outcome for a peace conference.

The war ends, and the wisest man in the world appears.

Made, according to one version, from everyone's collective saliva.

Once again, Norse mythology demonstrates that conventional ideas of solemnity have their limits.

But Kvasir's unusual origins weren't the most important consequence of the peace.

**After the reconciliation, the Æsir and Vanir were no longer two entirely separate opposing sides.**

Their stories became intertwined.

Their gods became part of one shared mythological world.

Asgard changed.

There were new powers, new relationships, and plenty of new opportunities for conflict.

The last of those, naturally, wasn't going anywhere.

## Chapter 6. Who's in Charge Here? And What Does Everyone Actually Do?

---

![Who's in Charge Here? And What Does Everyone Actually Do?](/visuals/norse/m02/06-asgardian-council-in-the-great-hall-1448.webp)

---

Now that the two divine families have made peace, let's take a closer look at life in Asgard.

If you're imagining all the gods sitting around one enormous table while Odin hands out assignments, it's a lovely picture.

Just not a particularly accurate one.

The gods have their own territories, halls, interests, and abilities.

Their relationships are considerably more complicated than any formal hierarchy.

**Odin** occupies a special position.

He is the Allfather, associated with wisdom, war, poetry, and magic.

In some stories, he can survey the worlds from his high seat, Hliðskjálf.

He is accompanied by two ravens, Huginn and Muninn, whose names are associated with thought and memory.

He has many names and takes many forms.

He can appear as a fearsome ruler, a wanderer, a magician, or a man who asks far too many questions.

But the most important thing about Odin is his insatiable hunger for knowledge.

He is willing to sacrifice almost anything to understand how the world works and what awaits it in the future.

Just how much he'll sacrifice is something we'll discover in the next module.

**Frigg** holds an important place beside him.

She knows the fates of living beings, but that doesn't mean she feels obliged to reveal them to every curious god who asks.

She is associated with marriage, family, and motherhood, although reducing her importance to those subjects alone would be far too simplistic.

The *Prose Edda* emphasises the significance of the goddesses known as the Ásynjur.

It's an important reminder that the divine world isn't populated exclusively by heavily armed men with anger management issues.

**Thor** is famous for his extraordinary strength and his role as a protector of gods and humans.

His territory is called Thrúdvangar, and his enormous hall is Bilskirnir.

Thor possesses Mjölnir, the hammer he uses to solve problems with remarkable regularity.

Not every problem requires a hammer.

Thor appears to regard this as a matter of opinion.

**Týr** is associated with war, courage, and obligations.

Later, we'll discover that keeping a promise can sometimes cost you a hand.

**Baldr** is a beautiful and beloved god, described in the sources as wise, radiant, and kind.

Unfortunately, in Norse mythology, those qualities are no guarantee of a happy ending.

**Njord**, who came from the Vanir, is associated with the sea, winds, and wealth.

**Freyr** is connected with fertility, peace, and prosperity.

**Freyja** is associated with love, wealth, magic, and a share of the fallen warriors.

Her authority extends considerably further than beautiful jewellery and romantic adventures.

And then there's **Loki**.

Putting him into a neat category is rather difficult.

He isn't one of the Vanir. He comes from a family of jötnar, yet he lives and acts among the Æsir.

He is intelligent, inventive, capable of changing his shape, and frequently helps the gods escape from the most extraordinary situations.

Sometimes situations he created in the first place.

We'll be returning to Loki.

Frequently.

Unfortunately for everyone else.

But there is one figure in Asgard whose responsibilities seem refreshingly straightforward.

**Heimdall.**

He guards the approach to the gods' realm and watches over **Bifröst**, the bridge connecting their world with other parts of the cosmos.

The ancient texts describe Heimdall as a guardian with extraordinary sight and hearing.

He can see across enormous distances.

Hear grass growing.

And even detect the sound of wool growing on sheep.

He needs less sleep than a bird.

He also possesses a great horn called **Gjallarhorn**, whose sound will one day announce the arrival of terrible events.

Imagine a security officer who can hear grass growing.

And one day, the enemy will still get through.

But for now, Bifröst stands, Heimdall keeps watch, and the gods continue their lives in their respective halls.

You might think that, following the reconciliation, everything was finally running smoothly.

But even a community this powerful has a very practical problem.

Security.

And as we'll discover, the gods aren't always particularly good at choosing contractors.

## Chapter 7. The Fortress Wall. Someone Should Have Read the Contract.

---

![The Fortress Wall. Someone Should Have Read the Contract.](/visuals/norse/m02/07-the-giant-s-asgard-wallbuilders-1448.webp)

---

One day, while the gods were still establishing their settlement, a builder arrived.

He offered to construct a fortress around their home, so strong that even the giants would be unable to break through it.

It was an attractive proposal.

Strong walls could provide the gods with security.

But the builder demanded a rather unusual payment.

**Freyja.**

**The Sun.**

**And the Moon.**

Not a bag of gold.

Not a herd of cattle.

Not everlasting glory.

One of the most important goddesses and two celestial bodies.

You might expect the gods to reject the offer immediately.

Instead, they began discussing the terms.

Eventually, the Æsir agreed to the deal, with one important condition: the fortress had to be completed within a single winter.

If even one section remained unfinished by the first day of summer, the builder would receive nothing.

He was also forbidden from using anyone else's labour.

The builder asked for just one exception.

He wanted permission to use his stallion, **Svaðilfari**.

According to the *Prose Edda*, Loki was the one who advised the gods to accept this condition.

The Æsir decided the task was practically impossible.

And agreed to the contract.

Not literally in writing, of course.

But the source specifically mentions that the agreement was sealed with oaths and witnesses.

The builder needed protection from the gods, particularly Thor.

Work began.

And almost immediately, it became clear that the gods had seriously underestimated the horse.

Svaðilfari hauled enormous stones with such speed and strength that he performed more of the heavy labour than the builder himself.

Day after day, the walls rose higher.

Winter drew to an end.

And construction was nearly complete.

With just three days remaining before summer, it became obvious that the builder was dangerously close to meeting his deadline.

The Æsir were suddenly facing the prospect of losing Freyja, the Sun, and the Moon.

Which, you'll agree, is a fairly substantial price for a perimeter wall.

The gods immediately called a council.

And began looking for someone to blame.

They found him rather quickly.

**Loki.**

After all, he had recommended letting the builder use Svaðilfari.

The gods demanded that Loki fix the situation.

Otherwise, things would end very badly for him.

Loki, as usual, came up with a solution that wouldn't have occurred to anyone else.

He transformed himself into a beautiful mare.

That night, the mare appeared before Svaðilfari.

The stallion gave chase and ran away from his master.

Without his most valuable assistant, the builder could no longer finish the fortress on time.

When it became clear that the builder was actually a giant, and his anger turned threatening, the Æsir called for Thor.

Thor returned and killed the giant with his hammer.

The agreement, previously secured by solemn oaths, had been broken.

From the perspective of fairness, the whole affair looks decidedly questionable.

The gods entered into a risky agreement, deliberately interfered with its fulfilment, and then used force to get rid of the other party.

But that wasn't quite the end of the story.

Some time later, Loki returned.

And brought an extraordinary foal with him.

It had **eight legs**.

Its name was **Sleipnir**.

It would become Odin's famous horse — the finest and fastest of all horses among gods and humans.

And there's one detail we really can't ignore.

Sleipnir's mother was Loki.

Not his adoptive mother.

His biological mother.

Norse myths rarely offer straightforward solutions.

But even by their standards, this was an exceptionally unexpected outcome of a construction contract.

Still, the Æsir had got what they wanted.

The fortress was almost complete.

Freyja remained among the gods.

The Sun and Moon continued on their journeys.

Odin acquired a magnificent eight-legged horse.

And Asgard gained another incident best left out of the official meeting minutes.

And remember: we've barely even begun to explore Loki's story.

## Chapter 8. Divine Order. No Warranty Included.

---

![Divine Order. No Warranty Included.](/visuals/norse/m02/08-odin-overlooking-the-winter-realm-1448.webp)

---

By now, we know a little more about Asgard.

It's more than a collection of magnificent halls where the gods happen to live.

It's a place where different powers, interests, and destinies meet.

The Æsir and Vanir once fought against one another.

Then they made peace and exchanged hostages.

Former enemies became members of the same divine community.

New knowledge and traditions spread among the gods.

Odin strengthened his position.

Freyja brought powerful magical practices with her.

Heimdall guards the approach to the gods' realm.

Thor protects them against external threats.

And Loki periodically demonstrates why every organisation should be extremely suspicious of offers that sound too good to be true.

At first glance, Asgard seems like the very embodiment of power.

But look a little closer, and something far more interesting emerges.

The gods aren't all-powerful.

They can make mistakes.

They can lose.

They can break promises.

They can seek advice from those they once considered enemies.

And they can't always undo the consequences of their own decisions.

Norse mythology rarely portrays its gods as perfect beings.

They are powerful, but flawed.

Wise, but not always capable of seeing what lies ahead.

Capable of extraordinary courage — and extraordinary cruelty.

They create order, only to keep putting that order at risk themselves.

And that's one reason these ancient stories still feel so alive.

In Asgard, we recognise problems that are surprisingly human.

The difference is that the consequences aren't measured in damaged relationships or lost money.

Sometimes, an entire universe is at stake.

But there's one important detail we've barely explored.

Even among all these gods, Odin occupies a unique position.

Not simply because he is called the Allfather.

He knows that power alone won't protect the world from what fate has in store.

Thor's strength won't solve every problem.

High walls won't stop every threat.

And even the most successful agreement can't change destiny forever.

To understand what awaits the gods, Odin needs knowledge.

And for knowledge, he is prepared to do things others would never consider.

Sacrifice an eye.

Endure extraordinary suffering.

And one day spend nine nights hanging from the World Tree, pierced by his own spear.

But that's another story entirely.

**In the next module, we'll meet Odin.**

And discover why being the wisest of the gods comes at an extraordinary price.

Especially when you're paying in parts of your own body.

# Self-check

## 1. Which of these gods came to the Æsir from the Vanir following the peace treaty?

A. Týr  
B. Heimdall  
C. Thor  
D. Njord  

**Answer:** D.

Njord and his son Freyr were sent to the Æsir as part of the peace agreement. Norse diplomacy involved some remarkably long-term work placements.

---

## 2. How did Loki prevent the builder from completing the fortress on time?

A. He transformed into a mare and lured away the stallion Svaðilfari.  
B. He stole all the building stones.  
C. He asked Thor to destroy the completed wall.  
D. He hid the Sun and Moon.  

**Answer:** A.

Loki disrupted construction by turning into a mare. He later gave birth to the eight-legged Sleipnir. Probably not something anyone included in the original project plan.

---

## 3. Which statement best describes the gods' authority in Asgard?

A. Odin makes every decision alone, and the other gods never disagree.  
B. The gods possess absolute power and cannot make mistakes.  
C. The gods are powerful but must negotiate, make decisions, and live with the consequences of their mistakes.  
D. After the war with the Vanir, there were no more conflicts in Asgard.  

**Answer:** C.

Divine authority doesn't guarantee good judgment. Asgard survives despite a truly remarkable number of management failures.
