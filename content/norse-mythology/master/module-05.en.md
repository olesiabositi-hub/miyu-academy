# Module 5

# Loki. Some Problems Are Better Left Unsolved. Especially His Way.

*The story of Asgard's most inventive trickster. He saved the gods from disasters he'd caused himself, transformed into animals, stole other people's treasures, and could talk his way out of almost anything. Until one day, his jokes led to Baldr's death. And even the gods stopped laughing.*

**Time:** about 32–41 minutes

---

## Chapter 1. Loki. An Outsider Among His Own.

---

![Loki. An Outsider Among His Own.](/visuals/norse/m05/01-loki-in-the-winter-mead-hall-1448.webp)

---

Last time, we met Thor.

He fought giants, tried to pull the World Serpent out of the ocean, dressed as a bride, and repeatedly demonstrated that a hammer is an excellent way to solve problems.

Especially if you don't spend too much time wondering how those problems happened in the first place.

And somewhere nearby, almost every time, was one particular character.

**Loki.**

Sometimes he helped.

Sometimes he offered advice.

Sometimes he saved everyone from certain disaster.

But quite often, he was the reason the disaster had happened at all.

And now it's time to find out who he really was.

Let's start with a surprising fact.

Loki wasn't born into the family of the Æsir.

His father was a jötunn named **Fárbauti**, and his mother was **Laufey**.

The ancient sources frequently call him Laufey's son, using his mother's name alongside his own.

So why was someone descended from giants living among the gods of Asgard?

We don't have one definitive explanation.

But in *Lokasenna*, or *Loki's Flyting*, he reminds Odin that they once mingled their blood and made a pledge to drink together.

The passage is generally understood as referring to a special bond between them.

That doesn't mean Loki was Odin's biological son or brother, as people familiar mainly with Marvel might assume.

Their relationship in the myths is rather different.

Loki lives among the Æsir.

Travels with them.

Attends their feasts.

Helps obtain magical treasures.

Yet his origins and behaviour continually remind us that he doesn't quite belong.

And ancestry is only part of it.

Loki can change his shape.

He transforms into birds, fish, and other animals.

He can look completely different from one moment to the next.

Sometimes he even changes sex.

We've already encountered one example.

When a giant was building Asgard's defensive wall, Loki transformed into a mare, lured away the stallion Svaðilfari, and later gave birth to the eight-legged Sleipnir.

Yes. Loki is Odin's horse's mother.

We're going to accept that and move on.

Loki has a wife named **Sigyn**.

He also has children with a giantess named Angrboda.

Their names are Fenrir, Jörmungandr, and Hel.

Each will play an extraordinary role in the world's future.

We'll explore their stories properly in Module Seven.

For now, let's simply acknowledge that Loki's family tree looks as though several people were responsible for compiling it, and none of them agreed to compare notes.

But Loki's most dangerous quality wasn't his magic.

**His greatest weapon was his mind.**

He noticed other people's weaknesses.

Invented unexpected solutions.

Knew how to persuade, deceive, disguise himself, and negotiate.

And frequently turned out to be the only person capable of fixing a situation when every conventional approach had failed.

Thor had Mjölnir.

Odin had wisdom and secret knowledge.

Loki had the ability to think of something nobody else would even consider.

Sometimes this saved the gods.

Sometimes it created entirely new problems.

Which raises an important question.

Was Loki a friend of the gods or their enemy?

A clever helper or a dangerous destroyer?

The answer is more complicated than it first appears.

The ancient myths don't give us a simple story about someone who was evil from birth.

In some tales, Loki is indispensable.

In others, his actions cause catastrophe.

But one feature connects almost all of them.

Loki rarely thinks about the consequences for as long as he probably should.

He's exceptionally quick, however, at working out how to avoid punishment.

And we're about to see that in action.

In the next story, Loki will get captured by a giant.

Arrange the kidnapping of a goddess.

Threaten the youth of every god in Asgard.

And eventually save the day.

Quite a productive working day.

## Chapter 2. Idunn's Apples. How to Age Every God in a Single Day.

---

![Idunn's Apples. How to Age Every God in a Single Day.](/visuals/norse/m05/02-eagles-flight-through-the-storm-1448.webp)

---

One day, Odin, Loki, and Hœnir went travelling.

They walked across unfamiliar lands until they grew hungry.

The travellers found an ox and decided to cook it over a fire.

But something was wrong.

No matter how long they tried, the meat refused to cook.

The fire burned.

The pot grew hot.

Time passed.

And dinner remained stubbornly inedible.

Suddenly, a voice came from above.

An enormous eagle was sitting in a tree.

It announced that it was responsible for the cooking difficulties.

And it would happily stop interfering if the travellers shared their food.

The gods agreed.

The eagle descended.

And immediately tried to take an enormous portion of the ox.

Loki was furious.

He grabbed a long pole and struck the bird.

But the pole stuck to the eagle.

And Loki's hands stuck to the pole.

The eagle took flight.

Loki found himself dangling helplessly beneath it as the bird soared over rocks, trees, and cliffs.

His feet struck obstacles below.

His arms ached.

The situation was becoming extremely unpleasant.

Loki begged to be released.

And then the eagle named its price.

Loki had to lure **Idunn** out of Asgard, bringing her precious apples with her.

Loki agreed.

Naturally.

When a giant bird is dragging you across rocks, long-term strategic planning tends to become less of a priority.

But this was no ordinary eagle.

It was a giant named **Thjazi**, disguised in bird form.

And he wanted Idunn's apples.

Idunn guarded special apples that preserved the gods' youth.

This is an important feature of Norse mythology.

The gods aren't automatically and unconditionally immortal.

Their continued youth depends on a magical resource that must be protected.

Now imagine someone deciding to hand its guardian over to a giant.

Loki returned to Asgard.

Found Idunn.

And told her he'd discovered some extraordinary apples growing in the forest.

They were so remarkable, he said, that she really ought to bring her own apples and compare them.

Idunn believed him.

She took her apples and stepped outside Asgard.

Thjazi was waiting.

The giant assumed the form of an eagle.

Seized Idunn.

And carried her away to Jötunheim.

Before long, strange things began happening in Asgard.

The gods were ageing.

Their hair turned grey.

Their strength began to fade.

The youth they'd taken for granted was disappearing.

It didn't take long for everyone to realise that Idunn's disappearance wasn't merely inconvenient.

It threatened the very existence of the gods.

The Æsir gathered for a council.

They began asking who had last seen Idunn.

The trail led straight to Loki.

Accusations followed.

He was threatened with death and torture unless he fixed the situation immediately.

Once again, Loki faced a familiar choice.

Save everybody.

Or be the first to suffer the consequences of his own decisions.

He promised to bring Idunn back.

But first, he needed Freyja's help.

Loki borrowed her magical falcon cloak and flew to Jötunheim.

Thjazi happened to be away.

Idunn was alone in his home.

Loki transformed her into a nut.

Yes. A nut.

He picked her up in his talons and flew towards Asgard.

But Thjazi soon returned.

Discovered the theft.

Transformed into an enormous eagle once more.

And gave chase.

Picture the scene.

A small falcon is carrying a nut.

An enormous eagle is racing after it.

Ahead lie the walls of Asgard.

The gods spot the approaching birds and realise there's almost no time left.

They gather wood shavings beneath the fortress walls.

The instant Loki flies inside, they set the shavings alight.

Flames leap into the air.

Thjazi can't stop in time.

His feathers catch fire.

He falls.

And the gods kill him at the very gates of Asgard.

Idunn is safely returned.

The gods regain access to their apples.

Their youth is saved.

Disaster averted.

Loki, undoubtedly, deserves everyone's gratitude.

Provided nobody remembers who arranged the kidnapping.

Unfortunately, Thjazi had a daughter.

And when she heard what had happened to her father, she decided to visit Asgard.

Not, however, to thank everyone for the successful rescue.

Her name was **Skadi**.

And she arrived fully armed.

## Chapter 3. Skadi. You'll Laugh. It May Hurt.

---

![Skadi. You'll Laugh. It May Hurt.](/visuals/norse/m05/03-skadis-goat-drawn-loki-spectacle-1448.webp)

---

Skadi was Thjazi's daughter.

She lived among the harsh mountains of the North.

She was associated with hunting, skiing, and winter landscapes.

In the *Prose Edda*, Skadi appears as a strong, determined woman with no intention of overlooking her father's death.

She put on her helmet.

Took up her weapons.

And travelled to Asgard.

Her demand was straightforward.

**Compensation for Thjazi's death.**

The gods could have chosen to fight again.

Instead, they offered reconciliation.

Skadi agreed to discuss the terms.

Her first condition involved a rather unusual privilege.

She would be allowed to choose a husband from among the gods.

But she could only see the candidates' feet.

Their faces would remain hidden.

Why did the gods suggest such an arrangement?

The myth doesn't explain.

Clearly, Asgard had some fairly unconventional ideas about dating.

Skadi agreed.

She examined the candidates' feet.

One pair seemed particularly beautiful.

She assumed they belonged to Baldr, the handsome and beloved god of Asgard.

And announced her choice.

Unfortunately, the feet belonged to **Njord**.

The god of the sea.

Not Baldr.

Sometimes appearances really are deceptive.

Particularly when you're judging them exclusively from the ankles down.

But Skadi wasn't finished.

She declared that she would only make peace with the Æsir if they managed to make her laugh.

A simple enough request, you might think.

Except for the circumstances.

Here was an armed giantess.

Her father had just been killed.

She'd arrived demanding compensation.

And now someone was supposed to make her genuinely laugh.

The gods considered the problem.

And then, naturally, Loki stepped forward.

He had an idea.

It required a goat.

And a rope.

Loki tied one end of the rope to the goat's beard.

The other end to his own genitals.

Yes, you read that correctly.

Then the tug-of-war began.

The goat pulled in one direction.

Loki pulled in the other.

Both screamed.

Neither was particularly comfortable.

Finally, Loki collapsed into Skadi's lap.

And she laughed.

Perhaps for the first time since her father's death.

The second condition of reconciliation had been fulfilled.

You could say Loki had successfully resolved an international conflict.

At the expense of his dignity and an extremely sensitive part of his anatomy.

But Skadi still had a reason to grieve.

Her father was dead.

And no marriage could completely change that.

According to the *Prose Edda*, Odin took Thjazi's eyes, threw them into the sky, and turned them into stars.

This became another gesture of reconciliation.

Skadi remained among the gods.

Her marriage to Njord, however, was not especially happy.

She loved mountains, snow, and hunting.

He loved the sea and coastal landscapes.

They tried living together, alternating between the mountains and the shore.

But each found the other's favourite home unbearable.

Njord couldn't stand the howling wolves in the mountains.

Skadi couldn't bear the cries of seabirds.

Eventually, they went their separate ways.

A very Norse love story.

He wanted the seaside.

She wanted the mountains.

Neither was willing to compromise.

And even divine status couldn't resolve their disagreement about where to live.

But notice something about Loki.

This is already the second time he's become entangled in the affairs of Thjazi's family.

First, he arranged Idunn's kidnapping.

Then helped rescue her.

Then helped negotiate peace with Skadi.

Every time, he found a solution.

And every time, the consequences turned out to be considerably more complicated than the original problem.

Yet the gods continued accepting his help.

Because he really was useful.

And because they still didn't understand how dangerous his talent could become.

## Chapter 4. Cursed Gold. Should Have Left the Otter Alone.

---

![Cursed Gold. Should Have Left the Otter Alone.](/visuals/norse/m05/04-loki-and-andvaris-gilded-bargain-1448.webp)

---

One day, Odin, Loki, and Hœnir were travelling through the world.

They were walking along a river when they noticed something unusual.

An otter was sitting on the bank.

It had caught a salmon and was peacefully eating it.

Loki looked at the animal.

Picked up a stone.

And killed the otter.

Why?

In the ancient account, he's pleased that one throw has provided both food and a valuable pelt.

A fairly practical approach.

Apart from one important detail.

This wasn't an ordinary otter.

It was **Otr**, the son of a powerful being named Hreidmar.

Otr could take the form of an otter and loved fishing.

The travellers knew none of this.

They collected their prize and continued their journey.

That evening, they reached Hreidmar's home.

And showed their host the otter.

Imagine the situation.

You welcome visitors into your house.

They arrive with a gift.

And the gift is your dead son.

Dinner conversation becomes slightly awkward.

Hreidmar recognised Otr.

His other sons, **Fafnir** and **Regin**, saw what had happened.

The gods were captured.

Hreidmar demanded compensation for the killing.

His terms were extremely specific.

The otter's skin had to be removed.

Filled with gold.

And then covered with more gold on the outside, until not a single hair could be seen.

The travellers agreed.

Loki was sent to obtain the treasure.

He went looking for an exceptionally wealthy dwarf named **Andvari**.

Andvari lived near a waterfall and often took the form of a pike.

Loki caught him with a net.

And demanded all his treasure.

Andvari didn't want to surrender his accumulated wealth.

But he had little choice.

He produced his gold.

Loki took everything.

The dwarf, however, tried to keep one ring.

It was called **Andvaranaut**.

The ring was particularly precious to him.

But Loki noticed it.

And demanded it too.

Andvari begged him to reconsider.

Asked to keep at least that one possession.

Loki refused.

And took the ring.

Then the dwarf pronounced a curse.

The gold would bring death to those who possessed it.

And the ring would prove particularly dangerous.

Loki heard the warning.

And went on his way.

Not especially concerned.

After all, the gold wasn't intended for him.

Odin, Loki, and Hœnir brought the treasure to Hreidmar.

They filled the otter skin with gold.

Then covered its exterior.

Hreidmar inspected the payment carefully.

And discovered one uncovered whisker.

One.

A single whisker.

But an agreement is an agreement.

So Odin placed the ring Andvaranaut over it.

The compensation was finally complete.

The gods were released.

As they departed, Loki warned Hreidmar about the curse.

Hreidmar paid little attention.

He was now enormously wealthy.

And the future, presumably, looked rather promising.

But the gold was already beginning to destroy his family.

Fafnir and Regin demanded their share.

Hreidmar refused.

So Fafnir killed his own father.

Then decided he wasn't going to share with his brother either.

He took possession of the treasure and eventually became a terrifying dragon guarding the gold.

This story would later become part of the great legendary cycle surrounding Sigurd the Dragon Slayer.

And it all began with an otter.

A stone thrown by Loki.

And one ring he refused to leave with its owner.

Notice how the character of the story changes.

At first, the situation seems almost absurd.

The gods accidentally kill the wrong animal.

Then they're forced to pay compensation.

Loki obtains somebody else's gold and fixes the problem.

But the result is the destruction of an entire family.

And the curse continues to affect later generations.

That's how many stories about Loki work.

**There's always a solution. But the solution can end up costing more than the original problem.**

By this point, the gods really ought to have learned something.

Unfortunately, Asgard seems to have had a rather unusual approach to recurring mistakes.

And things are about to get worse.

Much worse.

Because the next story concerns someone almost every god loved.

And for the first time, Loki won't be able to dismiss the consequences as merely a joke gone wrong.

## Quick check

Why did the gods begin ageing after Idunn disappeared?

A. Odin forgot to renew their immortality subscription.  
B. Thor accidentally smashed the fountain of youth with his hammer.  
C. Idunn guarded the apples that preserved the gods' youth.  
D. Loki put all the Æsir on a special goat-meat diet.  

**Answer:** C.

Without Idunn and her apples, the gods began to age. Loki created the crisis and then heroically resolved it. An efficient business model, if we overlook the consequences.

## Quick check

Why was Andvari's gold considered cursed?

A. It transformed its owner into an otter at night.  
B. Andvari cursed his treasure after Loki took his gold and ring.  
C. Thor accidentally struck the treasure with Mjölnir.  
D. The gold turned out to be counterfeit. Hreidmar's family was extremely unhappy with their investment.  

**Answer:** B.

Andvari cursed the treasure, and the gold did indeed bring death to its later owners. The investment's risk profile had been somewhat understated.

## Chapter 5. Baldr. The Joke That Stopped Being Funny.

---

![Baldr. The Joke That Stopped Being Funny.](/visuals/norse/m05/05-baldrs-tragic-fall-in-asgard-1448.webp)

---

There was a god in Asgard named **Baldr**.

People loved him.

Which was rather unusual.

Because, as we've already discovered, among the Norse gods it wasn't always easy to find someone who hadn't insulted, deceived, or accidentally killed somebody else.

But Baldr was different.

He was described as beautiful, radiant, wise, and kind.

He was the son of Odin and Frigg.

His wife was named Nanna.

It seemed that a god like this should enjoy a long and happy existence.

Then Baldr began having terrible dreams.

Dreams that foretold his death.

The gods were worried.

Frigg most of all.

She decided to do everything in her power to protect her son.

So she travelled through the world, securing promises from everything that existed.

From fire.

From water.

From stones.

From metals.

From trees.

From diseases.

From animals.

From poisons.

From all sorts of beings and forces.

Everything had to swear never to harm Baldr.

And everything agreed.

Baldr became virtually invulnerable.

Naturally, the gods immediately invented a new game.

They began throwing things at him.

Stones.

Weapons.

Other dangerous objects.

Nothing could hurt him.

Baldr remained completely unharmed.

The gods were delighted.

And continued testing his invulnerability.

It's a rather strange idea when you think about it.

Their beloved god is protected from every danger.

And the first thing his friends decide to do is start throwing weapons at him.

But that's how the *Prose Edda* tells the story.

Loki watched.

And he didn't like what he saw.

He wanted to discover the secret of Baldr's protection.

So he disguised himself as a woman and went to speak to Frigg.

During the conversation, Loki carefully asked about her son's remarkable invulnerability.

Frigg explained that she'd obtained promises from almost everything in the world.

**Almost.**

One small plant had been overlooked.

Mistletoe.

Frigg considered it too young to be asked for an oath.

That was all.

One exception.

One tiny detail.

Something nobody considered dangerous.

Loki immediately went looking for mistletoe.

He found it.

And fashioned it into a projectile.

Then he returned to the gods.

They were still entertaining themselves by throwing things at Baldr.

But among them stood **Hödr**, Baldr's brother.

He was blind.

And remained apart from the game.

Loki asked why he wasn't participating.

Hödr explained that he couldn't see where to aim.

And he had nothing to throw anyway.

Loki offered to help.

He placed the mistletoe in Hödr's hand.

Directed his aim.

And Hödr threw it.

The mistletoe struck Baldr.

And he fell dead.

Suddenly.

Without warning.

Without any chance to undo what had happened.

Silence fell over the gathering.

The gods were devastated.

Odin's beloved son was dead.

Killed by his own brother, who hadn't understood what he was doing.

Loki, however, knew exactly what the result would be.

And this is where the story changes.

Before now, Loki's actions had often been foolish, dangerous, or cruel.

But there had usually been a solution.

Sif received new hair.

Idunn returned to Asgard.

Thor recovered Mjölnir.

Even the most ridiculous situations somehow got resolved.

Now Baldr was dead.

And no amount of cleverness could simply reverse his death.

The gods prepared a funeral.

Baldr's body was placed upon an enormous ship named Hringhorni.

The *Prose Edda* tells us that the ship was so heavy that only a giantess named Hyrrokkin could move it.

Gods and other powerful beings gathered for the ceremony.

Nanna, Baldr's wife, died of grief and was placed beside her husband.

Odin leaned over his son's body.

And whispered something into his ear that nobody else could hear.

Remember?

That's what Odin asked Vafthrudnir about in Module Three.

We still don't know what he said.

The ship became Baldr's funeral pyre.

And the flames carried him away from the world of the living.

But the gods weren't ready to accept what had happened.

They decided to try bringing him back.

After all, if anyone might be able to negotiate with death, surely it was worth asking.

Unfortunately, death had terms and conditions too.

## Chapter 6. Everyone Must Cry. Except One.

---

![Everyone Must Cry. Except One.](/visuals/norse/m05/06-norse-giantess-at-the-funeral-shrine-1448.webp)

---

After Baldr's death, Frigg asked the gods who would be willing to travel to the realm of the dead.

Who would dare to find Hel and ask for her son to be returned?

**Hermod** volunteered.

Odin lent him Sleipnir, his eight-legged horse.

Hermod began his journey.

For nine nights, he travelled through deep, dark valleys.

Until he reached the river Gjöll.

It was crossed by a bridge guarded by a maiden named Módgud.

She asked who the traveller was and why he was travelling towards the dead.

Hermod explained that he was looking for Baldr.

Módgud told him that Baldr had already passed that way.

And directed him onwards.

Hermod continued.

He reached the gates of Hel.

And leapt over them on Sleipnir.

Inside, he found Baldr.

He was among the dead.

Hermod stayed the night.

The following morning, he asked Hel to release Baldr and allow him to return to the gods.

He described the grief throughout Asgard.

How empty everything felt after Baldr's death.

How many beings desperately wanted to see him alive again.

Hel listened.

Then she proposed a condition.

**Let everything in existence weep for Baldr.**

Not just the gods.

Not just humans.

Every living and non-living thing.

If absolutely everything wept, Hel would release him.

But if even one being refused, Baldr would remain among the dead.

Hermod returned with the news.

The gods sent messengers across the world.

They asked everyone to mourn Baldr.

And the world responded.

Humans wept.

Animals wept.

Trees wept.

Stones.

Metals.

Everything expressed grief for the fallen god.

It seemed the condition would be fulfilled.

But one day, the messengers came upon a giantess sitting in a cave.

Her name was **Thökk**.

They asked her to weep for Baldr.

She refused.

She declared that she had never loved Odin's son, alive or dead.

And Hel could keep what she already possessed.

Not a single tear.

Not the slightest expression of sorrow.

Baldr remained among the dead.

The entire universe had almost managed to agree.

And one refusal ruined everything.

The *Prose Edda* suggests that Thökk was probably Loki in disguise.

It's worth noting that this is presented as a traditional explanation, rather than a scene where we directly witness the transformation.

But if Thökk really was Loki, the situation becomes even more terrible.

He didn't merely cause Baldr's death.

He prevented his return.

And this is where Loki's familiar ingenuity turns into something fundamentally different.

Before, his cleverness helped him escape trouble.

Now it becomes a means of causing suffering.

Not for profit.

Not to save his own life.

Not even to achieve some obvious goal.

Simply because he can.

But the gods haven't finished dealing with Loki.

There's still a feast to come.

And he intends to tell practically everyone exactly what he thinks of them.

Possibly the last party he should ever have attended.

## Chapter 7. The Feast in Asgard. Every Family Secret in One Evening.

---

![The Feast in Asgard. Every Family Secret in One Evening.](/visuals/norse/m05/07-lokis-defiance-in-the-mead-hall-1448.webp)

---

Imagine a magnificent feast.

A grand hall.

Tables overflowing with food.

Mead and other drinks.

The gods gathered together.

Guests talking, laughing, and enjoying the evening.

The feast is hosted by **Ægir**, a powerful being associated with the sea.

Among the guests are Æsir, Vanir, and other inhabitants of the mythological world.

Odin, Frigg, Freyja, Njord, Týr, and many others.

And Loki.

Of course.

The story survives in the poem **Lokasenna**, usually translated as *Loki's Flyting*, from the *Poetic Edda*.

It's an almost uninterrupted exchange of accusations, insults, and vicious replies.

It's important to remember that the poem's precise place in the overall sequence of myths isn't established. We've placed it here to develop Loki's story, not because every surviving source follows this exact chronology.

At first, everything seemed perfectly peaceful.

The guests were praising Ægir's servants for their excellent service.

For some reason, even that irritated Loki.

He killed one of the servants, Fimafeng.

The gods were furious.

They seized their weapons.

And drove Loki out of the hall.

You might think the evening had been saved.

But Loki returned.

At the entrance, he met the other servant, Eldir.

He asked what the gods were discussing.

Eldir explained that they were talking about their achievements and weapons.

But nobody had anything good to say about Loki.

Perhaps this would have been an excellent moment to turn around and leave.

Instead, Loki went inside.

He demanded a drink.

The gods fell silent.

Nobody wanted to invite him to the table.

Then Loki reminded Odin of their old alliance.

How they'd once mingled their blood and promised to drink together.

Odin ordered a place to be made for him.

Loki sat down.

And began.

First, he attacked Bragi, the god of poetry.

Called him a coward.

Then turned on Idunn.

Then Gefjon, Frigg, Freyja, Njord, Týr, and the others.

He accused some of infidelity.

Others of cowardice.

Others of hypocrisy.

And reminded several of them about particularly unpleasant family incidents.

Everyone tried to respond.

Some told him to be quiet.

Others pointed out that he'd gone too far.

But Loki seemed determined to find the most painful subject for every person in the room.

Remember, the accusations in this poem aren't a neutral historical record.

This is a poetic contest of insults in which the characters are trying to humiliate one another.

Not every accusation Loki makes should be treated as an established fact about the gods.

But something else matters more for understanding his character.

He knows everyone's weaknesses.

And deliberately exploits them.

Even Skadi joins the argument.

Loki brings up her father's death.

She responds by warning him of the punishment that awaits him.

Baldr is mentioned too.

The exchanges grow increasingly dangerous.

And the atmosphere in the hall becomes almost unbearable.

Imagine someone arriving at a family celebration and publicly listing everyone's affairs, betrayals, unsuccessful marriages, and questionable life choices.

Then insisting they're merely sharing a few interesting observations.

But Loki doesn't stop.

And finally, Thor arrives.

With his hammer.

Possibly the only person at the feast who has no need to search for the right words.

Thor orders Loki to be silent.

Threatens to strike him with Mjölnir.

Loki tries to insult him in return.

But he knows perfectly well that Thor might actually carry out his threat.

Eventually, Loki leaves.

And the confrontation ends.

The poem goes on to describe Loki's punishment.

But before we get there, let's consider one important question.

Why did Loki behave this way?

Perhaps he was jealous.

Perhaps he wanted to humiliate those who considered themselves superior.

Perhaps he resented his own uncertain position among the Æsir.

The ancient sources don't offer one definitive psychological explanation.

But the result is clear.

Loki is no longer simply breaking the rules.

He's deliberately destroying the relationships that once allowed him to remain among the gods.

And now, even his intelligence won't be enough to put everything back together.

## Chapter 8. The Final Joke. A Salmon, a Net, and a Snake Overhead.

---

![The Final Joke. A Salmon, a Net, and a Snake Overhead.](/visuals/norse/m05/08-lokis-venomous-captivity-1448.webp)

---

The gods had finally had enough of Loki.

Too much had happened.

Baldr's death.

The failure to bring him back.

The endless deception.

The insults.

And the growing realisation that someone they'd trusted so many times had become a threat.

The *Prose Edda* tells us that Loki fled into the mountains.

There, he built a house with four doors.

One facing each direction.

That way, he could spot approaching enemies from anywhere.

During the day, Loki frequently transformed himself into a salmon.

And hid in the water near a waterfall called Fránangr.

It seemed almost impossible to catch him.

But Loki wanted to prepare for every possibility.

One day, he sat beside a fire and began weaving a fishing net.

He was wondering how the gods might attempt to catch him in the water.

He devised a design.

Interlaced the threads.

Constructed the net.

And suddenly noticed the gods approaching.

Loki threw the net into the fire.

Then transformed into a salmon and leapt into the river.

He assumed he'd destroyed the only evidence.

But among the arriving gods was Kvasir.

The same wise figure we encountered in the story of the Mead of Poetry.

When Kvasir examined the remains of the burned net in the ashes, he understood how it had been constructed.

The gods made another one.

And began searching the river.

Loki hid among the rocks.

At first, the net passed over him.

The gods changed their strategy.

Now Thor waded directly through the water, helping to hold the net in place.

Loki was faced with a choice.

Dash towards the sea.

Or jump over the net.

He tried to jump.

But Thor managed to grab him.

The salmon slipped through his hands.

Thor caught him by the tail.

According to the myth, this is why a salmon's body narrows towards the tail.

A rather unconventional explanation of fish anatomy.

But back to Loki.

The gods had caught him.

And this time, they weren't going to let him go.

They took him to a cave.

Prepared three great stone slabs.

And bound him to them.

The *Prose Edda* describes an exceptionally cruel punishment.

The gods seized Loki's sons. One was transformed into a wolf and killed his brother. The dead son's entrails were then used to bind Loki, turning into iron restraints.

Even in a brief retelling, the brutality of the scene is impossible to ignore.

The gods who are supposed to maintain order are themselves responsible for an act of terrible vengeance.

And that's another reason not to treat Norse mythology as a simple story about entirely good gods and an entirely evil Loki.

Then **Skadi** fastened a venomous serpent above Loki's face.

Poison dripped slowly down.

Directly towards him.

But Loki wasn't completely alone.

His wife, **Sigyn**, remained beside him.

She held a bowl above her husband's face.

Catching the drops of poison before they could hurt him.

How long did she stand there?

The myth doesn't say.

But eventually, the bowl would fill.

And Sigyn had to step away to empty it.

In those moments, the poison struck Loki.

He convulsed in agony.

So violently that the earth shook.

This, according to Norse mythology, was the cause of earthquakes.

Imagine the final scene.

A deep cave.

Cold stone.

Loki bound in place.

A venomous serpent above his face.

And Sigyn holding a bowl that cannot protect him forever.

There's no room for the old jokes now.

Loki is no longer laughing.

He cannot transform into a bird and fly away.

He cannot invent another clever bargain.

He cannot deceive another giant.

He remains in chains.

And he will stay there until Ragnarök.

When the final battle begins, his bonds will break.

Loki will be free again.

But his role in the world's future will be very different.

We'll return to that story later.

For now, let's go back to our original question.

Who was Loki?

A villain?

A trickster?

An indispensable helper to the gods?

An outsider who never quite managed to belong?

All of these are parts of his story.

He really did help the Æsir.

Recovered stolen treasures.

Found solutions to impossible problems.

But he crossed boundaries again and again.

Until one day, something happened that could no longer be undone.

**Loki's most dangerous ability wasn't shapeshifting. It was his talent for destroying trust.**

And yet, we shouldn't completely forget the actions of the other gods.

They used his cleverness when they needed help.

Forgave his deception when the results suited them.

And eventually responded to his betrayal with a punishment so terrible that it shook the earth.

Norse mythology doesn't give us a simple answer about who was entirely right.

And perhaps that's exactly why Loki remains one of the most fascinating characters of the ancient North.

He's funny and frightening.

Useful and dangerous.

Brilliant and destructive.

Someone who would probably be incredibly interesting to talk to.

But someone you really shouldn't trust with the keys to your house.

Let alone Asgard.

Our introduction to Loki ends in a dark cave.

But his story isn't over.

Because Loki has children.

And if Loki's own actions have already endangered the gods, his descendants are destined to play a part in something far more terrible.

Before we meet them, however, we need to explore the beings who know more about fate than even Odin might want to hear.

**In the next module, we'll meet the goddesses, Valkyries, witches, and Norns.**

And discover why sometimes it's better not to ask what the future holds.

Especially if the answer is already known.

## 1. How did Loki help the giant Thjazi kidnap Idunn?

A. He stole the apples while disguised as Odin.  
B. He lured Idunn out of Asgard by claiming he'd found unusual apples.  
C. He transformed all the gods into old people while Idunn was asleep.  
D. He sold the apples to Thjazi on a subscription plan. First month free.  

**Answer:** B.

Loki persuaded Idunn to leave Asgard, where Thjazi kidnapped her. Loki later rescued her himself. It's convenient being both the cause of an emergency and the head of the rescue operation.

---

## 2. Why did Baldr die despite the promises that everything in the world would protect him?

A. Mistletoe hadn't sworn an oath, and Loki used it to direct the blind Hödr's fatal throw.  
B. Thor accidentally mistook Baldr for a giant.  
C. Baldr voluntarily travelled to Hel to review the accommodation.  
D. Frigg forgot to renew his annual protection plan.  

**Answer:** A.

Frigg hadn't asked mistletoe for an oath because she considered it too young. Loki found the exception. Sometimes an entire security system fails because of one overlooked detail.

---

## 3. How did the gods manage to catch Loki while he was hiding as a salmon?

A. Freyja lured him out with golden apples.  
B. Odin ordered the entire river to freeze.  
C. Sleipnir jumped over the waterfall and caught the fish with his hooves.  
D. The gods reconstructed the net Loki had invented, and Thor caught him by the tail.  

**Answer:** D.

Loki invented the very net that was used to capture him. He became the victim of his own invention. An extremely unfortunate intellectual-property outcome.
