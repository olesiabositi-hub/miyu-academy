# Module 1
# How the World of Greek Mythology Is Actually Structured

**Time:** about 15–18 minutes

---

## Let’s not start with Zeus

If you ask someone to name a Greek god, Zeus will probably be one of the first names they say.

Zeus, Poseidon, Hades — a familiar group.

But there is one problem.

If you start learning mythology with them, it can quickly feel as if all these gods just appeared from nowhere at the same time.

That is not true at all.

Before Zeus, there was another generation of gods.

And before them, there were even older beings.

So first I suggest building a very simple map. No huge family tree and no attempt to remember twenty names in one lesson.

For now, we need only three levels:

**the first world → the Titans → the new generation of gods**

After that, everything will slowly start to make sense.

---

# 1. First, a small warning

There is one important thing about Greek mythology.

There is no single book with one correct version of every event.

People told these myths for hundreds of years. Different poets, cities, and periods could tell the same story in slightly different ways.

So sometimes you will see:

**“in Hesiod’s version…”**

or:

**“in another tradition…”**

This does not mean that someone made a mistake.

That is simply how mythology works.

I do not want to turn this course into a study of ten versions of every myth. We will use the main version that helps us understand the bigger picture, and we will mention differences only when they are really interesting.

### If you remember one idea:

**Greek mythology is a large network of stories, not one book with one single canon.**

---

# 2. Good. So what was there at the very beginning?

One of the best-known versions of the beginning of the world comes to us from the ancient Greek poet Hesiod.

And it begins with:

# Chaos

Just do not immediately imagine the mess in Jira before a release.

Our modern word “chaos” can send us in the wrong direction.

Here, Chaos is closer to a first emptiness, a gap, a space that existed at the very beginning.

Then the first beings appear.

And one of them is:

# Gaia

**Gaia is the Earth.**

And there is an interesting detail here.

Do not ask:

> “Who were Gaia’s parents?”

In this version of the myth, she has none.

Gaia is one of the **first beings**. She simply appears at the beginning of the universe.

So she is not the daughter of Chaos.

Chaos appears first, and then Gaia and other first powers appear.

For our course, Gaia is enough for now.

### Gaia = the Earth itself.

Not a goddess who was given the job of “being responsible for the earth.”

She is literally the Earth, shown as a living divine being.

---

## Quick check

**Where did Gaia come from?**

A. Chaos gave birth to her  
B. She is one of the first beings and has no parents in this version  
C. She is Zeus’s daughter

**Answer:** B.

Yes. In Hesiod’s version, Gaia is not the daughter of Chaos — she is one of the very first beings in the world.

---

# 3. And then the Sky appears

Gaia gives birth by herself to:

# Uranus

And here there is one language detail that I really like.

In Greek:

**ουρανός — ouranós — “sky.”**

So the name Uranus is directly connected to what he is:

**Gaia = Earth**

**Uranus = Sky**

And yes — the planet Uranus that we know today was named after the mythological Uranus, not the other way around. In modern Greek, the word *ουρανός* still means “sky.”

So for now, just imagine a very old picture of the world:

**Earth — Gaia**

and above her

**Sky — Uranus**

From them comes the next generation.

And this is where we meet:

# The Titans

---

# 4. So who are the Titans?

Today, the word “titan” sounds as if a giant as tall as a skyscraper is about to appear.

But that is not exactly what the ancient Greeks meant.

In Hesiod’s version, Gaia and Uranus have **12 Titans**.

Six men:

Oceanus  
Coeus  
Crius  
Hyperion  
Iapetus  
Cronus

And six women:

Theia  
Rhea  
Themis  
Mnemosyne  
Phoebe  
Tethys

No, you **do not need to remember them now**.

I included the list only to answer one question:

> “So how many of them were there?”

**There were twelve main elder Titans.**

---

## Were they huge?

Not necessarily.

This is another thing that is easy to imagine incorrectly because of the modern meaning of the word “titan.”

The Titans are first of all **an older generation of divine beings**, not a separate kind of huge monster.

In ancient art, Titans could be shown in human form. For example, Atlas and Prometheus look like men, not fantasy monsters.

And it is also useful not to confuse these two groups:

**Titans ≠ Giants.**

In Greek mythology, the Giants are a completely different group of beings.

---

## So why do the Titans matter?

For our course, the main point is this:

**The Titans are the older generation of gods between the first world and the gods of Zeus.**

So our map now looks like this:

**Gaia + Uranus**

↓

**Titans**

↓

**next generation of gods**

Out of the twelve Titans, we need only two for now:

# Cronus
and
# Rhea

Because their children will become the gods we know much better.

---

# 5. And this is where it is easy to get confused

Cronus is a Titan.

Rhea is a Titaness.

Their children are:

Hestia  
Demeter  
Hera  
Hades  
Poseidon  
Zeus

And there is a very logical question:

> **“Wait. If their mother and father are Titans, does that mean Zeus, Hera, and Hades are Titans too?”**

No.

### Zeus, Hera, Hades, Poseidon, Demeter, and Hestia are not Titans.

They are the **next generation of gods**.

We connect Hera, Zeus, Poseidon, Demeter, and others with the Olympian order.

Hades is a little different: he belongs to the same generation, but he lives and rules not on Olympus, but in the Underworld. Because of this, he is usually not included in the classic list of the twelve Olympians.

For now, we only need one very simple line:

**Cronus and Rhea → Titans**

**their children → next generation of gods**

---

## Quick check

**Which one of them is a Titan?**

A. Zeus  
B. Hades  
C. Cronus

**Answer:** C. Cronus.

Zeus and Hades are his sons and belong to the next generation.

---

# 6. Why did Cronus lose power at all?

It is quite a family story. And, to put it mildly, this was not a simple family.

Cronus himself took power from his father Uranus.

Then he learned about a prophecy:

**one of his own children would overthrow him too.**

Cronus found the most radical solution possible.

He started to…

**swallow his children.**

Hestia.

Demeter.

Hera.

Hades.

Poseidon.

One after another.

But when the last child was born, Rhea decided she had had enough.

She hid the baby.

And instead, she gave Cronus a stone wrapped in baby clothes.

He swallowed it.

Yes, without noticing the difference.

The rescued child was called:

# Zeus

Zeus grew up.

Later, his brothers and sisters were freed, and the older generation finally lost power.

We do not need all the details of that war now.

For the rest of the course, this is enough:

**the Titans ruled first → then power passed to the generation of Zeus.**

---

# 7. Zeus, Poseidon, and Hades

Now we have three brothers:

# Zeus

# Poseidon

# Hades

Usually, this is where people are simply given a table:

> Zeus — sky.  
> Poseidon — sea.  
> Hades — Underworld.

You can remember that.

But it is much more interesting to understand:

**why exactly these areas?**

It turns out the myth gives a very clear answer.

---

# 8. They drew lots

When it was time to divide power over the world, the three brothers decided it literally **by drawing lots**.

Poseidon tells us about this in Homer’s *Iliad*.

By lot:

### Zeus received the sky.

### Poseidon received the sea.

### Hades received the Underworld.

But **the earth and Olympus stayed shared** by all three brothers.

I like this detail because it immediately removes the feeling that someone had already decided:

> “You will be the god of the sea, and you will be the god of the dead.”

No.

They **divided the areas of the world by lot**.

And now the three connections are much easier to remember.

---

# 9. Zeus

He received:

# The Sky

So we get:

thunder,  
lightning,  
power over the sky.

And over time, Zeus becomes the main ruler among the gods.

### Simple connection:

**Zeus → above**

---

# 10. Poseidon

He received:

# The Sea

That is why the sea is his most famous area.

Later, we will see that Poseidon is connected not only with the sea — for example, he also has a very surprising connection with horses and earthquakes.

But we do not need that yet.

### Simple connection:

**Poseidon → sea**

---

# 11. Hades

He received:

# The Underworld

The world of the dead.

### Simple connection:

**Hades → world of the dead**

And here we need to stop at one very common mistake.

---

# 12. No, Hades is not the Greek devil

I completely understand where this idea comes from.

He lives underground.

The souls of the dead are around him.

In films, he is often shown as a dark villain.

So it seems obvious:

> Hades = something like Satan.

But no.

For an ancient Greek, this was a completely different idea.

**Hades is the ruler of the world of the dead.**

Not the god of evil.

Not the enemy of all the other gods.

Not a character who wants to take over Olympus just because he likes being bad.

He has his own kingdom and his own role.

All dead people eventually come to his world — not because they were bad, but because they **died**.

So I would immediately replace this idea in your head:

❌ **Hades = evil**

with

✓ **Hades = ruler of the world of the dead**

After that, he becomes much easier to understand.

By the way, Hades has a very famous guard.

But we will get to him later.

---

# 13. Let’s put it all together

We do not need to remember every name now.

We need to see the structure.

### First

**the first world**

Chaos

Gaia — Earth

Uranus — Sky

↓

### Then

**the older generation of gods**

12 Titans

the two we need now:

Cronus + Rhea

↓

### Then

**the new generation**

their children:

Hestia  
Demeter  
Hera  
Hades  
Poseidon  
Zeus

And for now, remember the three brothers:

**Zeus → sky**

**Poseidon → sea**

**Hades → Underworld**

## Sources for fact-checking — do not publish inside the lesson

The basic cosmology comes from Hesiod’s *Theogony*: Chaos appears first; then Gaia, Tartarus, and Eros; Gaia gives birth by herself to the starry Sky — Uranus. Then Gaia and Uranus have the twelve Titans.

Hesiod lists six male Titans and six female Titans. Ancient images show that “Titan” does not necessarily mean a giant: for example, Prometheus and Atlas were shown in human form.

In modern Greek, **ουρανός** means “sky,” and the name of the planet Uranus comes from the name of the mythological Uranus.

The division of the world between the three brothers by lot is described directly in Book XV of the *Iliad*: Poseidon received the sea, Hades received the darkness below, Zeus received the sky; the earth and Olympus stayed shared.
