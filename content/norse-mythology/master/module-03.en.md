# Module 3

# Odin. Knowledge Comes at a Price.

*The story of a god who sacrificed an eye, spent nine nights hanging from the World Tree, uncovered the secrets of the runes — and still couldn't answer the one question that mattered most: how to escape his own fate.*

**Time:** about 27–34 minutes

---

## Chapter 1. The Allfather. Knows a Lot. Not Enough.

---

![The Allfather. Knows a Lot. Not Enough.](/visuals/norse/m03/01-elder-of-the-misty-fjord-1448.webp)

---

Last time, we explored Asgard.

We discovered that the gods were remarkably good at fighting wars, negotiating questionable agreements, exchanging hostages, and making decisions that required increasingly bizarre solutions.

Now it's time to get to know the god who holds a particularly important position among them.

**Odin.**

They call him the Allfather.

He is associated with wisdom, power, war, poetry, magic, and death.

He has many names, because he appears in many different forms throughout the myths.

Sometimes he is the majestic ruler of Asgard.

Sometimes he's a grey-haired wanderer wearing a wide-brimmed hat and a dark cloak.

Sometimes he's a dangerous sorcerer.

And sometimes he's a stranger who arrives at your door, asks a few questions, and unexpectedly reveals that he knows considerably more about your life than you do.

One thing sets Odin apart from many of the other gods.

He doesn't simply want power.

**He wants knowledge.**

He wants to know where the world came from.

Who lives in its most distant regions.

What secrets the giants are hiding.

What will happen tomorrow.

What will happen a thousand years from now.

And, above all, what awaits the gods at the end of their existence.

Odin possesses enormous authority in Asgard. But even he understands that strength alone cannot answer every question.

You can defeat an enemy in battle.

You can build an enormous fortress.

You can gather an army of the greatest fallen warriors.

But how do you defeat something that hasn't happened yet?

How do you prepare for a catastrophe known only through prophecy?

And can fate be changed at all?

For Odin, these aren't abstract philosophical questions.

Norse mythology constantly reminds us that even the gods are not safe from the future.

So Odin searches for knowledge everywhere.

He travels between worlds, consults seeresses, speaks to giants, and studies ancient magic.

Two ravens, Huginn and Muninn, assist him. Two wolves, Geri and Freki, are associated with him.

He has an eight-legged horse named Sleipnir, whose rather unusual family history we've already discussed.

And a spear called Gungnir.

Quite an impressive collection for one god.

Yet none of it makes Odin all-knowing.

He continues searching for answers.

Sometimes in places nobody else would even consider visiting.

And very often, he pays dearly for what he discovers.

Perhaps that's what makes Odin so fascinating.

He is powerful, yet afraid of something.

Wise, yet constantly seeking more wisdom.

He commands the gods, but cannot simply order the future to change.

And one day, his hunger for knowledge leads him to a well whose waters promise an extraordinary understanding of the world.

Unfortunately, free samples aren't available.

---

## Chapter 2. Mimir's Well. One Eye for One Answer.

---

![Mimir's Well. One Eye for One Answer.](/visuals/norse/m03/02-odin-at-mimir-s-enchanted-well-1448.webp)

---

Beneath one of Yggdrasil's roots lay a well associated with ancient wisdom.

It was called **Mimir's Well**.

We've met Mimir before.

Remember the wise adviser whose head the Vanir cut off and sent back to Odin as a rather forceful diplomatic complaint?

Odin preserved his head through magic and continued consulting him.

But in Norse mythology, Mimir is also associated with another image.

He is the guardian of the Well of Wisdom.

The *Prose Edda* tells us that Mimir drank from this well and possessed profound knowledge of ancient things.

Odin wanted to drink from it too.

So he travelled to Yggdrasil.

Imagine a place hidden among the roots of the World Tree.

There are no noisy halls of Asgard here.

No feasts or councils.

Only ancient water and a being who possesses secrets unavailable to anyone else.

Odin asked for a drink.

But Mimir wasn't about to share his wisdom simply because the Allfather had turned up.

The well had a price.

**Odin had to surrender one of his eyes.**

Not a piece of jewellery.

Not gold.

Not a weapon.

A part of his own body.

Why an eye?

The myth offers no detailed explanation.

But the symbolism is striking: to gain a deeper understanding of the world, Odin must sacrifice part of his ordinary sight.

He agreed.

And left his eye as a pledge.

According to the *Prose Edda*, Odin's eye remains in Mimir's Well.

The chief of the gods had acquired another form of knowledge, but at the cost of an irreversible loss.

From then on, he lived with one eye.

And judging by what happened next, the experience did absolutely nothing to discourage his interest in further education.

There's something worth considering here.

We tend to think of wisdom as a reward for patience, experience, and study.

In Odin's story, wisdom demands a genuine sacrifice.

And the highest price is paid voluntarily.

Nobody forced Odin to visit the well.

Nobody demanded that he become wiser.

He chose this path himself.

And accepted its terms.

A payment policy like this would raise quite a few questions at a modern university.

But Odin got what he came for.

And still, it wasn't enough.

Because understanding the world is one thing.

Mastering the forces hidden within it is something else entirely.

Odin's next ordeal would make losing an eye look rather like an enrolment fee.

---

## Chapter 3. Nine Nights. The Most Expensive Education in History.

---

![Nine Nights. The Most Expensive Education in History.](/visuals/norse/m03/03-odin-s-sacrifice-beneath-yggdrasil-1448.webp)

---

Yggdrasil connects the worlds.

Its roots reach into ancient regions of existence, while its branches stretch above the earth and heavens.

We already know that life isn't particularly peaceful for this tree.

Terrifying creatures gnaw at its roots.

Birds live among its branches.

A squirrel runs up and down the trunk, carrying hostile messages between its inhabitants.

But one day, Yggdrasil became the setting for something entirely different.

Odin himself hung from its branches.

In **Hávamál**, usually translated as *The Sayings of the High One*, Odin describes his own ordeal.

He hung from the tree for nine nights.

He was wounded by a spear.

He was given neither food nor drink.

And he offered himself as a sacrifice.

To himself.

It's one of the most mysterious scenes in all Norse mythology.

A god sacrifices himself to a god.

And both parties are Odin.

The poem doesn't explain every detail. It doesn't describe exactly who suspended Odin from the tree, how the rite was carried out, or what anyone watching might have seen.

But the ordeal is connected with the acquisition of secret knowledge.

Odin looked down into the depths.

And at the end of his suffering, he grasped the **runes**.

The poem describes him taking up the runes with a cry before falling from the tree.

We aren't told that somebody handed him a beautifully organised textbook.

No instructor appears with a carefully prepared learning programme.

Knowledge comes through suffering, sacrifice, and an experience that transforms the person seeking it.

It's important to understand that this is a mythological narrative, not an instruction for something ordinary people were expected to repeat when learning runes.

Odin's nine nights belong to a sacred story about the acquisition of knowledge.

And there's an interesting detail here.

In the previous modules, we learned that Norse mythology speaks of nine worlds.

Now Odin spends nine nights hanging from the World Tree.

The number nine appears repeatedly in the myths of the North.

But that doesn't mean every appearance of the number automatically has the same fixed symbolic meaning.

The ancient sources didn't leave us a universal handbook explaining the significance of every number.

Back to Odin.

According to *Hávamál*, the ordeal is followed by further discoveries.

The poem mentions nine powerful songs that Odin learned from a son of Bölthorn, a relative on his mother's side.

It also introduces the image of precious mead, associated with inspiration and wisdom.

Odin grows more powerful.

He gains the ability to understand and work through words, knowledge, and magical formulas.

And here's something we need to keep straight.

Odin sacrifices his eye to gain access to Mimir's Well.

He spends nine nights hanging from the World Tree to master the runes.

These are two separate mythological episodes.

But both express the same idea.

**Knowledge isn't something you can simply demand. It comes at a price.**

And if you're thinking that losing an eye and spending nine nights on a tree might finally have convinced Odin to finish his studies, you clearly don't know him very well yet.

But first, let's explore what he actually gained.

Because today, runes are surrounded by so many modern interpretations that the ancient Norse might be just as surprised as we are.

## Chapter 4. Runes. An Ancient Alphabet with a Complicated Reputation.

---

![Runes. An Ancient Alphabet with a Complicated Reputation.](/visuals/norse/m03/04-elder-scholar-and-the-runestone-1448.webp)

---

Imagine finding a stone.

Carved into its surface are angular symbols.

They don't look like the letters you're used to.

Some have long straight lines with shorter strokes branching away from them.

Others resemble arrows, branches, or geometric shapes.

You're looking at runes.

And the first thing you should know is this:

**Runes are, first and foremost, a writing system.**

Ancient Germanic peoples used runic scripts to record words, names, and messages.

They carved runes into stone, wood, metal, weapons, and everyday objects.

The angular shapes were well suited to being cut into hard surfaces.

The best-known early system is called the **Elder Futhark**.

It contained 24 characters.

Its name comes from the sounds of the first six runes:

**ᚠ ᚢ ᚦ ᚨ ᚱ ᚲ**

F — U — TH — A — R — K.

Together, they give us the word *futhark*.

It's rather like the word *alphabet*, which comes from the names of the first two letters of the Greek alphabet: alpha and beta.

But there's a catch.

The Elder Futhark belongs primarily to an earlier period than the Viking Age.

By the beginning of the Viking Age, Scandinavians were using a different version of the script — the **Younger Futhark**.

It contained just 16 characters.

Yes, fewer letters.

Not because the language had suddenly become easier.

Some runes had to represent several related sounds.

So reading an ancient inscription isn't always as simple as swapping one rune for one modern letter.

You need to understand the language, the period in which the inscription was made, and the particular runic system being used.

Now here's where things get interesting.

Runes had names.

Some of these names were associated with concepts familiar to the people who used them.

For example, the name of the rune ᚠ is reconstructed as *fehu* in the Elder Futhark tradition, a word connected with cattle and wealth.

Cattle weren't simply animals. They were an important form of property.

The rune ᛋ in the Younger Futhark was called *sól* — meaning "Sun".

But that doesn't mean every rune was automatically a separate magical tool with one fixed, universal interpretation.

Rune names and later rune poems help scholars study these traditions. They don't amount to a universal fortune-telling manual.

In ancient texts, runes certainly can be associated with magic.

In *Hávamál*, they appear alongside secret knowledge and powerful spells.

Some archaeological discoveries also provide evidence of ritual uses of inscriptions.

But enormous numbers of runes were used for much more ordinary purposes.

To identify the owner of an object.

To write a name.

To record an event.

To commemorate someone who had died.

In the Viking Age, memorial stones often recorded who had erected the stone and whose memory it honoured.

Not every runic inscription was a spell.

Sometimes an ancient person simply wanted to write, "Made by so-and-so."

Which, admittedly, is a little less exciting than opening a portal to the hidden powers of the universe.

Now, let's try a small thought experiment.

Look at these symbols again:

**ᚠ ᚢ ᚦ ᚨ ᚱ ᚲ**

You already know they aren't random marks.

They're the opening sequence of the Elder Futhark.

And the symbol ᚦ represents a sound similar to English *th*.

You've just learned to recognise a small part of an ancient writing system.

No amulets required.

No candles.

And no need to pay someone to decode your destiny.

Speaking of destiny.

Modern books, films, and websites often present runes as a universal system of divination, with each sign assigned a fixed mystical meaning.

Such practices exist today.

But they shouldn't automatically be treated as accurate reconstructions of Viking Age customs.

The historical evidence is considerably more complex, and some popular interpretations are relatively recent inventions.

So at MIYU, we'll distinguish between three things:

Ancient writing.

Magical beliefs actually attested in historical sources.

And modern interpretations.

Not because modern interpretations are necessarily uninteresting.

But because knowledge begins with understanding the difference.

And in that respect, Odin might approve of our curiosity.

Although he'd probably be disappointed by the price of admission.

So far, you still get to keep both your eyes.

## Chapter 5. Huginn and Muninn. Intelligence Gathering. No Days Off.

---

![Huginn and Muninn. Intelligence Gathering. No Days Off.](/visuals/norse/m03/05-odin-s-ravens-over-the-frozen-fjords-1448.webp)

---

After everything we've learned about Odin's eye and the World Tree, you might think he acquires knowledge exclusively through agonising ordeals.

Fortunately for him, there are less painful methods.

For example, sending someone else to find out what's happening.

Odin has two ravens.

Their names are **Huginn and Muninn**.

Their names are generally associated with thought and memory.

The *Prose Edda* tells us that every morning, Odin sends them flying across the world.

They observe what happens, then return and tell him everything they've seen and heard.

Imagine two black birds crossing cold seas, forests, mountains, and human settlements.

They fly over the lands of gods and giants.

They witness events happening far beyond Asgard.

They hear words others cannot.

Then they return to Odin, settle on his shoulders, and deliver their news.

In the ancient sources, this makes Odin exceptionally well informed about events throughout the world.

But there's a surprising detail in the poem *Grímnismál*, or *The Sayings of Grímnir*.

Odin admits that he fears Huginn might not return one day.

And he's even more worried about Muninn.

Why?

The poem doesn't directly explain.

Some scholars have interpreted the ravens as symbolic representations of thought and memory, and Odin's fears as anxiety over the possible loss of these faculties.

But we can't be certain that this was precisely what the ancient poet intended.

Either way, it's a remarkable image.

The god who wants to know everything worries about the creatures that bring him knowledge.

Even the universe's finest intelligence service apparently can't guarantee the safety of its employees.

Odin has another way of observing the world.

In Asgard stands a high seat called **Hliðskjálf**.

According to the *Prose Edda*, when Odin sits upon it, he can survey all lands.

Yet even this ability doesn't eliminate his need to travel.

Throughout the myths, he repeatedly leaves Asgard.

Sometimes he journeys to distant lands.

Sometimes he disguises himself as an old man.

Sometimes he introduces himself under a false name.

And here we discover another characteristic of Odin.

He doesn't always announce his arrival.

An encounter with an unfamiliar traveller can turn into a test.

In the ancient Norse world, hospitality was enormously important.

A house might be the only shelter available to someone caught in a cold or dangerous landscape.

But a host couldn't always know exactly whom they were inviting inside.

And if the stranger happened to be Odin, an ordinary conversation might suddenly become an examination of wisdom, generosity, and the ability to keep one's word.

So don't imagine the Allfather spending all his time sitting on a throne and issuing instructions.

He wanders, listens, watches, and asks questions.

But knowledge doesn't interest him solely as a way of governing the gods.

Odin is also closely associated with poetry.

For the ancient Norse, words weren't simply tools for communicating information.

They preserved the memory of ancestors, praised rulers, celebrated great deeds, and transformed events into stories that could outlive everyone involved.

Poets known as skalds composed intricate works full of elaborate imagery and demanding rules of verse.

And according to one myth, poetic inspiration itself became associated with Odin in a particularly unexpected way.

First, an extraordinarily wise being had to die.

Then came several more murders.

And finally, Odin decided to steal the result.

The history of artistic inspiration was off to a promising start.

## Chapter 6. The Mead of Poetry. Inspiration with a Questionable Origin.

---

![The Mead of Poetry. Inspiration with a Questionable Origin.](/visuals/norse/m03/06-norse-eagle-chase-above-asgard-1448.webp)

---

Remember Kvasir?

The extraordinarily wise being whom, according to one version of the myth, the Æsir and Vanir created after making peace.

From their collective saliva.

Not the most elegant raw material, but the result was exceptional.

Kvasir was so wise that he could answer practically any question.

He travelled the world sharing his knowledge.

And one day, he visited two dwarves.

Their names were **Fjalar and Galar**.

The dwarves invited him to a private conversation.

Then murdered him.

They collected his blood in vessels, mixed it with honey, and prepared an extraordinary drink.

**The Mead of Poetry.**

According to the *Prose Edda*, anyone who drank it would gain the gift of poetry or wisdom.

You can imagine how valuable such a drink would be.

No need to spend years learning how to write verse.

No need to search for inspiration.

Just take a sip.

Although its production process would obviously fail every modern quality-control inspection imaginable.

The dwarves told the gods that Kvasir had died from his own wisdom.

Apparently, nobody had been clever enough to ask him a sufficiently difficult question.

An explanation so unconvincing that it's remarkable anyone thought of using it.

But the dwarves' adventures were far from over.

Later, they caused the deaths of a giant named Gilling and his wife.

Gilling's son, **Suttung**, decided to take revenge.

He captured the dwarves and threatened to kill them.

To save their lives, the brothers surrendered the Mead of Poetry.

Suttung hid the precious drink deep inside a mountain.

He entrusted its protection to his daughter, **Gunnlöd**.

You might think the mead was finally safe.

But Odin heard about it.

And by now, we know what happens when Odin discovers another source of wisdom.

He goes after it.

Using the false name **Bölverkr**, Odin takes a job working for a giant named Baugi, Suttung's brother.

He spends the summer doing the work of nine men.

In exchange, Baugi promises to help him obtain some of the mead.

But Suttung refuses to share a single drop.

So Odin decides to enter the mountain another way.

Using an auger named Rati, Baugi drills a hole through the rock.

Odin transforms into a snake and crawls inside.

There he meets Gunnlöd.

According to the *Prose Edda*, Odin spends three nights with her.

Afterwards, she allows him three drinks of the mead.

Unfortunately, Odin has a rather creative understanding of what constitutes a drink.

With his first, he empties one vessel.

With his second, another.

With his third, the last.

He now has all the mead.

Odin transforms into an eagle and flies out of the mountain.

Suttung spots the escaping thief, assumes the shape of an eagle himself, and gives chase.

Two enormous eagles race towards Asgard.

The gods see Odin approaching and set out containers.

When Odin reaches Asgard, he brings up the mead into the waiting vessels.

The precious drink has finally reached the Æsir.

But during the dangerous escape, some of the mead is lost.

And this is where the ancient story becomes unexpectedly cruel.

According to the *Prose Edda*, the portion that was lost became the share of bad poets.

So poetic talent didn't just have a divine origin.

Terrible poetry had its own mythological explanation too.

Not particularly flattering for the people producing it.

But there's more to this story than an amusing tale about a magical drink.

Odin obtained the mead.

Yet he did so through deception.

He took advantage of Gunnlöd's trust.

He broke the terms of their arrangement.

And he carried away a treasure that didn't belong to him.

Once again, we encounter a rather unusual image of the chief of the gods.

He is a patron of wisdom and poetry.

But his actions don't always align with modern ideas of fairness.

Knowledge makes Odin more powerful.

Yet his pursuit of it can cause harm to others.

And perhaps that's one of the most interesting things about Norse mythology.

It doesn't require us to treat every action of a god as morally right simply because a god performed it.

Sometimes we witness a genuinely great deed.

Other times, we're watching a remarkably inventive theft.

Even if the result happens to be beautiful poetry.

## Chapter 7. The Giant Who Knew Too Much.

---

![The Giant Who Knew Too Much.](/visuals/norse/m03/07-wisdom-duel-in-the-mead-hall-1448.webp)

---

Odin gathered knowledge from seeresses, sages, and ancient beings.

But one day, he decided to meet someone whose wisdom was considered exceptional even among the giants.

His name was **Vafthrudnir**.

And he possessed extraordinary knowledge of the world's structure, its past, and its future.

The story of their meeting survives in the poem *Vafthrudnismál*, or *The Sayings of Vafthrudnir*.

Odin told Frigg that he intended to visit the giant.

Frigg tried to discourage him.

She believed Vafthrudnir was so wise and powerful that the encounter could end badly.

Odin, naturally, went anyway.

If warnings worked on Norse gods, a substantial portion of these myths would never have happened.

Odin arrived at the giant's hall under the false name **Gagnrad**.

He introduced himself as a traveller who wanted to test his host's wisdom.

Vafthrudnir accepted the challenge.

At first, the giant questioned his guest.

About day and night.

About rivers separating the worlds.

About ancient beings and the structure of the universe.

Odin answered correctly.

Then Vafthrudnir proposed a more serious contest.

They would question one another.

And stake their heads on the outcome.

The loser would pay with his life.

A rather unusual way to spend an evening.

But Odin agreed.

Now it was his turn to ask the questions.

Where did the earth and sky come from?

How did the giants originate?

What would happen to the gods at Ragnarök?

Who would survive the end of the world?

Vafthrudnir answered.

He knew that the earth had been made from Ymir's body.

He knew about the ancient generations of beings.

He knew about the terrible winter that would precede the final catastrophe.

He knew that life would continue after the old world was destroyed.

And he knew something particularly disturbing.

At Ragnarök, Odin himself would be swallowed by an enormous wolf.

**Fenrir.**

Consider that for a moment.

Odin came looking for knowledge.

And received a prediction of his own death.

From his opponent, no less.

Perhaps not quite the information anyone wants to hear during an intellectual competition.

But Odin didn't stop.

He continued asking questions.

And Vafthrudnir continued answering.

Until the final question.

**"What did Odin whisper into his son Baldr's ear before he was placed upon the funeral pyre?"**

It was an impossible question.

Only Odin himself knew those words.

And suddenly, the giant understood who stood before him.

His opponent wasn't an ordinary wanderer.

He was competing against the Allfather.

Vafthrudnir acknowledged Odin's wisdom and realised he had lost.

And that's where the poem ends.

It doesn't describe the giant's execution, nor does it reveal what Odin actually whispered to Baldr.

The answer remains unknown.

And that matters.

In the greatest contest of wisdom, Odin doesn't win simply because he knows more about everything in the universe.

He asks a question whose answer is known to him alone.

An extremely effective strategy.

Although most modern quiz competitions would probably consider it a violation of the rules.

But there's a darker side to this story.

Odin discovered how he would die.

He learned that some beings would survive the end of the world.

He learned that destruction would eventually be followed by new life.

Yet none of this knowledge guaranteed that he could change his own fate.

He won the contest.

But he didn't defeat the future.

## Chapter 8. The Wisest God. And the Most Unpleasant Truth.

---

![The Wisest God. And the Most Unpleasant Truth.](/visuals/norse/m03/08-odin-beneath-the-storm-wolf-1448.webp)

---

By now, we know Odin a little better.

When we first met him, he was the Allfather — the chief of the gods, possessing enormous authority.

But behind that title is a far more complicated character.

Odin wasn't born knowing everything.

He constantly seeks to expand the limits of his understanding.

He sacrificed an eye for the wisdom of Mimir's Well.

Spent nine nights hanging from the World Tree to acquire the runes.

Sends Huginn and Muninn to observe events throughout the world.

Assumes disguises to travel and test others.

Acquired the Mead of Poetry through cunning and deception.

And once staked his own head on a contest of wisdom with an ancient giant.

Each of these stories reveals another side of his character.

He is curious.

Brave.

Patient.

But he can also be cruel, cunning, and ruthless.

His pursuit of knowledge isn't always safe for those who happen to be nearby.

And even his greatest abilities don't give him complete control over life.

In *Hávamál*, Odin appears not only as a god of magic, but as a source of practical wisdom.

The poem contains numerous pieces of advice about human behaviour.

How to welcome guests.

How to choose friends.

Why drinking too much is dangerous.

Why it's worth thinking before you speak.

And why we should remember that neither wealth nor life lasts forever.

Surprisingly down-to-earth advice from someone who spent nine nights hanging from the World Tree.

But perhaps that's what makes it valuable.

Wisdom isn't just the ability to understand ancient secrets.

Sometimes it's knowing when to keep quiet, when not to trust a stranger, and when to avoid doing something obviously foolish.

Although Odin himself wasn't always particularly good at following those principles.

And one question remains.

Why does he need so much knowledge?

Why can't he stop?

Why does he continually risk his life, lose things he values, and set out on yet another journey?

The answer lies in the future.

**Odin knows that the world will not last forever.**

He knows about Ragnarök.

The final battle.

The deaths of many gods.

And the wolf destined to kill him.

But knowledge doesn't mean surrender.

Odin keeps acting.

He gathers fallen warriors.

Seeks allies.

Learns more and more about the events to come.

Tries to prepare for something that may be impossible to prevent.

This makes him very different from an all-powerful god who can simply cancel anything he doesn't like.

Here is a being with enormous authority, but limited control.

And that contradiction lies at the heart of Norse mythology.

**You can know that one day you will lose — and still choose to fight.**

Odin receives no guarantee of survival.

But he keeps searching for answers.

Perhaps because knowledge offers more than power.

It can help us understand what is worth fighting for, even when the outcome may already be decided.

And here, our introduction to the Allfather comes to an end.

We leave him among his ravens, ancient prophecies, and endless questions.

But there's another god in Asgard who prefers a rather different approach to solving problems.

He doesn't spend nine nights hanging from a tree to uncover another secret.

He doesn't insist on consulting every wise being he can find.

And he rarely considers a complicated philosophical discussion the best way to resolve a conflict.

What he does have is a hammer.

A very good hammer.

**In the next module, we'll meet Thor.**

And discover why even the most powerful weapon in the universe isn't always useful when a problem really ought to have been thought through first.

Thor, of course, would probably disagree.

Quite possibly with the hammer.

Odin asked what he'd whispered to Baldr before the funeral. Technically, a victory. From an academic integrity perspective, serious questions for the exam setter.
