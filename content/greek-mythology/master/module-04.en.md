# 1. Hero ≠ good person

The modern word “hero” can easily confuse us.

We imagine someone who:

saves people,

does the right thing,

sacrifices themselves,

and generally deserves a medal.

In Greek mythology, it is a little more complicated.

A hero can be:

incredibly brave,

very strong,

clever,

the son or daughter of a god,

someone who does something great —

and still make absolutely terrible decisions.

Sometimes in the very next scene.

So here:

# a hero is not necessarily a moral example.

It is a person whose actions are so great, dangerous or unusual that they become myth.

And if, after one of a hero’s decisions, you think:

> “Why did he even do that?”

it is fine.

You are fine.

# 6. Ariadne appears

Ariadne is the daughter of the Cretan king Minos.

She falls in love with Theseus and decides to help him.

And gives him something that looks extremely unheroic:

# a ball of thread.

No lightning.

No magical sword.

No dragon.

Thread.

Theseus fixes it at the entrance and lets it unwind as he moves into the Labyrinth.

He finds the Minotaur.

Kills him.

And then simply goes back:

# following the thread.

That is why we still have the expression:

# “Ariadne’s thread”

meaning a clue, guide or logical path that helps you find your way through a complex problem.

Ancient tradition directly connects Theseus’s escape from the Labyrinth with the thread Ariadne gave him.

# 9. And yes — Hera really is inside the name Heracles

Remember I promised to return to this in the module about the gods?

Here we are.

The name:

# Heracles

is traditionally understood as:

**“glory of Hera”.**

Which is quite ironic.

Because Hera did not exactly ask for this child.

Heracles is the son of:

# Zeus

and a mortal woman:

# Alcmene.

And Hera is Zeus’s wife.

You already understand why the atmosphere in the family was tense.

Many of Heracles’s troubles in myth are directly connected with Hera’s anger.

The name Heracles really is connected with the idea of “glory of Hera”.

# 10. Why does Heracles have 12 Labours at all?

This part of the story is quite dark.

And it is a good reminder that:

Greek mythology is not a children’s fairy tale.

Hera sends Heracles into madness.

In that state, he commits a terrible crime against his own family.

Later, trying to make amends, he enters the service of King Eurystheus.

And receives a series of almost impossible tasks.

The famous:

# 12 Labours of Heracles.

We are **not going to learn all twelve**.

For our course, it is enough to understand the pattern:

> Heracles receives a task that a normal human cannot complete.

And completes it.

That even gives us the English word:

# Herculean

which can mean:

**requiring enormous effort.**

So if someone says:

> “This is a Herculean task.”

they are not necessarily asking you to clean the stables.

They are simply saying:

> “There is a lot of work there. Thank you very much.”

# 19. Achilles’ heel

And now the expression almost everyone knows:

# Achilles’ heel.

Meaning:

**one weak point in something very strong.**

A company can be huge but depend on one critical supplier.

A product can be excellent but have one single point of failure.

A very strong specialist may be unable to present their ideas.

All of these can be called:

# an Achilles’ heel.

---

# 20. Why the heel?

The most famous version says:

Achilles’s mother, Thetis, wanted to make her son invulnerable.

She dipped the baby into the waters of the river:

# Styx.

But she held him by the heel.

The water touched his whole body —

except this spot.

Basically an almost perfect warrior build.

And one very small vulnerability.

Later Achilles dies from a wound in that area.

But here comes a very important:

# BUT.

---

# 21. If you open the *Iliad* looking for the Styx story — you will not find it

The story about:

**Thetis**

↓

**baby Achilles**

↓

**the river Styx**

↓

**the invulnerable heel**

is **a later tradition**.

Homer does not tell this story.

The *Iliad* actually ends **before Achilles dies**.

Later ancient sources tell stories about Achilles receiving a fatal wound, and the version where the baby is dipped into the Styx belongs to an even later tradition.

And I really like this example.

Because once again we see:

# myth does not stand still.

The story keeps collecting new details.

And sometimes the version everyone knows today —

is not the oldest one at all.

## Sources for fact-checking — editorial version only

Perseus: Pseudo-Apollodorus describes Medusa’s reflection in the bronze shield, Athena’s help, Medusa’s death and the appearance of Pegasus and Chrysaor.

Theseus and Ariadne: ancient sources connect the victory over the Minotaur and the escape from the Labyrinth with Ariadne and her thread. The official Cyprus tourism resource separately records a local tradition in which Theseus leaves Ariadne in Amathous, about 11 km from modern Limassol.

Heracles: ancient tradition includes the Lernaean Hydra and Cerberus among his Twelve Labours. The Greek form Heracles differs from the Latin Hercules; the name is traditionally interpreted as “glory of Hera”.

Jason: ancient texts place the Golden Fleece in Colchis and connect its capture with Medea’s help. Modern official Georgian sources connect ancient Colchis with western Georgia, and Adjara’s tourism service notes the region’s and Batumi’s connection with Colchian culture. The Batumi Archaeological Museum also keeps objects from Colchian culture and directly mentions the region’s connection with the myth of the Argonauts.

Achilles: the *Iliad* presents Achilles as a great hero of the Trojan War but does not contain the popular story about dipping the baby into the Styx. The story of the invulnerable body and vulnerable heel belongs to later tradition; later ancient evidence also connects his death with a wound in the heel.

Odysseus: a hero of the Trojan War whose long journey home becomes the story of the *Odyssey*.
