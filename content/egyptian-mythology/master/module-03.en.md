# Module 3

# Ra Works Seven Days a Week. The Other Gods Are Busy Too.

**Time:** about 20–25 minutes

---

## Introduction. The Morning That Might Never Have Come

---

Imagine a night in Egypt. The Nile nearly merges with the darkness, the temples fall silent, and the oil lamps in people's houses go out. Somewhere beyond the western horizon, the sun has disappeared. For us, it's simple: the Earth rotates; the sun will be back in the morning. You can set your alarm and sleep soundly.

For an ancient Egyptian, the situation was considerably less reassuring. The sun did rise again and again, but something being familiar did not make it guaranteed. While people slept, the gods undertook an extraordinarily dangerous journey: they crossed the realm of the dead, opened locked passages, fought off monsters, and prevented primordial chaos from returning the world to a state in which absolutely nothing worked.

At the center of this operation was Ra, the sun god. His work schedule was straightforward: travel across the sky every day, pass through the dangerous realm of the Duat every night, and be born anew at dawn. No vacation days. Judging by the myths, no sick leave either. And Ra could never manage alone: other deities traveled with him—guides, protectors, embodiments of knowledge, magic, and order. Occasionally the team even included figures with reputations so questionable that the Egyptian pantheon's HR department might reasonably have had a few concerns.

Over the course of this module, we'll live through one complete solar cycle. We'll discover why the sun had several divine names, what the crew of the celestial barque actually did, who was trying to cancel sunrise, why Ra once nearly destroyed humanity, and why every new day was not simply routine to the Egyptians, but a victory.

> **Historical note.** Egyptian myths evolved over thousands of years and differed between cities, periods, and religious texts. Here, we follow one continuous narrative, but we'll point out where different traditions are involved rather than treating them as a single “official version.”

## Chapter 1. Three Faces of One Sun. Even Gods Work Shifts

---

![Khepri, Ra and Atum: the morning, midday and evening sun](/visuals/egypt/m03/02-ch01-khepri-ra-atum-solar-cycle-1448.webp)

---

The eastern sky begins to brighten. The cool sand still holds the night's chill, but the horizon is already taking on the color of glowing copper. Somewhere in the city, people open their doors. Water splashes. The day begins. The sun rises—and with it, the Egyptians believed, the world comes alive.

But who, exactly, has risen above the horizon? If you answer “Ra,” you're right—and at the same time, you're oversimplifying things. Egyptian gods were not especially fond of fitting into a single job description. They could merge with one another, appear in different forms, bear several names, and still remain recognizable. This flexibility is particularly striking when it comes to the sun.

At dawn, solar power was often envisioned as **Khepri**—the god of becoming and rebirth, associated with the scarab beetle. The Egyptians watched dung beetles rolling round balls in front of them and saw an extraordinary image: it was as though someone were rolling the sun disk across the sky. The scarab became more than an insect with an unusual hobby. It was a sign that life could come into being, renew itself, and begin again. Even Khepri's name is connected to an Egyptian root meaning “to become” or “to come into being.”

As the day gathers strength, we meet **Ra**—the radiant sun at the height of its power. In images, he is recognizable by his falcon head and the sun disk above it. Sometimes this image merges with Horus, producing the name **Ra-Horakhty**—“Ra-Horus of the Two Horizons.” In later religious thought, the sun god also becomes associated with the great Amun, giving rise to **Amun-Ra**. For an Egyptian, this did not necessarily mean that a new god had replaced an old one. Rather, different aspects of divine power had found expression in a single name.

Toward evening, as the sun descends, it becomes increasingly associated with **Atum**—an ancient creator god connected with completion and the fullness of existence. In some conceptions, Atum is the evening sun, aged and preparing to disappear from the world of the living. Khepri in the morning, Ra in the daytime, Atum in the evening: it's a useful way of understanding the solar cycle, although the images overlap in actual Egyptian texts far more intricately than three shifts on a roster.

And here is the essential detail: the sun did not merely illuminate fields and warm walls. It was connected to the very possibility of the world's existence. The Egyptians lived in a place where almost everything depended on the regularity of natural cycles: harvests, work in the fields, the Nile's floods, and the life of cities. The sun's return therefore meant more than a lovely view for sunrise enthusiasts. It confirmed that the cosmos was still holding together.

Nor did the Egyptians have a single, simple explanation of *how* the sun traveled. In some traditions it sailed in a barque; in others, the sky goddess Nut swallowed it in the evening and gave birth to it in the morning; in still other images, deities supported or raised the solar disk. This was not a mistake made by ancient authors. Myths could offer different answers to the same question because they spoke not only about the sun's route, but also about the meanings of time, death, and rebirth.

By midday, the light has become dazzling. Ra has reached the highest point of his daytime journey. You might think he could enjoy a moment of triumph. But his greatest trial still lies ahead: after sunset, he'll have to cross a place no living person would ordinarily be eager to visit. And for that, he's going to need a crew.

## Chapter 2. The Celestial Barque. A Project with a Never-Ending Deadline

---

![Ra sails across the daytime sky in the solar barque](/visuals/egypt/m03/03-ch02-ra-daytime-solar-barque-1448.webp)

---

Imagine the sky not as empty space, but as an immense route. Along it moves a barque with a high-curving prow and stern. On board is the sun god. Around him are the forces that make his progress possible at all. Below, the world is busy with trade, agriculture, and palace intrigues; above, an operation is under way that will determine whether anyone will still be around to do those things tomorrow.

The **solar barque** is one of the central images of Egyptian religion. In some traditions, the daytime vessel is called **Mandjet**, and the nighttime vessel **Mesektet**. Names and details vary between sources, but the idea is recognizable: Ra crosses the daytime sky, then continues his journey beyond the visible world. The choice of an Egyptian boat is no accident. In a civilization built along the Nile, traveling by water was so natural that it made sense to imagine even a divine route as a voyage.

Ra is the heart of the journey, but not its only participant. Different deities appear in images and texts. **Sia** embodies perception and understanding; **Hu**, the effective power of divine speech; **Heka**, the magical force without which almost no serious undertaking in the Egyptian cosmos could be completed. **Thoth**, the god of writing and knowledge, is associated with the preservation of knowledge, calculation, and the proper arrangement of things. **Ma'at** personifies truth, justice, and order. We should not imagine all of them as permanent staff members in every single picture of the barque: their presence and functions vary by period and monument. But the overall meaning is clear—physical strength alone cannot save the world. Words, knowledge, magic, and the right order of things are also needed.

Why do words have such power in the first place? Because in Egyptian religious thought, naming a thing, knowing its secret name, or uttering an effective formula was not the same as merely making conversation. Speech could help shape reality. Think of the way various creation myths give speech a creative force. On the solar barque, this idea becomes a means of protection: the gods know how to deal with dangers that ordinary weapons cannot defeat.

And then there is one character who seems particularly difficult to imagine as a reliable ally. **Set** is a god associated with the desert, storms, power, and the destructive aspects of the world. Yes, that Set from the story of Osiris. The murderer of his own brother. A rather unconventional applicant for the role of protector of the cosmos.

Nevertheless, in some depictions of the nighttime journey, it is Set who helps repel the attack of the enormous serpent Apep. Ancient Egyptian gods did not divide neatly into “good” and “evil” according to modern rules. Set's power could destroy established order—or be turned against a threat even more terrible. In a world where a nocturnal monster is ready to destroy the very continuation of time, even a dangerous ally can be useful.

The crew of the solar barque is not a team of superheroes who pose for a victory photograph after the battle. None of them earns the right to declare the mission complete forever. Every evening, everything begins again. Worse still, the hardest part awaits not in the sunlit sky, but after its light disappears.

## Chapter 3. After Sunset. Twelve Hours in a Place Nobody Is in a Hurry to Visit

---

![The barque's night journey through the Duat](/visuals/egypt/m03/04-ch03-night-journey-through-duat-1448.webp)

---

The sun has touched the western horizon and vanished. The daytime world still holds the last of its warmth, but Ra must now take a different route. The barque heads into a region without familiar light, where the boundaries between living people, the dead, and divine beings are arranged quite differently from those on earth.

The Egyptians called this region the **Duat**. We often translate the term as “afterlife” or “underworld,” but we should not imagine an ordinary cave beneath the sand. The Duat is a complex sacred reality; in different texts, it is connected with night, the sky, invisible regions of the cosmos, and the existence of the dead. It operates according to its own rules and contains divine beings, guardians, gates, waterways, and secret names.

The night journey is described in particular detail in the royal funerary texts of the New Kingdom, such as the **Amduat**, whose title can be rendered as “That Which Is in the Duat.” It divides Ra's route into **twelve hours of the night**. This is not a timetable down to the minute or a tourist's geographical map. Each “hour” is a distinct region, scene, and stage in the sun god's transformation. Later, images and texts of this kind also appeared in funerary manuscripts belonging to people outside the royal family.

Imagine the first hours of the night. The inhabitants of the invisible world greet the barque. Some welcome the sun god; others help him move forward; still others guard the passages. Gates do not swing open just because a traveler happens to be important. Knowledge, the correct words, and the ability to cross boundaries are required. In the Egyptian world, a name is not a decorative sign on a door; knowing a name can mean gaining access to the power hidden behind it.

As the journey continues, it becomes more unsettling. In some nocturnal scenes, the solar barque no longer moves in the usual way: deities pull it, and it travels through mysterious spaces governed by unfamiliar laws. Ra changes too. At night, the sun god can be depicted with a ram's head—as a concealed form undergoing renewal. The Egyptians did not imagine that someone simply switched the sun off at night and switched it back on in the morning. Night was a time of perilous transformation.

But the Duat is not merely a realm of threats. For the dead, encountering Ra meant the possibility of sharing in his light and life-giving power. Texts show the sun god bringing light to the inhabitants of the invisible world, while his presence contributes to processes of rebirth. That is why tombs were decorated with images of the night journey: the deceased hoped to accompany the god, overcome the dangers, and gain a chance at a new existence. A cosmic journey became a personal hope.

In the deepest episodes of the nighttime voyage, an especially important encounter takes place. **Ra unites with Osiris**—the ruler of the realm of the dead. Don't picture it as a business meeting between two gods at a conference table. In the theological imagery of the Amduat, their connection helps explain the renewal of life's power: as the solar deity passes through the realm of death, he gains the possibility of being born again, while Osiris becomes part of the cycle of cosmic regeneration. Death and life are not opposites permanently cut off from one another here; they participate in an intricate rhythm of renewal.

But it is too early to count on a morning rebirth. Somewhere in the darkness waits a creature with an entirely different plan for tomorrow. And that plan has only one item: **tomorrow must never come**.

## Chapter 4. Apep. The One Trying to Cancel Sunrise

---

![Set fights off the serpent Apep](/visuals/egypt/m03/05-ch04-set-confronts-apep-1448.webp)

---

Imagine the barque moving across dark water. Protective deities surround it, protective spells are recited, and another stretch of the route lies ahead. Suddenly, the water seems to stop obeying its own current. An immense serpentine body emerges from the gloom, so enormous that you cannot take it all in at once. This is **Apep**, also known by the Greek-derived name **Apophis**.

Apep is not just another monster added for a spectacular action scene. He embodies the destructive forces of chaos, hostile to the established cosmic order. If the solar barque is stopped, the solar cycle itself is endangered. In texts and images, the serpent attacks Ra and attempts to prevent his vessel from moving; the gods oppose him, armed not only with physical force but also with magical means. This is not a dispute over land or a throne. What is at stake is the very possibility that the world will continue.

This is where Set reappears—a character we already know from his less appealing side. In some traditions, he destroys the order of family and kingship by turning against Osiris and Horus. But in other depictions within the same broad religious culture, Set stands on Ra's side and strikes the serpent threatening the solar barque. It is a paradox only to us: we are accustomed to villains having consistent biographies. For an ancient Egyptian, a power associated with storms and danger could become a weapon against an even more destructive enemy.

Defense is not limited to a single thrust of a spear. In solar myths and rituals, the dangerous serpent is bound, pierced, cut apart, and deprived of his power through names and spells. People on earth also took part in this symbolic struggle: in certain rites, they could make images of the enemy, destroy them, and recite protective texts. This was how Egyptians understood the connection between temple and cosmos. The ritual was not a game of “slay the monster yourself.” It helped sustain the victory of order over the threat of disintegration.

An obvious question arises here: if the gods are so powerful, why don't they get rid of Apep once and for all? Because the Egyptian worldview did not promise that chaos could be eliminated permanently. Ra wins—but night will return, and so will the threat. In mythological terms, Apep belongs to the forces that perpetually oppose the ordered cosmos. The gods' task cannot be reduced to one decisive battle. They must preserve the world again and again.

There is something almost painfully human about this story. You've finally solved the problem, closed every tab, sent the last report, and confidently declared the job finished—and you know that tomorrow it will all come back. Except that, for Ra, “it will all come back” means an enormous snake and the risk of losing the sun. Suddenly, Monday seems bearable.

But the real subtlety goes deeper. **Chaos is not necessarily the same thing as night.** For the Egyptians, night could be filled with gods, rules, passages, and the hope of rebirth. Apep, by contrast, represents an attempt to destroy the very possibility of order and cyclical movement. Ra's mission is not to “destroy darkness” itself, but to pass through it without allowing the world to disappear.

Once the danger has been repelled, the journey continues. It is not yet dawn, but hope for the dawn has been preserved. You might think the sun god had earned humanity's gratitude. And yet, in one of Egypt's most disturbing myths, it was Ra's relationship with human beings that nearly ended in catastrophe.

## Chapter 5. The Eye of Ra. When Security Decided to Eliminate Everyone

---

![Sekhmet and the red-dyed beer](/visuals/egypt/m03/06-ch05-sekhmet-and-red-beer-1448.webp)

---

Again and again, Ra protects the world's existence. People receive sunlight, crops ripen, and life goes on. You might expect universal gratitude. But the Egyptians told a story in which things took a very different turn.

One version survives in a text modern scholars call the **Book of the Heavenly Cow**. In it, Ra once ruled among human beings, but he grew old. Even a great god could not escape the signs of age: the myth describes his body in precious substances—gold, silver, and lapis lazuli. While Ra was young and inspired fear, people apparently preferred to behave themselves. Now, however, a conspiracy is brewing against the aging ruler.

Ra learns of the discontent and summons a council of gods. Among his advisers is the ancient Nun, associated with the primordial waters from which the world emerged. The gods propose punishing the rebels by sending the **Eye of Ra** against them—an embodiment of his solar, protective, and fearsome power. This is important: the “eye” is not a missing body part, nor is it a separate surveillance camera. In Egyptian myths, a divine eye can act independently and take the form of a powerful goddess.

In this story, the punishing force is **Hathor**—usually associated with joy, music, love, and maternal care as well. But solar power does not have to be gentle. In its destructive, leonine aspect, the story becomes closely intertwined with the figure of **Sekhmet**, the fearsome goddess of war, disease, and protection. Different retellings present the names and relationships between these manifestations in different ways. What matters most here is the plot: divine punishment exceeds its intended limits.

The goddess descends upon the rebellious people. She crushes them in the desert, and Ra soon realizes that revenge has gone too far. This is no longer about punishing conspirators, but about the destruction of humankind. An unpleasant problem even by the standards of the Egyptian divine family: Ra himself unleashed this force, but simply ordering her to stop is not enough.

Then comes one of the strangest methods of saving the world in ancient mythology. Ra orders an enormous quantity of **beer** to be prepared, mixed with **red ochre** so that it will look like blood. In the Book of the Heavenly Cow, the amount is measured in thousands of vessels—one well-known version mentions seven thousand. The mixture is poured across the fields before the enraged goddess can return to resume the slaughter.

In the morning, she sees the red liquid all around her. Taking it for blood, she drinks it—and becomes so intoxicated that her destructive campaign comes to an end. Humanity survives. One of the worst catastrophes in mythic history has been stopped with malt, ochre, and a distinctly risky improvisation. If this operation had been proposed in an ordinary meeting, it would probably have been sent back for revisions.

But the story does not end with a joyful reconciliation. Ra grows weary of ruling on earth and withdraws to the sky. In the myth, **Nut** lifts him up in the form of a celestial cow. A new distance opens between gods and people: Ra's direct kingship on earth ends, and the celestial and earthly realms are separated. The story thus explains not only how humanity came under threat, but also why the great sun god now dwells above the world rather than walking among human beings as their king.

This myth should not be read as a set of instructions proving that the ancient Egyptians approved of divine violence. Instead, it reveals the dual nature of solar power: it gives life, but it can also scorch and destroy; it protects order, yet can become a threat itself when it knows no restraint. Hathor and Sekhmet remind us that divine power in Egyptian thought was not a gentle lamp you could always use safely. Sometimes it resembled the desert sun: you cannot live without it, but you cannot afford to take it lightly.

And here a question arises that is far more interesting than “Who's stronger, Ra or Sekhmet?” If even the gods can become angry, make mistakes, or reveal dangerous power, what keeps the world in balance at all? The Egyptians answered with a single short word: **Ma'at**.

## Chapter 6. Ma'at. The Order That Makes the Sun Rise

---

![A temple rite for Ma'at](/visuals/egypt/m03/07-ch06-maat-temple-ritual-1448.webp)

---

Imagine a morning in an Egyptian temple. Behind closed doors, the daily ritual begins. Priests purify themselves, open the sanctuary, tend to the deity's statue, and bring offerings of food, drink, incense, and cloth. To us, this may look like a complex ceremony involving countless objects and rules. But to those who performed it, something much greater was at stake than the temple's routine: the human world had to respond to the care of the gods and take part in sustaining the cosmic order.

That order was called **Ma'at**. The word brought together ideas of truth, justice, proper order, rightful relationships between people, and the stability of the universe. Ma'at was both a concept and a goddess—a woman with a distinctive ostrich feather on her head. In Egyptian history, Ma'at's feather would also become an important image in the judgment of the dead. But restricting her to the scene of a heart being weighed would be a serious mistake. Ma'at was not needed only by the dead. The living needed her—and, in a mythological sense, so did the sun itself.

Ma'at's opposite was falsehood, disorder, violence, and the destruction of proper relationships. The Egyptians did not think of the world as a perfect place where order had been established once and the matter permanently settled. Quite the contrary: order had to be sustained constantly. Ra's nightly encounter with danger is a cosmic expression of this idea. The problem of chaos was not resolved long ago. It demanded a response every day.

The pharaoh was represented as the chief earthly guardian of Ma'at. On temple walls, he can be seen offering the gods a small figurine of the goddess Ma'at—a gesture loaded with meaning. The king was, in effect, presenting the divine world with what he was obliged to ensure on earth: a just and properly ordered life. This was, of course, an ideological image of royal power, not proof that every pharaoh really was a fair ruler. The real Egypt knew wars, coercion, conflict, and political crises. But the ideal of Ma'at made it possible to declare what the world *ought* to be.

The priests also had an important role. Daily temple rites were performed in the king's name, although the pharaoh could not physically be present in every sanctuary across the country. Worship of the gods, recitation of sacred formulas, offerings, and depictions of victory over the enemies of order connected human activity with the life of the cosmos. When an image of Apep was symbolically destroyed during a ritual, it was not a theatrical performance, but a way of participating in the endless struggle to keep the world in existence.

Yet Ma'at was not confined to royal authority or temple ceremonies. For an individual, it meant striving not to lie, cheat, or cause unjust harm, and fulfilling one's responsibilities to others. Egyptian moral teachings and funerary texts show how important it was to live up to this ideal. The world did not depend exclusively on how effectively Set stabbed a serpent. People, too, had a duty not to turn their own lives into miniature branches of primordial chaos.

Now return, in your mind, to the nighttime barque. The final hours of the journey lie ahead. The dangers behind it have not disappeared from the universe, but they have been overcome this time. In depictions of the Amduat, the sun god passes through renewal and emerges again in the form of Khepri. The reborn sun approaches the eastern horizon. Shu, the god of air, may greet it by raising the solar disk. And somewhere on earth, a person who knows nothing of the night crew's complicated work opens a door, squints into the light, and begins an ordinary morning.

Perhaps this is what gave the Egyptian solar myth its special power. The gods' greatest victory looks like something completely ordinary to human beings. The sun has risen again. That means the world is still holding together.

Ra has completed another twenty-four hours. In a few hours, his next night shift will begin. Unfortunately, there is nowhere to hang a sign saying “closed for scheduled maintenance.”

## Conclusion. See You After the Most Dangerous Night Shift

---

![A new sunrise over Egypt](/visuals/egypt/m03/08-ending-sunrise-renewed-1448.webp)

---

Over the course of one module, we have completed a journey that the Egyptians understood on a cosmic scale. We watched the sun god change his forms from dawn to dusk, met the crew of his barque, descended into the Duat, survived Apep's attack, and discovered that even Ra himself could create a danger that everyone would then have to work together to overcome.

If you remember just one idea, let it be this: **for the ancient Egyptians, sunrise was not an automatic function of nature, but a sign that life, death, divine power, and Ma'at had once again found their balance**.

And yet not every question has been answered. Ra is responsible for the return of light; Osiris rules the realm of the dead and is associated with the possibility of rebirth. But who will take care of the bodies of the deceased, their names, their memory, and their passage into the afterlife? The Egyptian system clearly needs a few more specialists. And, as you will soon discover, some of them have very unusual working methods.

**Module 03 complete.** Sunrise has been achieved. You may go about your day—the gods have things covered for now.

## 1. You see three images: a scarab beside the rising sun; a radiant sun god at the height of the day; an aging creator at sunset. Which sequence best matches common solar representations?

*Three Names, One Cycle*

A. Atum → Ra → Khepri.  
B. Khepri → Ra → Atum.  
C. Osiris → Set → Thoth.  

**Answer:** B.

**Option A:** Not quite. Sunrise and sunset have been swapped: Khepri is primarily associated with the rising sun and renewal, while Atum is associated with the day's end.

**Option B:** Correct. This is a useful model of a solar cycle: Khepri at dawn, Ra in the full power of daylight, and Atum at sunset. Actual traditions could combine the images in different ways, however.

**Option C:** Not quite. These gods play important roles in Egyptian myths, but they do not form a sequence of morning, daytime, and evening suns.

## 2. Why does Set, who committed a crime against Osiris, also protect Ra's barque in some myths?

*The Most Inconvenient Ally*

A. Because Egyptian mythology allowed one god to have different roles: Set's destructive power could be used to protect the world against Apep.  
B. Because after the story of Osiris, Set permanently became a flawless god of goodness.  
C. Because Apep was Set's brother, and they staged the battle for the benefit of humankind.  

**Answer:** A.

**Option A:** Correct. In Egyptian traditions, a deity could have seemingly contradictory functions. Set is dangerous—and that very dangerous power can be directed against the chaos embodied by Apep.

**Option B:** Not quite. Protecting Ra does not cancel the other stories about Set or turn him into an unambiguously positive hero.

**Option C:** Not quite. There is no basis for treating their confrontation as staged or explaining it through kinship. Within the myth, Apep poses a genuine cosmic threat.

## 3. Which statement most accurately captures the central idea of Ra's journey and the concept of Ma'at?

*Who Saves the World?*

A. Chaos was defeated once and for all long ago, so gods and humans now need only leave things as they are.  
B. Order must be maintained again and again: the gods defend the cosmic cycle, while people and earthly rulers must strive to preserve Ma'at in their lives.  
C. Ma'at refers only to the judgment of the dead and has nothing to do with human life or the sun's movement.  

**Answer:** B.

**Option A:** Not quite. The repeated battle with Apep itself shows that the Egyptian cosmic order was never regarded as permanently secure.

**Option B:** Correct. Ma'at links cosmic stability to human responsibilities, justice, and ritual. Sunrise is not a one-time victory, but order renewed each day.

**Option C:** Not quite. Ma'at's feather is indeed important in beliefs about the judgment of the dead, but the concept is much broader: truth, justice, and the proper order of the world.
