# Module 1

# In the Beginning, There Was Chaos. That Wasn't Even the Worst Part.

*How a world emerged from nothing, ice, fire, and one particularly unfortunate giant — a world that gods and humans would eventually have to live in.*

**Time:** about 15–20 minutes

---

## Chapter 1. Before Everything Went Wrong

---

![Ginnungagap — Ice and Fire](/visuals/norse/m01/01-ginnungagap-ice-and-fire-1448.webp)

---

Try to imagine a world where nothing exists.

Not an empty room. Not an endless ocean or a black sky without stars.

There is no room. No ocean. No sky. No ground to stand on. No time to tell you how long any of this has been going on.

There isn't even darkness as we understand it, because light doesn't yet exist for us to compare it with.

In Norse mythology, this state was called **Ginnungagap** — the great, yawning primordial void.

It appears in *Völuspá* (The Prophecy of the Seeress), one of the most important poems in the *Poetic Edda*. Before there was earth, sea, or sky, there was only an immense emptiness.

But emptiness wasn't the only thing that existed before the world as we know it.

To the north lay **Niflheim**, a realm of mist, cold, and ice. There stood the spring Hvergelmir, the source of ancient rivers. Their waters flowed ever outward, until great masses of ice began to fill the edges of the void.

To the south was **Muspelheim**, a realm of fire and unbearable heat. Flames raged there, sending sparks flying into the darkness. Later, we'll meet Surtr, a terrifying being with a flaming sword, closely associated with this realm.

Between them lay Ginnungagap.

The north froze everything it touched. The south melted everything within its reach.

And one day, these two forces met.

The heat of Muspelheim reached the ice that had spread from Niflheim. The ice began to melt. Drops gathered in the void, and from them, life awakened.

There was no thunder. No magnificent voice announcing the creation of the universe.

Instead, something enormous and alive emerged from the melting ice.

His name was **Ymir**.

He was a giant — the ancestor of an ancient race of beings known in Norse mythology as the jötnar.

If you were expecting a wise and benevolent god to appear first and bring order to everything, I'm afraid you're going to be disappointed.

The first thing to show up was a giant.

And things were about to get considerably worse.

---

## Chapter 2. A Giant, a Cow, and a Rather Unusual Family Tree

---

![Ymir and Audhumla](/visuals/norse/m01/02-ymir-and-audhumla-1448.webp)

---

Ymir existed in the frozen emptiness, at a time when there was no earth and no ordinary source of food.

Fortunately, life had already found a way to provide for him.

From the melting frost emerged an enormous cow named **Audhumla**.

Yes. A cow.

In a story where there are no humans, no cities, no forests, and not even a proper surface to stand on, Norse mythology suddenly introduces a cow.

Four rivers of milk flowed from her udder, nourishing Ymir.

Audhumla, of course, had no need to drink her own milk. She survived by licking salty blocks of ice.

And one day, she discovered something rather unusual inside one of them.

On the first day, human hair appeared through the ice.

On the second, a head.

By the third day, an entire man stood before her.

His name was **Búri**.

He was beautiful, strong, and would become the ancestor of the gods.

---

![Audhumla Reveals Buri](/visuals/norse/m01/03-audhumla-reveals-buri-1448.webp)

---

The ancient Norse sources don't explain why there was a man inside a block of ice, or how he got there in the first place.

Apparently, nobody thought to question the cow, either.

Meanwhile, Ymir was keeping himself busy.

As the giant slept, a man and a woman emerged from beneath his arm, while his legs produced yet another descendant. And so began the line of the frost giants.

It's worth remembering that the jötnar weren't simply enormous villains. Later, we'll meet wise ones, powerful women, allies, and even relatives of the gods among them. The English word *giant* doesn't always capture what these beings really were.

But already, two family lines were taking shape.

One descended from Ymir.

The other from Búri, whom Audhumla had freed from the ice.

Búri had a son named Borr. He married Bestla, a giantess and the daughter of the jötunn Bölthorn.

Together, they had three sons:

**Odin, Vili, and Vé.**

These three would change the fate of the entire world.

There's one particularly interesting detail worth noticing: Odin's mother was a giantess.

The future chief of the gods, who would fight giants and defend the order he had helped create, was himself related to them by blood.

From the very beginning, Norse mythology makes one thing clear: family relationships are going to be complicated.

But first, the brothers had to decide what to do about Ymir.

And their decision would be final.

---

## Chapter 3. The First Murder. Absolutely Enormous Consequences.

---

![Odin, Vili, and Ve Slay Ymir](/visuals/norse/m01/04-odin-vili-ve-slay-ymir-1448.webp)

---

Odin, Vili, and Vé killed Ymir.

Why did they decide to do it?

The myth doesn't give us a detailed psychological profile of the brothers. There are no lengthy negotiations, no account of the giant's final words, and no explanation involving the sort of personal grudge we might recognise.

In the *Prose Edda*, this event marks a turning point: the ancient being dies, and from his remains, the creation of an ordered world begins.

But Ymir's death came with consequences that were rather difficult to ignore.

So much blood poured from his wounds that it caused an enormous flood.

The blood filled the surrounding space and drowned almost the entire race of frost giants.

Only Bergelmir and his wife survived. According to the *Prose Edda*, they escaped on a wooden structure, sometimes described in translation as a boat or an ark.

From them descended the later generations of frost giants.

So the gods committed the first great murder, nearly wiped out an entire ancient race, and still failed to eliminate it completely.

The descendants of the surviving giants would meet the gods again and again — sometimes as enemies, sometimes as relatives, and sometimes as guests nobody particularly wanted.

For now, however, Odin and his brothers had other things to deal with.

Lying before them was Ymir's enormous body.

And they decided to use it to build the world.

This may have been the first occasion in history when the phrase "waste not, want not" was taken a little too seriously.

---

## Chapter 4. How to Build a Universe from a Dead Giant

---

![Creating the Cosmos from Ymir](/visuals/norse/m01/05-creation-from-ymir-1448.webp)

---

Ymir's body became the raw material for everything that existed.

From his flesh, the gods made the earth.

From his blood, the seas and oceans.

From his bones, the mountains.

From his teeth and fragments of bone, the stones.

His hair became forests and plants.

They lifted Ymir's enormous skull above the earth, turning it into the vault of heaven.

To hold the sky in place, the gods positioned four dwarves beneath its four corners: Norðri, Suðri, Austri, and Vestri.

Their names correspond to north, south, east, and west.

Even the clouds had a rather unpleasant origin.

According to the *Prose Edda*, the gods threw Ymir's brain into the air, and from it, the clouds were formed.

Now, every time you look up at a beautiful Nordic sky, you'll be able to remember that little detail.

You're welcome.

But creating the earth and sky didn't mean the world was safe.

Beyond the seas lay the lands of the giants — descendants of those who had survived the flood of blood.

The gods decided to separate the central part of the world from those lands, creating a protected place where humans could eventually live.

From Ymir's eyebrows, they built a defensive boundary.

The enclosed space became known as **Midgard** — the Middle Enclosure, or the Middle World.

The name was fairly literal: the human world would exist within a protected boundary, while dangerous territories lay beyond it.

Not everything outside the gods' domain was necessarily hostile, but plenty of things remained beyond their control.

And here we encounter one of the most important ideas in Norse mythology.

**Order wasn't part of the world from the beginning. It had to be created within chaos — and then constantly defended.**

The gods hadn't destroyed every ancient force. They had merely established boundaries between them.

And although the earth now existed, the sky stretched above it, and oceans surrounded it, something was still missing.

Light.

Time.

And even the slightest indication of when morning was supposed to begin.

According to Norse mythology, how did life first emerge?

A. Odin created the first beings from stone.  
B. The first giants grew from Yggdrasil's roots.  
C. The heat of Muspelheim melted the ice of Niflheim.

When fire met ice, Ymir emerged from the melting drops. A rather dramatic start to life.

C. His hair  
D. His skull  

**Answer:** B.

The giant's eyebrows became the human world's first line of defence. Even in death, Ymir remained useful. Not that he had much say in the matter.

## Chapter 5. The Sun, the Moon, and a Spectacularly Bad Security System

---

![Sol, Mani, Skoll, and Hati](/visuals/norse/m01/06-sol-mani-skoll-hati-1448.webp)

---

To illuminate the new world, the gods took sparks and burning embers from Muspelheim.

They placed them in the heavens, set the celestial bodies on their paths, and established the order of their movements.

This made the passing of days, nights, and seasons possible.

But the ancient Norse weren't content to simply say that the Sun moved across the sky.

The Sun needed a story.

And, naturally, a threat.

The *Prose Edda* tells of a man named Mundilfari. He had two children so beautiful that he named his daughter Sól — the Sun — and his son Máni — the Moon.

The gods were not particularly impressed by this display of parental confidence.

They took the children and put them in charge of the celestial bodies.

Sól was to drive the Sun's chariot, pulled by two horses named Árvakr and Alsviðr.

Máni was responsible for guiding the Moon.

On paper, it sounded like an excellent job. The heavens, the stars, an important position with considerable responsibility.

There was just one problem.

A wolf named **Sköll** was chasing the Sun.

Another wolf, **Hati**, was hunting the Moon.

They weren't simply pursuing the celestial bodies for entertainment. According to prophecy, one day their chase would end in catastrophe.

But for now, Sól and Máni continued their endless journey.

Every new day, the Sun rose above the world.

And every new day, a predator followed.

Remarkably, in Norse mythology, even the sunrise has a natural enemy.

So, the earth had been created. The sky was in place. The celestial bodies were moving.

All that remained was to create someone who could look up and admire the whole arrangement.

Humans.

---

## Chapter 6. The First Humans. No Instruction Manual Included.

---

![Awakening Ask and Embla](/visuals/norse/m01/07-ask-and-embla-1448.webp)

---

One day, Odin, Vili, and Vé were walking along the seashore.

There, they found two trees — or perhaps two pieces of wood, depending on how we interpret the ancient account.

The brothers stopped.

And decided to make humans out of them.

They named the first man **Ask** and the first woman **Embla**.

The name Ask is associated with the ash tree. Embla's name is more mysterious. Scholars have suggested connections to various trees and plants, but its precise meaning remains uncertain.

A wooden body, however, wasn't enough.

The gods gave their creations life, intelligence, senses, speech, and the ability to experience the world around them.

In the *Prose Edda*, these gifts are divided between Odin and his brothers.

In an older poetic tradition, preserved in *Völuspá*, Odin appears alongside two different figures — Hœnir and Lóðurr. This is a useful reminder that the surviving myths don't always tell one neat, universally agreed-upon version of events.

Whatever the details, two pieces of wood became living human beings.

They could breathe.

Feel warmth.

Hear the sea.

See the light.

Speak to one another.

The gods gave them clothing, names, and a place to live in Midgard.

For the first time, the new world had inhabitants capable of understanding that they existed.

There was, however, one small catch.

Midgard didn't belong entirely to humans.

Beyond its boundaries lay other lands and other beings. Powerful gods lived above them. Somewhere in the depths, forces were waiting that the first humans knew absolutely nothing about.

And, rather inconveniently, the stability of the entire world depended on something far more complicated than the ground beneath their feet and the sky above their heads.

To understand that arrangement, we need to meet the most extraordinary being in all of Norse cosmology.

The tree that connects the worlds.

---

## Chapter 7. Yggdrasil. The Tree Holding Everything Together.

---

![Yggdrasil — The World Tree](/visuals/norse/m01/08-yggdrasil-world-tree-1448.webp)

---

Somewhere within the very structure of the universe stands an enormous ash tree.

Its name is **Yggdrasil**.

This isn't simply a very large tree. Its branches stretch above the worlds, while its roots reach deep into different regions of existence.

An ordinary tree connects earth and sky. Yggdrasil connects entire realms of reality.

The ancient Norse imagined the cosmos not as a single flat surface with gods living somewhere overhead, but as a complex arrangement of places inhabited by different beings.

There are lands belonging to humans, gods, giants, and other creatures of myth.

The expression **"nine worlds"** does appear in the Old Norse tradition.

But here's an interesting detail: none of the surviving ancient texts provides a single, universally accepted map listing all nine worlds and showing exactly where each one belongs.

Those beautiful modern diagrams, with the worlds arranged neatly among the tree's branches and roots, are attempts at reconstruction — not architectural plans left behind by the Vikings.

Yggdrasil itself, however, is described in considerably more detail.

The *Prose Edda* tells us of three enormous roots extending into different regions.

Beneath one lies the spring Hvergelmir.

There lives Nidhogg, a terrifying creature that gnaws at the tree's root.

Beneath another lies Mimir's Well, associated with wisdom and knowledge.

Later, Odin will seek a drink from its waters and agree to pay a terrible price.

Beneath the third lies the Well of Urd, where the gods gather and the Norns — mysterious beings associated with fate — carry out their work.

But even Yggdrasil doesn't enjoy a particularly peaceful existence.

An eagle sits high in its branches.

Between the eagle and Nidhogg runs a squirrel named Ratatoskr, carrying hostile messages from one to the other.

Deer feed on the tree's foliage.

Nidhogg damages its roots.

The World Tree is constantly subjected to forces that threaten to destroy it.

Even the load-bearing structure of the universe requires regular maintenance.

And judging by the relationships between its residents, nobody is making the job any easier.

Yet the tree continues to stand.

The Norns tend to it, drawing water and special clay from the Well of Urd to preserve its branches.

And once again, we encounter something fundamental about Norse mythology.

The world doesn't survive because it was created perfectly once and for all.

**It continues to exist because countless forces are simultaneously sustaining, destroying, and transforming it.**

Nothing here is completely safe.

Not even the greatest tree in existence.

And not even the gods.

---

## Chapter 8. The World Is Ready. Let the Problems Begin.

---

So, let's return to where we started.

Once, there was nothing but a yawning void between fire and ice.

Then came Ymir.

Then a cow.

Then the ancestors of the gods.

Three brothers killed the first giant and made the earth, sky, and seas from his body.

The gods set the celestial bodies in motion, established the boundaries of the human world, and gave life to the first people.

And above all these stories rises Yggdrasil, connecting the worlds.

You might think everyone could finally settle down and enjoy a little peace.

But let's revisit a few details.

The giants haven't disappeared. Their descendants still exist beyond the boundaries of Midgard.

The Sun and Moon are being pursued by wolves destined to catch them one day.

Yggdrasil is under constant attack.

Even the boundary between the gods and the ancient beings is far from absolute: they're relatives, enemies, allies, lovers, and future participants in a remarkable number of catastrophes.

And far to the south, in the fiery realm of Muspelheim, stands Surtr.

We met him right at the beginning of our story.

One day, he'll emerge with his flaming sword and take part in the destruction of the world.

But that's still a long way off.

Before then, we have wars between gods, kidnappings, deception, questionable marriages, sacrifices, an enormous wolf, a serpent that encircles the world, and quite a few decisions everyone would probably like to undo.

The most fascinating thing is that this world, destined to be destroyed one day, began with a death.

For the earth, seas, forests, and sky to exist, Ymir had to die.

Death wasn't simply an ending.

It became a beginning.

This idea will return again and again throughout Norse mythology.

But before we talk about the end of the world, we should probably meet the people who are supposed to be running it.

**The gods of Asgard.**

And a word of warning: having divine authority is absolutely no guarantee of common sense.

# Self-check

---

## 1. Who chases the Sun and Moon across the sky?

A. Nidhogg and Ratatoskr  
B. Odin's two ravens  
C. Ymir and Bergelmir  
D. The wolves Sköll and Hati  

**Answer:** D.

Sköll pursues the Sun. Hati hunts the Moon. Apparently, even celestial bodies don't get a day off.

---

## 2. According to the myth, how were the first humans, Ask and Embla, created?

A. The gods brought pieces of wood found on the shore to life.  
B. They were born from Ymir.  
C. They emerged from Mimir's Well.  
D. The Norns made them from Yggdrasil's roots.  

**Answer:** A.

The gods gave wood life, intelligence, and senses. An instruction manual for surviving Midgard was apparently not included.

---

## 3. What do we actually know about the nine worlds of Norse mythology?

A. All nine are shown on a detailed ancient map.  
B. Each world sits on a separate branch of Yggdrasil.  
C. Ancient sources mention nine worlds but provide no single, definitive map.  
D. The nine worlds were invented by modern fantasy writers.  

**Answer:** C.

Modern diagrams of the nine worlds are reconstructions. The Vikings never left us an official map of the universe. Documentation was clearly not a priority.
